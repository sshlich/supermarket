# 15. The underside: crime, weapons and drugs

Speculative. Plain text is the main version; **Alternative:** marks another version of the same thing; **Hook:** marks a
mystery or story seed; **In play:** marks where it surfaces in the game.

The other files have the Company's crimes (a litre of water is a standards matter, a repaired meter is "damage") and the
gangs' laws (the Level, the first hook, the toll). They are thin on the rest of it: the person who gets stabbed on a stair, the
pills under the tea urn, the knife on the belt. A tower with forty thousand people in it, most of them poor, tired, in pain and
packed eight to a square, would have all of it. This file adds it, in plain words, on top of what is already there.

Three rules for the file.

1. **No combat as content.** The canon brief rules out combat and night trips, and this file keeps to that. Violence shows up
   the way it does in real life for most people: as something that already happened. A bill, a bandage, a missing neighbour, a
   hole in the rota, a slip that says *the knife is not mine*. The player may be asked to mend, carry, hide or refuse. The player
   is never asked to fight.
2. **Plain names for plain things.** A club is a club. A pill is a pill. The invented brand names are Vey's, because Vey would
   name them.
3. **Everything here is made from what the Stack already has:** cells, pipe, conduit, hot-knives, bale hooks, stills, grow beds,
   frass, mould, Vey's dispensers, the sweet chemical breath from the drowned floors, the Spire's expired stock. No new
   chemistry, no imported villains.

---

## 1. Four things worth stealing

Everyone in the Stack is poor in the same four ways, and so crime in the Stack is mostly about four things.

**Water.** The first law of the Sump is about it and the first law of the Mills is too (05). A litre is worth more than the
person who takes it. Water theft is the one crime every floor deals with itself, quickly and in the open, because the cost of
letting it slide is a dry bed. Nobody needs this file to explain it.

**Cells.** A power cell is the only thing in the Stack that is small, valuable, anonymous and the same everywhere. Every
socket board, every drone, every lamp, every wand and every medical device runs on one. A new H-cell costs 18 cr at the store, more than half a day's
wage, and fits in a coat. A dead one is worth a fifth of that and is still worth carrying, because a dead cell can be
recharged for under 3 cr or rebuilt at a Mills bench (09). Cell theft is the pickpocketing of the Stack: constant, small, mostly unreported and the
reason that people in the Mills carry their cells in a sock around the neck.

**Medicine.** Vey sells it dear and locks it in dispensers; the Mills crack the dispensers; the counterfeiters press new
pills from old ones; the Spire dumps its expired stock in the sea. More of the Mills's grey economy is made of pills than of
anything else except water (08). Pills are stolen from nurses, pharmacists, chutes, clinics, children and the dead.

**The right to be somewhere else.** A day pass to the Middle, a band pass, a lane permit, a Hatch card, a Trader's Licence, a
clean incident number: paper (or tin, or a number in a book) that lets you stand on a floor you do not live on. Bylaw 64 says
movement downward is unrestricted. Everyone understands what that implies about movement upward. Passes are lent, rented,
forged, stolen from the dead and sold at the turnstile. The Stack's version of a passport forger is a cheerful person with a
punch and a stamp pad on floor 29.

Everything else a thief might want (food, tools, furniture) is either too heavy to carry up a stair, too identifiable on a
floor where everyone knows the neighbours' things, or worth less than the beating you would get for it.

**In play:** the player's own stock is mostly outside thieves' interest unless it is one of the four. A **theft event** (05:
"Someone was in the shop overnight") should lean on them: a cell from the socket board, a water vessel from the grow bed, a
packet of pills from the mender's order, a pass left in a coat. Not the machine. Nobody steals a machine; it is 3x3 squares and
has a Company serial.

## 2. How people get hurt

### 2.1 Where

A Stack floor is crowded, loud and watched. The Mills especially: thin partitions, shared stairwells, hot-bunk racks a metre from
the next sleeper, a warden on every landing paid to notice. Nobody is ever really alone, which makes violence rarer than the
rest of this file might suggest. When it happens it needs a place nobody is looking at.

| Place | Why nobody is looking | Who gets hurt |
|---|---|---|
| **Stair landings between floors** | Bylaw 68: stairs are for emergencies. Daily use of stairs is not an emergency, so nobody is supposed to be there and no warden is posted | Anyone carrying cells or pay; couriers; night-shift workers going home |
| **The Corner Stairs after 22:00** | Dark, long, between the Mills halls. The stair crews work them | Anyone alone, anyone who looks like they carry something |
| **Service voids and crawlspaces** | Vent kids own them, adults can't fit | Kids; the occasional thief who got in |
| **The Combs and shaft hatches** | Between floors, behind grilles; tollers and netters | Rats, ledge recoverers |
| **Chute rooms and the Bale Hall (8)** | Noisy, wet, covered by the Jaws' clatter. Bylaw 58: persons found in a chute are waste | Pickers; people who owed the wrong pickers |
| **The drops in the Sump** | The Hooks' territory. Bodies are found there and the drop is closed for the day | Whoever was put there |
| **The outer stair and the Flats** | Not part of the Structure (Bylaw 69). Nobody's law | Anyone who is sent out |
| **The Old Post (8)** and other bars | Drink, cheap spirit, old grudges. The cage front is for keeping drunks away from the bottles | Drunks, mostly each other |
| **Hot-bunk racks** | Strangers sharing a bed in turns; things go missing and people argue about it | The loudest arguer |
| **The lift cars** | Old Two stops at every call; a car is a small locked room for the length of a ride | Women, usually, and the Guild knows it and does not like to say |

### 2.2 Why

Mostly for small, ordinary reasons. In rough order of how often:

1. **Quarrels.** A noise complaint, a shared bed, a debt of three credits, a child insulted, a card game. Alcohol and bad sleep
   in the mix. These are the majority, and they end in a black eye, a cut hand or, now and then, a funeral.
2. **Debt.** A visit from a collector (05 §6), a keeper's lad, a "friend of the house". The visit is rarely violent the first
   time. It is *close*. A hand on the shoulder in a stairwell. A message with a name in it. The worst injuries are from the
   third visit.
3. **Robbery.** A cell, a pay packet, a bag of pills, a pass. A teenager with a washer-sling and a friend. Mostly the victim is
   alone and carrying.
4. **Homes.** Most violence in most places happens indoors, between people who share a bed. It is no different here, and in
   a stack with eight people to a square there is more of it, and a wall that carries sound has taught the neighbours not to
   hear.
5. **Enforcement.** A bench's punishment. A floor meeting that went too far. The Hooks' hook. A sublet baron's man collecting
   rent. A Protective who is tired and has a wand.
6. **Pay-for-hire.** Rare, expensive, and nearly always arranged above floor 60 and carried out below it.

### 2.3 What holds it back

- **Witnesses.** The best protection is that someone knows your mother. A burglar seen by two neighbours who know his family
  usually stops (06a, the Co-op).
- **The Round.** Whistle, don't fight. Three short blasts call the floor, and the floor does the rest. People turn up in their
  socks. It is startlingly effective, because it is not about courage.
- **The bench and the meeting.** For small things, restitution in kind. For big things, a hearing the next evening in front of
  everyone, which most people fear more than a beating.
- **The keepers.** Hot-bunk and sublet keepers throw out troublemakers with a lad and a brass ring. Keepers hate trouble: it
  costs them custom.
- **The Quiet.** HPS and the Ninefold; the Sump's version of policing (05).
- **HPS.** A four-hour response (Essential cover) and a form. In practice HPS exists to produce the **incident number** the
  Mutual wants. It prevents nothing and solves nothing and everyone knows it.

### 2.4 Numbers

A working estimate, for a Mills floor like 17: about three thousand people. In an ordinary year:

- About **one death by violence**, two in a bad year. A knife or a pipe. A third of them involve somebody who was drunk.
- **Three to six** serious injuries a year that need a mender: a broken arm, a cut that needs closing, a head wound.
- **Several hundred** thefts, most of them a cell or a few credits, nearly all unreported.
- **Dozens** of fights, mostly noisy, mostly in or just outside the crust halls.
- About **four to five deaths** from the wet chest or the dust for each one by violence, which is a thing Mills people say when
  somebody tells them the Sump is dangerous.

In the Sump, double all of it. On the Flats, nobody counts.

### 2.5 Fire

The crime that every floor punishes together is fire. A Mills hall is dry timber partitions, plastic sheet, cell cases,
cooking oil and a hundred people asleep. The Works had sprinklers; they went off the first time anyone remembers and flooded 14,
and nobody has dared fix them. In Y9 a solvent fire on Line Two killed six workers, and the union (the Cell) was founded on
it. The Mills do not let you forget.

- **Arson** is the one crime where the floor meeting can order *exile*, and most do, on the nearest outer stair.
- Every landing has a **bucket rack**: two sand buckets, a bell. The bell is kept in tune; a fire bell is not a noise.
- A **cell fire** is the one people dread: a shorted cell cannot be put out with water. The answer is sand, a bucket, and
  distance. A bay of cells charging overnight on a bad socket board is how most Mills fires start.
- A **still fire** (a Stinker's still going up) is the second-most-common cause, and the reason a landlord will not let you keep
  one.
- The Company's response to a fire is a **standards matter** about the damage to the fixtures.

**In play:** a **fire event** (late in the day, in a neighbour's unit, spreading along the cell racks) in which the player's
bucket rack or sand sack matters and a bad socket board is the cause. Not a combat mini-game; a quarter-hour of bucket-passing
that shows the floor cooperating. The aftermath is the part that sticks: a burnt hall's tenants arrive at the player's door
looking for a corner.

### 2.6 After

Someone has been hurt. What happens next is a ladder like medicine's (08):

1. **The floor.** A neighbour binds it. Clean cloth and gut tabs and "sit down".
2. **A mender.** Sister Odette on 19 closes a cut for what you have. Haskell on 15 sets bones, **badly if he's tired**, and the
   Mills know which days he is tired.
3. **Vey's counter.** Wound film, 8 cr. The pharmacist cannot diagnose.
4. **Vey Sixty-Four.** 180 cr before anyone looks at you.
5. **The Carry.** If it was the end.

The Company bills for none of it, but it bills for the damage to the fixtures.

## 3. Weapons

### 3.1 Why there are almost no guns

There are a few, and they are a rumour with a price. The reasons are the Stack's, not a rule:

- **The Works made cells and chips.** Not weapons, not ammunition. There is no casting shop, no powder, no tooling for it.
  Whatever came up the coast in a crate before the Drowning is a very short list.
- **The tower is full of things you must not shoot.** Pipes that carry water to a thousand people, cell banks that burn, glass
  that cannot be replaced, pumps that hold the Sump up. Every adult knows this. The Pumpmen's code says the pump comes first;
  a person who fired a weapon on 5 would be put in the water by their own neighbours before the bench met.
- **The Company holds them.** HPS keeps a small armoury at Station Sixty-One (the **Cage**). The Sovereign details on the Crown
  floors carry sidearms; no other Protective carries more than a wand. The Company's reasoning, in a staff memo that went
  down the chute in Y74: *the fewer firearms in the Structure, the fewer reasons to be afraid of the people holding the keys
  to the lifts.*
- **The Bylaws never mention a weapon.** If HPS ever finds a gun it charges it as an unregistered fixture (Bylaw 14, class D,
  10 cr) and keeps the gun. Nobody can remember the charge being used on a knife.

So what exists is rare, pre-Drowning, hidden, and valuable. A working **pistol with six rounds** is worth a year's rent on a
Mills lease. Rounds are sold singly, in a tin, by the same people who sell a surgeon's needle. The Sump has a few. The
Crown's households have more than they have admitted. Bastion's guards carry them; the prisoners do not.

**Alternative:** firearms do not exist at all in the Stack. The tower has kept itself disarmed by accident and by design (the
Pumpmen, the Round, the Company), and the weapons below are the whole armoury. This is the version the file leans on. If the
designer wants a rumoured gun for a hook, it is a hook, not a feature.

### 3.2 What people carry

Everything is made from what the tower throws away. Prices are Mills street prices.

| Thing | What it is | Who carries it | Price |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A sock** | A dead H-cell (a brick of metal and chemistry, as heavy as two Vitabricks) in a long sock, swung. Cheap, anonymous, and heavy enough to break bones | Everyone who wants to be left alone; robbers; the worst thing in the Stack for its price | free (2 cr as scrap) |
| **A pipe** | A forearm's length of Works conduit, ends filed. Looks like a tool | Pickers, carriers, boys | free |
| **A mill knife** | A bale-picker's hook-knife or a scoring knife off a Vitabrick press, honed | Pickers, ranchers, cooks. Everyone has a knife for work, and what it is "for" is a matter of mood | 6 cr |
| **A hot-knife** | A heating blade used to weld cartridges and quilt liners. It cuts, it burns, it cauterises. The smell is distinctive | Workshop people. Hated as a weapon because it brands | 10 cr |
| **A dive knife** | The Flats' blunt-tipped cutter for line and net | Divers, magnet fishers | 8 cr |
| **A sling** | A strap and a handful of washers, bearings or cell-casing slugs. A catapult for the clever | Kids; stair crews; roof-watchers | 1 cr |
| **A hook** | The Hooks' long drag hook: a three-metre pole with a steel hook that can catch a coat, a collar or a rope | The Hooks only | not for sale |
| **A pole-net** | Two poles and a net. Used for drones; used on people in a narrow shaft | Netters, tollers | 3 cr |
| **A wand** | The HPS stun baton (see 3.3) | HPS | 120 to 200 cr on the grey market |
| **A poker** | A cut-down conduit with a cell bank and two bare studs. A burn, a smell, a shock that stops a heart in the unlucky. Homemade | Gangs; people who think they are clever | 15 cr plus a cell |
| **A bottle** | A thrown bottle of trike (the poisonous first cut of a still), lye or Works thinner. A stinking mess; a face injury; seldom fatal; lasting | Quarrels | 1 cr |
| **A spear** | A fish-spear or a harpoon head on a pole, from the Flats' divers and eel-men | The Flats and the Sump | 5 cr |
| **A drone** | See 3.4 | Anybody with a hopper and a grudge | 30 cr |

Nothing on this list is a surprise to the Mills. A Mills person does not talk about it; they learn it by growing up there.

### 3.3 The wand, and the cell racket

HPS's only weapon below the Crown is the **wand**: a short stun baton, cell-powered, which runs flat often because HPS buys the
cheapest cells (05). That single fact explains most of how HPS actually works.

- A wand with a full cell stops a grown man for ten seconds and leaves a burn mark. A wand with a flat cell is a very
  expensive club.
- Each Attendant is issued **one cell** a week. Their shift is eight hours and the wand takes a cell in about three. So the
  Attendants buy their own, or don't use the wand, or trade a favour for a cell from the people they are meant to be
  controlling.
- A **Protective at Post Nineteen**, **Aurel Voss**, will let a fight run itself out and file the incident number for 10 cr if
  you ask. He has never used his wand on anyone he did not know to be unarmed.
- **A stolen wand** is a status object. Bylaw 93 (repairing a Company fixture) makes mending one a crime; a Mills repairer will
  do it for the price of a day's work and never let it be seen.

**In play:** **wand cell** is an ordinary 1x2 cell item with a tag that makes the Mills nervous. An HPS Attendant is a client
who wants cells, and a bad one: pay in credits, ask no questions, and the player owns a small favour in Post Nineteen.
**Mending a wand** is a repair contract that is, quietly, a crime. The player can decline; the Round will think better of them
for it.

### 3.4 Drones as weapons

The Stack is full of drones now (09 §3.9) and everything that can fly can fall.

- **Dropping.** "What you drop lands on somebody" is painted in tar at the Spill Door on 4. A hopper with a heavy bay,
  parked over a stairwell, is a brick on a string. Landings have bells; the bell means *look up*.
- **Netting.** The toll nets in the risers are for drones. A net in a stairwell is for a person who is running.
- **Cargo.** A hopper carries two kilos. A two-kilo twist of anything is a message; sometimes it is a threat.
- **The rat-hunter.** A grey drone with a hook and a loud motor that some looters fly into shafts to bring down a rat's frame
  (06b). The rats retaliate in kind and the shafts are worse for it.

The Company's answer has been **net grilles** on band gates and the landings, which stop hoppers and anyone thrown through.

### 3.5 The Flats and the Sump

The Flats have the harpoon and the eel-spear because they have fish; the Sump has the pole-hook because it has the drops. Both
are tools first. Neither is carried in the way a knife is in the Mills: a diver without a knife is dead, and a diver with a
spear on a landing is just a diver.

The Pumpmen go nowhere without a spanner. A **pump spanner** is a metre of founding-era steel with a flat jaw and the weight to
hold a valve against a flood. A sixty-year-old Pumpman with a spanner can do more with it than most people with a poker, and
knows that if he ever does, he will be on the bench the next evening, and so will his son.

### 3.6 The Crown

The Crown has the Stack's weapons, and has never needed to use them. Sovereign details carry holstered sidearms they have not
drawn in the Structure since Y70. Crown households keep antiques (a duelling pair, a hunting rifle, a ceremonial sword, all
real, all licensed "for display") and a few things in a locked drawer that are neither. The Crown's attitude, in a Founders'
Table minute: *an armed household is a household that has lost an argument.*

### 3.7 In play

- **No fight mechanic.** Weapons are items, contracts and consequences.
- **Contraband tag.** Weapons ride in a bay with a **hot** tag: the Mutual won't insure it, the Guild spotters log it, the Ninefold
  charge double and a Round roundsman will want a word. Most are never asked for by name; they come as unmarked "don't open"
  parcels (the rat code forbids opening sealed parcels).
- **Repair and refusal.** Mend a knife? A hot-knife is a tool. A poker is a contract with a name on it. Declining is a standing
  rise with the Round; accepting is a standing rise with the stair crews and a reason for a warden to look closer.
- **Aftermath slips.** After an event, the board carries bills (a mender's, Sister Odette's), a wanted-list ("who has seen a
  grey sock with a name on it"), and a funeral-supplies order (candles, a sheet, a carrying door).
- **What the player can sell.** Cells, hot-knife repair, wand mending (grey), sand buckets, bells (always), mill knives (honed),
  sock stitching (a sock is a sock). The Round buys bells and buckets by the dozen, and the stair crews buy slugs.

## 4. Crime as a trade

Crime in the Stack is a job like the others. People do it because the other jobs pay thirty credits a day, the rent never
stops climbing, and the stair is dark. Most never think of themselves as criminals; they think of themselves as people with a
side line. The trades below are the ones the player will actually bump into. None of them is glamorous.

### 4.1 Sock-lifters and line-stealers

**Sock-lifters** take cells. The commonest are children, aged nine to fourteen, working in pairs in a crush: the lift lobby at
06:00, the Turnstile at 20:00, the Fall-line outside the crust halls. One bumps; one cuts the sock string. A cell is sold to a
bench on the same floor within the hour: a tin-lid price, 5 cr for a charged H-cell that costs 18 new. The bench has a
cell-shaped hole in its books where the origin should be.

**Line-stealers** take power. A Mills hall's socket board is metered; the power behind it is not. A man with a screwdriver and
a morning can tap a lease off its neighbour's line so that the neighbour pays. Cells "charged off stolen lines at half the
store price" is a trade of its own (02): the price you see on a charge-shop sign is the price of the risk. Many Mills fires
begin with a tap.

Both are mostly done by the poor to the poor. The Round will deal with a sock-lifter by sitting the child's mother down at
the floor meeting; she pays back the cell in work, and the child goes on the bin rota for a month. It is generally the same
child again by spring.

### 4.2 Stair crews

The Mills's stairs belong to whoever is on them. Young people of fourteen to twenty-one, three or four to a crew, hold the
Corner Stairs after dark. They carry slings and a lamp. They charge **stair money**: one credit to pass a landing, two if you
carry a bag. Pay it and nothing happens. Don't, and a washer hits the wall beside your head, and the next one doesn't.

- They are not strangers. Every crew member is somebody's child on the same floor, and their mothers know it, and the Round
  knows it, which is how most crews are restrained.
- They pay up. The usual tithe is a third, to a keeper or a baron's man, in exchange for being left alone and for names.
- They are apprentices. Everything in the Mills (rats, collectors, thieves, wardens) starts as a boy on a stair.
- The **Ninety-Nines**, after the Long Stair's *Ninety-Nine Steps*, hold the Corner Stairs on 15 to 19. Their leader is
  **Tobin Hallett**, nineteen, who is kind to old women, has a washer-sling and a way of looking at a bag. He wants to be a rat
  and he is a good enough shot with a sling to fly a hopper through a hoop.

**In play:** a **stair money** event on the way to a night errand: pay a credit or take the lift (a toll), or go by hopper (which
the crews hate). A crew member can be a client: they buy slugs, string, a mended lamp, and a favour.

### 4.3 The pass trade

A pass is paper saying you may be here. The pass trade is where the divide meets the street.

- **Lending.** A Middle widow with a standing pass to the Terraces (her daughter works on 72) rents it by the hour, five
  credits, to a Mills person with an appointment. The deal is "go, come back, leave it in my box". It works for weeks, then a
  warden notices that the widow has been up four times today.
- **Tailing.** A person with a day pass and a friend walks up together. The Turnstile counts heads in a crush; it fails at
  20:00, when everyone is crowding down. A **tail** pays 3 cr.
- **Forging.** Day passes are on blue card with a punched number. **Noor Aldous**, sixty-two, on 29 (the Chalk), has a punch
  and a stamp pad and a cheerful manner. Her passes pass. She won't forge for the Crown, "because they would know".
- **Stealing from the dead.** A pass is registered to a name. A dead name's pass is good until the register catches up, which
  can be weeks. The Carry is a seller's market for coats.
- **20:00.** The Turnstile on 30 closes the band at 20:00 for anyone without a pass. A crush of people with the wrong card
  gathers at 19:50 every night. Selling them a "late pass" is a trade.

**In play:** **pass** items with a **warm** or **cold** status (genuine, forged, borrowed, dead). A forged pass is the cheapest
key to an upper contract and the most likely thing to cost the player a night in the Pen. Nobody makes the player use one.

### 4.4 Fences and hocks

Stolen goods have to go somewhere. In the Stack the answer is nearly always **down**, because the further down the tower, the
less anyone asks.

- **The hocks** (the Old Hands' pawn stalls, Bylaw 79: pawnbroking needs two licences, "because pawned goods are presumed to
  need repair") are the polite end of it.
- **The Voided** (06b) resell undone voided goods; the same shop sells a stolen coat as VOID-restored.
- **The Exchange on 28** is the best market in the Mills. A stolen first-fit part goes through three hands before it arrives
  there and every hand is clean.
- **HPS's Recovery Store** is, in practice, the largest fence in the tower. What it "recovers" goes on the company store's
  discount shelf after thirty days (05). A Mills shop that is robbed sees its stock there a month later at 80% off. It is
  perfectly legal and nobody has ever found it funny.

### 4.5 Shakedowns

The commonest crime by value is not theft but **payment for the absence of trouble**.

- **Wardens.** Floor wardens are paid per report. A warden who needs a good month visits a shop, notes an irregularity, and
  mentions that the report has not yet gone in. "Pooling" is the word on 17: you pay the pool, he files nothing. The usual
  rate is 10% of a week's rent.
- **Brand protection agents.** Paid 150 cr and a 10% cut of fines (05). Not corrupt in the bribe sense. They simply have an
  incentive.
- **Keepers' lads.** A hot-bunk keeper's tall nephew, a ring of brass on his belt, who checks on a late payer.
- **Sublet barons' "friends of the house".** They collect for the baron on a Friday.
- **Collectors.** Brisk & Lowe and the Varga Books (05, 06b). Both are legal.

Everyone calls it "pooling" and everyone understands what it means. Nobody calls it protection, because nobody is protecting
anything.

### 4.6 The tin lender

Credit above the Company's level is a **tin**: a biscuit tin with names written on the lid in pencil. You borrow 10 credits
from the tin on Monday; on Friday you owe 12. The "Friday fifth" is a fifth a week, which is simple interest of 1,040% a year
and which nobody calls interest, because it is "a friendly turn". A tin lender is usually someone's grandmother. The
enforcement is a conversation on a landing, and she has a grandson.

A debt that cannot be paid goes in the next tin up: a keeper's, a baron's, and finally, if it is large enough, to a
collector firm who buys it for a tenth of its face and is much less friendly (05 §6).

### 4.7 Scams

The Stack is full of people smart enough to do a small bad thing well.

- **The stamped half.** A real VOID stamp on a good item, bought cheap; the stamp is faked, the item is the thing it should
  not have been. Sold as "voided and restored" at a discount, to someone who will resell as "Restored".
- **The wet cup.** Skywater rain in a Skywater bottle, 40 cr a litre. Mills tap water in a Skywater bottle, 15 cr. The cap's
  tamper ring is reglued with a hot-knife; the label is printed on a screen from the chute. Skywater's inspectors do not
  look below 30.
- **The Ascension Draw.** Tickets bought in bulk with scrip from a child, resold at a markup with a story: "My cousin won the
  lease". Nobody has ever won. People buy anyway.
- **False incident numbers.** The Mutual won't look at a claim without one. A form can be bought for 10 cr from a Protective, or
  printed for 2 cr from a friend. The Mutual denies both.
- **The sob story at the Turnstile.** A woman and a child, a lost day pass, a mother dying on 41. The child is hers. The mother
  is dying. The pass was never lost. It is a cheap trick because it is half true.
- **Rigged Fall-book.** A bookie who knows the first big thing before the bell does, because he sorted the chute himself.

### 4.8 The Company's own crimes

Without a sermon, a list of what is plainly in the books:

- **HPS's incident numbers** are sold by the people who issue them. Dispatch accepts calls in batches at the top of the hour
  and books the response as "inside four hours" (05).
- **The Recovery Store** sells back recovered goods to the people they were stolen from, at storage rates.
- **Vey's Continuity pricing** (08) makes the first dose cheap and every refill dear, on purpose, on products that
  people need monthly for life. The Company calls this a "patient journey".
- **The Ledger rounds against you** and says so, visibly (04).
- **The Pen** charges a night's board at a Terraces rate to anyone it holds.
- **The sanitary service** collects bodies for free and returns them as "recovered process water" (08).

None of these is secret. All of them are legal. That is what the Mills mean, when they say the Company has no crimes, only
standards.

### 4.9 Contract harm

A hired killing in the Stack is rare, costly, and arranged above 60. Below 60 it is simpler to arrange a fall, a debt, a
lost pass, a bed in the wrong house, a name sent to the Pen. The Crown settles upstairs. The Terraces have lawyers. The Middle
file standards charges against each other, which is its way of murder. In the Mills a quarrel that reaches a knife is mostly
done in anger, and the knife is a mill knife, and the owner is on the stairs the next morning sitting very still with a mender.

**Hook:** every few years a body is found in the Spill with its pockets turned out and a Terraces pass in a boot. Nobody
claims it, nobody reports it. The Hooks close the drop, as always, and the Tide Folk are sent for, as always, and the Ninefold
write it in the Sump register, as always. The pass is the thing. It has been used in the week since the person died.

## 5. What people take

### 5.1 Why

For the oldest reasons: because they are in pain, or tired, or cold, or bored, or hungry, or frightened, or grieving, or they
simply want to be somewhere else for an hour. The Stack supplies every one of these in quantity. A hot-bunk sleeper on the
second shift, sleeping through the afternoon noise on a shared sheet, takes a Lull. A bale picker with a cracked rib takes
whatever the mender has. A night crew worker takes a Rouse at 03:00. A teenager with nothing to do takes the breath from the
low-tide grate. A Terraces manager takes an evening tablet and a glass of something and does not discuss it.

None of this is exotic. The invented names are Vey's (they had a marketing department) and the Mills's (they had each other).

### 5.2 The rule: the Company licenses, it does not ban

The Company does not have a drug law. It has a **medicine licence** (Bylaw 90: only Vey or the Spire may treat, and "first aid
that works is treatment"), a **smoking-square fee** (Bylaw 86: smoking is permitted only in squares designated by fee), and
a **water-treatment licence** under which a still is a still, whatever it makes. So:

- Spirit made without a licence is **unlicensed water treatment** (250 cr, apparatus confiscated).
- Tobacco-substitute smoked in an undesignated square is a **standards matter** of a few credits.
- A pill sold by a non-Vey seller is **unlicensed provision of care** (200 cr).
- A counterfeit pill is **a brand offence**: the gravest the Company has (05 §4). Counterfeiters are chased harder than
  poisoners, because counterfeiting costs Vey money and poisoning only costs the buyer.

The only drugs the Company hunts are the ones it doesn't make money on.

### 5.3 Everyday

| Thing | What it is | Price (Mills) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Kof** | Orrin's synthetic coffee, "tastes of toast" | 5 cr for 100 g | The only stimulant in the Middle's cafés. A cup lasts an hour |
| **Tea** | The Canteen's; mint and thyme tea in the Co-op halls | 1 cr a cup | The Mills's actual comfort. A tea urn is the centre of a hall |
| **Bed-leaf** | Dried grow-bed trimmings, rolled in blotter scrap | 1 cr a day | Harsh, grey, popular. Must be smoked in a designated square (B86) |
| **Real leaf** | Tobacco-substitute from the Lee (07) | 25 cr a pouch | A Terraces thing; a Mills man gets one a year |
| **Lowbeer** | A ferment of grow-bed trimmings and insect sugar | 1 to 2 cr a cup | Weak and sour. The Mills's table drink |
| **Mill ale** | Real small beer from grain | 4 cr a mug | Rare, because grain is dear. Celebrations |
| **Ninefold white** | Gin from crust dregs, at the Old Post on 8 | 6 cr a glass | Strong and rough. A cage-front bar |
| **Pale** | A Stinker's clean spirit (03) | 14 cr a bottle | The reason a still is worth more than a still. A real pale tastes of nothing and warms like a lamp |
| **Trike** | The poisonous first cut of a still | 1 cr | Sold as pale by cheats and the unlucky; the Old Hands notch the cap three times (10) |

**Blind spirit** is the Stack's quiet killer: a bad still run, or a mislabelled bottle. Every Mills shop has a story about
somebody who went blind or went to the Carry on a Saturday. The Stinkers' reputation depends on their cuts, and a Stinker
who loses a customer to trike loses a lot more than a customer.

### 5.4 Vey's shelf

Vey sells three pills that matter, and they all have a counter or a lock on the dispenser.

**Lull** (8 cr for ten). A sleeping tablet. Marketed as "Rest, as Directed". Taken nightly by hot-bunk sleepers on Second,
who have to sleep through the afternoon noise, and by anyone with the Hum in their ears. After a month, you cannot sleep
without it. After a year, you cannot sleep with it at the dose on the label.

**Rouse** (7 cr for ten). A stay-awake tablet, marketed as "Shift-Ready". Taken by night crews, rats on a long flight, chute
clearers, bin hands, and anyone owed a double shift. One gets you through a double shift. Four days later you are crying at the
bottom of a stair and cannot say why.

**Easement** (a strong painkiller; 35 cr for ten at a Middle pharmacy, with a clinic slip). For Works injuries, dust-lung,
a crushed foot, a cancer. Vey sells it carefully and prices it dearly. The Mills need it more than anyone and can afford it
least. Its price is why the grey market exists. It is also why the Mills bury more people of the pills than of the blades.

Each is sold from a **DoseLock**: a dispenser with a counter that gives out one dose at a time and locks after the course.
When a lock fails or a refill is overdue, the dispenser goes down the chute with the doses still inside (04).

### 5.5 Where the grey stuff comes from

Pills move in the Mills along five routes:

1. **Cracked.** DoseLock units chiselled open at the Hooks' sorting room on 6 or at a Mills bench. Some doses crushed.
   Cracked Lull is a steady grey good (04). **18 to 20 cr** for a course. Most doses are intact; the labels are not.
2. **Short-drawer.** A nurse at a clinic gives a patient the dose and keeps the rest of the blister. Yetunde on Vey Sixty-Four
   does this, and has done it for eleven years, for people who cannot afford the label. A drawer is not a crime in a pharmacy
   with no inventory; Vey audits quarterly.
3. **Vey-pattern.** Counterfeit, pressed from crushed retired Vey tablets mixed with chalk and sugar (05). The pill has "some
   of the real drug in unknown amounts". The stampers treat it as the gravest brand offence. The Rain Church treats it as
   charity. The Mills treat it as a lottery. **8 cr** for ten.
4. **Wrack.** The Spire's expired stock, dumped at sea on the ebb, washes up on the Flats in sealed blister packs (07). Free
   to the Flats, 5 cr a strip to the Landing, 15 cr by the time it reaches the Mills with a Ninefold seal on it. Often still
   good. Sometimes not.
5. **The Lee.** Pills in a cell casing, in a care parcel, in the lining of a coat, from Bastion by way of the Lee (07).

In practice the Mills buy from the person they know: the tea woman, the keeper's counter, the nurse who looks after their
mother. Nobody buys from a stranger more than once.

### 5.6 Made at home

**Bed-leaf** (above) and **lowbeer** (above) are the Mills's own. Two others deserve their own entries.

**Hum-caps.** A pale mushroom, grown in the dark cabinets (09) on frass substrate, and grown best, the Co-op growers swear, on
floors 20 to 28, next to the Dark Floors. A cap costs 4 cr. Eaten raw, it makes an evening of heavy limbs and a feeling that
the building is speaking. Old Hands say it is the Hum itself, put into words. Most users say it is nothing. It is the one
drug the Co-op has an official opinion on (*"We do not grow it. We know who does"*). Users are quiet, odd and rarely any
trouble.

**Hook:** hum-cap users who time it say the voice repeats every nineteen minutes. They do not say what it says. An Archivist
reader on 20 has asked three of them to write it down. They do, and the three pages do not match, but each page is nineteen
lines long.

**Low breath.** At low tide the sweet chemical breath from the drowned halls comes up through the grates on 4 to 6, and some
people sit by the grates and take it. It is cheap (free), it works in seconds, and it is how the Sump loses its young. A
bad tide takes three or four in a night: they pass out in the fumes and the water rises. The Pumpmen's code says anyone
found by the grates when the Level is rising is hauled out and put on the pumps for the shift, whether they want it or not,
which is the nearest to a kindness the Sump has. The Widows pull out the ones the Pumpmen miss.

**In play:** neither is a product the player sells, and the player cannot grow either one by accident. They are the floor's
weather. A **low-breath tide** closes a Sump grate. A **hum-cap customer** wants a cabinet's output and not the name.

### 5.7 Up the tower

The rich take their drugs in glass.

- **The Middle** is anxious, and takes **Even**: a Vey tablet "for the evening", 22 cr for ten, on a clinic slip, kept in
  a drawer next to the licence. A Middle shopkeeper takes it so that the warden's report doesn't make her shake. Nobody
  discusses it. A Mills person who asks for Even is told it isn't for them.
- **The Terraces** have wine, real glass bottles from the Pier, and Even, and a private Vey nurse who "has a word".
- **The Crown** has **Reverie**: a Vey Private Line vial, 400 cr, delivered by hand to the household's door by a steward who
  has never visited below sixty. It makes the Crown's famous stillness easy. It is used by people who believe stillness is
  a virtue and who find it difficult. They do not call it a drug. Their housekeepers know exactly how many vials come each
  week and not how many are used.
- **The Pleasure Pier** (07) sells every intoxicant that a licence can be written for, by the boat, to anyone who can get there.

The legal difference between the Crown's vial and the Mills's cracked dose is a stamp and a price.

### 5.8 What it does to people

Plainly, because the Mills would say it plainly:

- **Lull** is the quiet one. People look rested and are not. A Lull-taker drops an item, forgets a rent day, sleeps through
  a knock and is pulled out by the ankles. By the second year they are in a house that gives them a bed on credit.
- **Rouse** eats the body. Weight goes, hands shake, temper goes. A rat on Rouse crashes a drone.
- **Easement** takes everything else. A habit costs 6 to 10 cr a day. A Mills labourer earns 30. After
  bed and food, little remains, so the habit comes first and the rent second. The person who owes the tea woman owes
  everyone. Sooner or later they are on a landing at 05:00 and the Round finds them.
- **Counterfeits** kill in clusters. A batch of Vey-pattern "easers" from a press house on 11 killed eight people on 14 in a
  night, in the winter of Y83. It is why Sister Odette keeps a tin of samples from every death.
- **Hum-caps** do very little, and the worry is what they do for the Archivists.
- **Spirit** is the drug people forgive.

Withdrawal is the Stack's ordinary sickness, and the floors know it by name. **"A bad week"** is the shakes and the sweats; the
Mills put someone through it in a cot at a mender's, with a bucket and a neighbour. Sister Odette runs a four-cot room on 19
for exactly this, and charges what you have.

### 5.9 Who sells, and how it moves

Not dealers on corners. The Stack sells drugs the way it sells everything else: by someone you already know.

- **The tea woman.** A crust-hall urn and a drawer under the counter. **Lorna Quist** at the Coater has sold Lull from under hers
  for eighteen years. She gives credit and keeps it in pencil.
- **The keeper's counter.** Hot-bunk houses sell Lull and Rouse to their shifts, bundled with the bed. The rail has three names
  and one tablet; the second sleeper takes theirs.
- **The nurse.** A short-drawer, a cough candy, a small word.
- **The plasma queue.** After the draw room on 9, you get a sweet tea and a biscuit and, if you ask, a Lull. The buyers know
  their regulars.
- **Stair doctors.** People who say they are nurses on a landing, in a white coat from the chute.
- **Hoppers.** The Stack is full of drones now. A twist of paper in a hopper with a dummy disc goes from a hatch to a hatch,
  sender and receiver never meeting. The Round hates it: it takes away the one thing that kept trade honest, which was
  faces.

The Mills do not talk about a "dealer". They say *she sells*. Everyone is someone's cousin.

### 5.10 What the floors do

- **The Co-op's Round** walks for water. A pill is "a matter for the family". If it kills, it becomes a floor meeting. The
  sentence is restitution in kind (care for the family) and, for a seller who has killed twice, leaving the floor.
- **The Pumpmen** forbid breath near the pump stairs and run any seller of pills off 5 to 7 the same day. They need hands that
  can pull a valve.
- **The Ninefold** hold the dock where the Lee and the Flats land, and their toll is one in nine. Pills ride in loads of eight.
- **The Hooks** hold the drops. Dispensers from the chute pass through their sorting room; they sell them on for chiselling.
- **HPS** attends if Company property was involved (the dispenser was Vey's).
- **Vey** hires brand agents to find counterfeiters. It does not hire anyone to find where the legal ones went wrong.
- **The Rain Church** gives its water to anyone, and its Wet Servants find the dying on the stairs and give them a cup.
- **The Tide Folk** take the dead, whoever they were, for whatever is in their pockets.

### 5.11 In play

- **Medicine items** carry a three-way tag: **licensed** (Vey), **cracked** (grey, from a dispenser), **counterfeit** (Vey-pattern).
  A cracked course might be crushed; a counterfeit course is a lottery the player can see only after the harm is done
  (analyser: Edda Quaile's tin).
- **A mender's contracts** are honest work: cloth, sand, candles, clean water, a sterilising lamp, **Easement for the dying**.
  The player can supply her.
- **A dealer's contracts** are always dressed as something else: "a twist to 14", "a parcel that rattles", "hopper, no disc".
  The rat code says don't open it; the Round says nothing; the Mutual won't insure it. The player can refuse, which costs
  nothing, or take it, which costs standing with the Round and opens the door to a tea woman's trust.
- **Aftermath, not a mini-game.** A counterfeit batch shows up as **bills and bodies**: a funeral order, a mender's overload,
  the Round's grim mood, a rise in sand and sheets. The player may have sold the cloth that wrapped them.
- **Regulars.** A client who comes to the player's counter every day for a cheap Lull-adjacent order gets visibly worse over
  weeks. The player does not have to do anything about it; the game shows it anyway.
- **No score for any of it.** Dependence is a texture, not a stat; the world remembers.

## 6. People

Ten people who live in this file. None of them is a villain, a hero or a boss.

- **Lorna Quist, "Tea Lorna."** Fifty-one. Keeps the urn at the Coater and has done for eighteen years. Before that she
  packed pills on a Vey line in the Middle until the line was automated. She sells Lull and Rouse from the drawer under the
  counter at a small margin, and keeps credit in pencil on the back of tea-bag boxes. Her rules: nothing to anyone under
  eighteen, nothing to anyone who comes in with a child, and nothing to anyone she has already had to bury. The third rule
  has made her poorer than the others. Everyone in the hall knows. Nobody has ever reported her.
- **Tobin Hallett.** Nineteen. Leads the **Ninety-Nines**, the stair crew that holds the Corner Stairs from 15 to 19. Polite to
  old women, who he carries bags for (and charges nothing). Aims a washer-sling well enough to put a bearing through a hoop
  at twenty paces. Gives a third to a keeper's man on Fridays. Wants a rat's frame, and has stolen the parts for one in
  pieces, over a year.
- **Aurel Voss.** Thirty-eight. An HPS Attendant at Post Nineteen. His mother was a Protective too. Buys his own wand cells
  with his own pay because the issue cell dies in three hours. Writes the incident number for anybody who asks nicely and
  sells it for 10 cr if they don't. Has drawn his wand on a man once, a man he knew to be unarmed, in front of a witness, and
  has not stopped thinking about it.
- **Edda Quaile.** Forty-four. Counts doses for Sister Odette. A former Vey pharmacy assistant who was let go for refusing to
  stop giving advice. She tells a counterfeit by taste, weight and the sound of the pill on a spoon. Keeps a tin on a shelf of
  samples from every overdose she has cleaned up after. Each has a date and a floor on a slip of cloth. The tin has
  nineteen slips in it. It is not a collection.
- **Noor Aldous.** Sixty-two. Pass forger, floor 29 (the Chalk). A cheerful person who makes tea for customers. Her passes pass,
  and she says, when asked, that it is because she believes in them. Never forges for the Crown: "They'd know". Her one
  regret is a pass she made for a boy of sixteen who wanted to see daylight, and who used it, and did not come back.
- **Pim Garrity.** Fourteen. Goes for a breath at the 5 grate when the tide is low. Half the Sump's children have, and he
  is the one the Widows know by name, because he has been hauled out twice. His mother stands wet time for him when the
  bench sets it, and does not say what she thinks.
- **Bev Sandling.** Forty-nine. A roundsman on 21 with a bad knee and a bell. Has walked the Round for twenty-three years.
  Knows exactly who sells what on her floor, and has made a decision to know only half. "A floor needs one thing it doesn't
  look at," she says, "and I picked." Last winter she buried a nineteen-year-old and has been looking at it since.
- **Joss Penhale.** Twenty-six. The lad at the **Long Shift** hot-bunk house on 12 (06b). He carries a brass ring on his belt
  for the knocks and uses it for knocking. He is tall and was a gentle child, and the job has made him a different shape.
  He has never hit a sleeper who didn't deserve it, by his count. His count is his own.
- **Mother Varga.** (Existing, 05.) Keeps the Sump's chalk record on the cage wall at the Old Post. She knows who sold what to
  whom on the dock and writes none of it down.
- **Lucan Ashby-Reed.** Fifty-seven. A "wellness steward" on 93, in a grey suit, who delivers Reverie to six Crown households
  by hand each Thursday. He has not been below sixty since Y74. He believes he is a nurse. He would be surprised to be
  called a dealer.

## 9. Events

Random, daily or weekly, each one a small thing with a visible mark on the world.

1. **A bad batch.** Vey-pattern pills from a press house have killed some people on a nearby floor overnight. The next morning
   the board has twice the usual sand, sheets, candles and clean cloth, and the Round is in a mood.
2. **Wand shortage.** HPS has run out of cells. Post Nineteen is open and nobody comes out. Fights go on a little longer.
3. **Stair money up.** The crews have a new boy, or a debt. The Corner Stairs cost more for a week.
4. **The 20:00 crush.** A band-gate sweep. Passes are checked. A forged one is a night in the Pen; a borrowed one is a lesson.
5. **A low-breath tide.** Closed grates on 4 to 6, sand for the Pumpmen, three names on the Widows' list.
6. **A fire.** A shorted cell bank on 18 spreads along the racks. Bells, buckets, sand. The floor behaves well.
7. **A sweep.** A scheduled HPS sweep on the Sump. The Quiet delivers a still and a bootleg radio. The player's contacts vanish
   for a week.
8. **The Spire dumps.** A launch off the Spire throws expired stock into the bay on the ebb. A week later the Flats have
   more pills than they have shelves. Prices drop at the Landing; the Ninefold toll hits.
9. **A warden's bad month.** Reports are due. Shops get visited. A "pooling" rate of 10% goes round the floor in whispers.
10. **A death on the dock.** The Hooks close a drop; the Ninefold write it down; the Tide Folk come. A pass in a boot.
11. **A rigged Fall-book.** A bookie has been sorting the chute himself. The Round meets.
12. **Easement runs short.** Vey's shipment is late. The tea women's prices triple; Sister Odette's cots fill.

## 10. Hooks

**Hook:** the incident numbers in Aurel Voss's book skip. The skipped numbers are exactly the days when a body was found in
the Sump. They were not issued; they were not cancelled. Voss does not know who takes them.

**Hook:** the Vey-pattern pills from the press house on 11 are *too good*. A fake that was only chalk and sugar would not have
killed eight people. This one had a measured dose of the real drug. Someone is pressing them with access to Vey's own
powder.

**Hook:** the Spire's dumped blister packs carry expiry dates that are earlier than the Spire's founding. A strip from the
wrack line in Y84 was dated Y31.

**Hook:** a pass Noor Aldous forged for a boy in Y83 was used, a week later, at the Crown Comb. No one has ever got past the
Crown Comb. The pass was not a Crown pass. She would like to know who stamped it.

**Hook:** the sock-lifters never take a Halden-stamped cell with a yellow ring on the terminal. Not once in thirty years. No
one has asked why. The ring is the Dark Floors' mark.

## 11. Questions for the designer

144. **How dark?** Is the underside background (a sock, a twist, a bad week) enough, or does the game ever show a death? 15's
     rule: aftermath only, never the act.
145. **Are there any firearms at all?** The file says none, plus a rumour with a price. A single hook (a pistol in the Crown's
     drawer, a Bastion guard's sidearm) would cost little and change the tone a lot.
146. **Does the player's body ever get hurt?** There is a *worker injury* event (08), a mender, a free treatment. Does an
     injury cost hours, or something worse?
147. **Is dependence a stat?** The file says it is a texture, not a stat: the world remembers, the screen does not score it.
     Is that right, or does the game need an explicit mechanic for the cracked-dose economy?
