# Water

> "Halden Pure. The same water that made the cells."
> (Tap hall poster, floor 17. Someone has written underneath: *and look what it did to them*.)

Everything in the Stack can be argued about except this: a person needs about three litres a day to keep living, and
there is no free litre within sight of the tower. The bay is everywhere, right up to the fourth floor, and you cannot drink
a drop of it. Every drop that is drinkable has passed through somebody's hands and somebody's meter.

A working note on numbers. Prices below are in credits (cr) on a **working scale where a Mills day-wage for unskilled
work is about 30 cr** and a Vitabrick is about 3 cr. If file 04 (economy) sets a different scale, multiply through;
the ratios are the part that matters.

## 1. What is in the bay

### The ingredients

People in the Mills call bay water **brack**, and they know its ingredients the way people elsewhere know the weather.
Not by chemical name, mostly; by smell, colour and what it does to you.

| Local name | What it is | Where it comes from | How you notice it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Salt** | Seawater salt. The bay is near full seawater around the tower; a little less salty at the top in spring, when the old Calder river still pushes meltwater in | The sea, through the broken seawall | Taste; white crust on everything that dries |
| **Slurry** or **the Lagoon** | Metal sludge: nickel, cobalt, cadmium, manganese, lithium salts, a lot of iron | Halden Works' slurry reservoir (officially Lagoon Six, called the Lagoon), which burst in the Drowning and spread across old Calder's streets | Grey-green silt that never quite settles; it stains teeth and buckets |
| **Pyrol** | A heavy, oily solvent the cell lines used by the tank-load | Cell plant drums and pipework in the drowned floors 0–3, still leaking | A sweet, fishy smell in water left standing in the sun; a skin on the surface of a still's last finger |
| **Trike** | A light degreaser solvent from the chip lines, sweet like old hospital air | Chip-fab tanks, the soil under old Calder, which was soaked with it for forty years | You smell it first in a still's first cup. Some people can smell it at levels that already harm you; most can't |
| **Copper green** and **the arsenic** | Copper, arsenic and gallium from chip doping and plating | The chip lines' rinse water, dumped for decades into the ground under the plant | Green bloom on metal; nothing at all in the water itself, which is the problem |
| **Etch** | Fluoride from the acid baths | Chip-fab waste | Mottled teeth in children who grew up drinking ledge-still water made from salt-line feed |
| **The Slick** | A family of "forever" surfactants from coatings, cell separators and fire foam. They do not break down; they bead and sheen | Everywhere in the bay; also the founding-era fire-foam tanks | A rainbow sheen on still water; froth at the tideline that doesn't pop |
| **Outfall** | The tower's own sewage and grey water | The Stack's drains end at the Outfall below the Sump, straight into the bay | Smell, and the gulls |
| **The Red** | Summer algal bloom, toxic, feeding on the outfall | Warm months, calm weeks | The bay turns rust-coloured; the fog smells wrong |

The bay is not uniformly bad. It is **layered**, and people who work it know the layers:

- **The skin**: the top hand's depth, where the Slick and the pyrol film float, where fog and spray are born. Fog nets
  and ledge stills that take spray inherit the skin.
- **The body**: the middle water, salty, metal-heavy but clearer. This is what the salt line pumps.
- **The silt**: the bottom metre over old Calder's streets, where the Lagoon settled. Storms, tides and divers stir it.
  After a storm the whole bay is silt for a week, and every filter in the tower clogs.

### Why boiling doesn't help

The first thing a newcomer to the Mills learns is that boiling bay water makes it worse. Boiling kills germs, which
is the least of the bay's problems; it drives off a little trike and leaves everything else, including the salt and the
metals, more concentrated in what is left. The Company's own Boil Notices (see Events) tell you to boil the tap during
contamination scares, which everyone in the Mills understands to mean "the problem is not germs, and we would rather you
didn't know that."

Distilling (boiling and catching the steam) is different, and the whole stinker trade rests on the difference. Salt and
metals stay behind in the pot. But the two solvents misbehave in opposite directions:

- **Trike** boils easier than water, so it comes over **first**. The rule: **throw the first cup.** (Stinkers say "first
  cup for the bay"; the Rain Church says "first cup for the Crown".)
- **Pyrol** boils harder than water, so it stays in the pot and concentrates. Boil the pot too far down and it starts
  coming over too, along with spray that carries metals. The rule: **leave the last finger.**
- **The Slick** mostly stays behind, but a hard boil "bumps" and throws droplets into the steam. The rule: **slow fire**.

So a good still run is three decisions: when to start keeping, when to stop, and how hot to run. This is exactly the
heads, hearts and tails of the parked still idea, with a reason to exist in the world.

**In play:** the still (any of the water stills below) is a machine with a pot slot, a heat setting and a receiving
vessel slot. Each hour it drips into whatever vessel sits in the output. The first hours' drip is tagged **heads (trike)**,
the middle **clean**, the last **tails (pyrol)**. Swapping the receiving vessel at the right hour is the skill. Heads are
not worthless: trike is a degreaser and sells to the workshops of the Middle as **"cut"** (parts cleaner). Tails go back
into the next pot or down the drain.

**In play:** the heat setting is the "faster or finer" choice. Hot runs give more litres per hour and a wider heads/tails
band (more cup wasted, more risk of bumping). Slow runs give fewer litres and a cleaner heart.

### What it does to people

Nobody in the Stack drinks straight brack on purpose more than once. What harms people is the small, steady leak: badly
cut still water, a cheap filter past its life, salt-line water used for cooking because the tap was off, pipes leaching.

- **The shakes**: tremor in the hands, from the metals. Common in older stinkers and Sump people. A shaky hand is
  the first thing an employer in the Middle looks for.
- **Blue gums**: a dark line along the gums. Children call people with it "inkers".
- **Stone-belly**: kidney trouble. Slow, then sudden. Vey sells a kidney flush, "Vey Clearline", that does nothing a
  litre of clean water wouldn't, at the price of six litres of clean water.
- **Etch-teeth**: mottled, brown-spotted teeth from fluoride. A Mills face. People in the Terraces call a Mills accent "an
  etch-tooth voice".
- **The slows**: what Mills mothers call it when a child grows up drinking bad water and falls behind. Not said loudly.
- **Sweet-sick**: acute solvent poisoning from a badly cut still. Dizziness, vomiting, a sweet breath. If someone in a
  block has sweet-sick, every stinker within three floors expects a visit.

**Hook:** St. Ober's Spire (the hospital tower) runs a "Calder Kidney Programme" that buys stone-belly patients' urine
and blood samples from the plasma buyers for a few credits. Nobody knows what they're studying. The Archivists suspect
St. Ober's has the Works' old toxicology files and is building a case against Halden. More likely, they're building a
product.

### How people test water

Testing is the single biggest difference between licensed water and everyone else's water, and the law is built around
that difference.

- **Halden Purity Strips (PS-4)**: paper strips with four colour pads (salt, metals, solvent, germs). Accurate enough.
  **Sold only to licence holders**, one strip per batch mandatory, 2 cr each. An unlicensed person found with PS-4 strips
  is presumed to be treating water. A black market in strips exists; half of what it sells is old stock, faded and
  useless.
- **The coin**: drop a bright copper or silver-plated coin in a sample overnight. If it greens or blackens, there are
  metals or sulphur. Crude, free, about a third right.
- **The cress test**: the seed savers' method, and the most trusted one in the Mills. Sow a pinch of cress seed on a
  cloth soaked in the sample. In two days, healthy cress means the water won't kill you quickly. Yellow, stunted or
  twisted cress means it will kill you slowly. The seed savers sell **cress twists** (a paper twist of thirty seeds) for
  1 cr. Cress grown on test cloths that pass is eaten.
- **Noses**: people who can smell trike at low levels. A good nose is a job; stinkers pay a nose to check their first-cup
  cut. Noses lose the gift with age and exposure, and the old ones say they can smell nothing at all now, "which is how
  you know I worked."
- **The drop on glass**: let a drop dry on a clean pane. A clear spot is good; a white ring is salt; a rainbow edge is the
  Slick; a brown dot is metal. Fast and free. Every Mills child knows how to read a drop.

**In play:** a water vessel can carry hidden features (salt, metal, solvent, germs), shown as "?" until tested, the same
way the analyser idea reveals relic features. Each test is a different instrument with different reach:
- the drop on glass (free, instant, shows salt and Slick, nothing else),
- the coin (an item you put in the vessel overnight: shows metals),
- the cress test (a cress twist and a cloth in a tray; two days; shows "safe / unsafe" for everything at once, no detail),
- a PS-4 strip (instant, shows all four, consumable, illegal to hold without a licence),
- a nose (a client who comes to the counter; shows solvent; costs a fee).
Untested water sells at a discount; tested water at full price; PS-4-tested water can carry a **stamped batch tag** that
upper floors require.

## 2. How water moves through the Stack

### The Waterhouse

All of Halden's drinking water comes from one place: the **Waterhouse**, the founding-era water plant on floors 5 to 8,
in the Sump. It was never built to give anyone a drink. It was built to make **ultrapure process water** for the chip and
cell lines of Halden Works: the bay pumped in through the Intake on floor 3, then pre-filters, then three stages of
founding-era membranes, then ion beds, then ultraviolet, then a polishing loop. Ultrapure water is so clean it is
"hungry": it dissolves metal from whatever it touches, which is why the Works' pipes were special alloy and glass-lined.

When the Works stopped employing people (~Y41), the Waterhouse kept running for the automated lines. When the Drowning
took the old city's water supply (~Y57), Halden turned part of the Waterhouse's output toward the tower's taps and
discovered it had the only working source of drinkable water for kilometres. The Water Licence followed within the year.

Today the Waterhouse is a fortified Company island in the middle of the Sump: blast doors, HPS posts, its own lift. The
gangs of the Sump live around it the way people once lived around a castle, and for the same reasons.

What matters about the Waterhouse is that **it is failing, and nobody can make its parts.**

- **The membranes**: the heart of it. Founding-era spiral cartridges, each the size of a person, built with a process
  nobody alive understands. There were 640 cartridges in the original trains; around 210 still work. Each year more foul
  beyond cleaning. Output falls with them.
- **The ion beds**: the resin can be regenerated with acid and caustic, slowly, by hand. **Membrane Week** (see Events)
  is when the Company shuts trains down to clean them.
- **The pumps**: founding-era, magnetic-bearing, quiet. When one fails, Halden buys pumps from Vantage at ruinous cost or
  the Old Hands fix it for a fraction and a favour.

**Alternative:** the Waterhouse was never meant to make drinking water at all and still isn't fit to: the Company doesn't
remineralise it properly, so Halden Pure arrives at the tap "hungry" and leaches lead and copper from the old tower
pipework on its way down. Water that left the Waterhouse cleaner than anything on earth arrives in the Mills carrying the
tower's pipes in it. The Company sells a tap-mounted **Conditioner Cartridge** (16 cr, monthly) to fix the problem it
causes. This is the version the Old Hands tell, and it's mostly true.

**Hook:** a working membrane cartridge salvaged by divers from the drowned plant (floors 0–3 had spare-parts stores) would be
worth more than most people's lease. Divers have found casings, empty. Someone has been there first, and recently.

**Hook:** the Waterhouse's output figures are published every quarter in the tap halls. The Archivists have the founding
blueprints. The numbers don't add up: the published output, plus the published allocations to the bands, plus "system
losses", is about a sixth less than what 210 working cartridges should make. The missing sixth goes somewhere through a
line on the blueprints labelled **Process Line 3**, which runs up into the Mills band and stops at floor 22. The Dark
Floors are floors 22 to 26. Chips need ultrapure water.

**In play:** the Dark Floors' draw shows up as a weekly **pressure dip**: on certain nights the tap's flow in the Mills
drops by a third for no announced reason. Players who chart their tap's flow against the hum from the Dark Floors find they
match. Nothing pays for knowing it, except a contract from the Archivists or Static (pirate radio) that pays in
information, and a later choice about what to do with it.

### Three lines: sweet, salt and soft

The Stack has three water systems, and anyone who lives below the Terraces can tell you which pipe is which by touch.

- **The sweet line**: drinking water. Halden Pure from the Waterhouse, pumped up the risers to the top, then fed down by
  gravity through the break tanks. Blue-banded pipes. Metered at every tap.
- **The salt line**: raw bay water, pumped from the Intake, lightly screened, never treated. Used for **flushing toilets
  and for fire sprinklers**, as the old harbour cities did. Red-banded pipes, everywhere, unmetered on most floors (the
  Company charges a flat "Sanitation and Safety" fee inside rent). The salt line is **free poison on tap**, and it is
  the feedstock of nearly every still in the Mills. Treating salt-line water to drink is the commonest water crime in the
  tower.
- **The soft line**: the Crown's and upper Terraces' grey water: baths, showers, basins, garden overflow. Lightly used
  drinking water. It runs down its own green-banded drain stack (founding-era design kept them separate so the plant
  could recover it) to a treatment tank that works badly (its output is sold as service water at 0.15 cr a litre, file 04), and the rest to the Outfall. **Crown grey** is cleaner than
  most of what the Mills drinks. It smells of soap and perfume. Tapping it is theft, and a living.

**In play:** your shop may have a salt-line outlet on the field (free, unmetered, gives brack), a metered sweet-line tap
(costs per litre, gives Pure), and, if you are lucky or criminal, a soft-line tap (free once installed, gives Crown grey;
discovery means a fine and a removal). Three taps, three grades, three risks.

### Fill order and the break tanks

Water is pumped from the Waterhouse at the bottom to the **roof cisterns** at the top, then falls through **break tanks**,
one per band, which stop the pressure building up into something that would burst the pipes at the bottom. The Crown is
fed from the roof cisterns directly; then the Terraces Header (floor 89), the Middle Header (floor 59), the Mills Header
(floor 29), the Sump standpipes.

The tanks fill **from the top down.** That is a matter of engineering: the risers deliver to the roof, and water reaches
the lower tanks by overflowing through the ones above. It means that when the Waterhouse's output drops, the shortage does
not spread evenly. The Crown's cisterns stay full; the Terraces barely notice; the Middle is told to "use water
thoughtfully"; the Mills Header runs dry for hours a day; the Sump's standpipes stop.

Nobody had to decide that the bottom goes dry first. The building decides it, every day, and the Company has never seen
a reason to fit the valves that would change it. (There are founding-era bypass valves on every header that could
deliver to the lower tanks first. They are welded shut. The weld dates to Y58.)

**In play:** the tap is not always on. Each band has **tap hours**, set by the fill order and the day's output:
- Crown and Terraces: always.
- Middle: always, at reduced pressure 13:00–17:00.
- Mills: **06:00–09:00 and 18:00–21:00** on a normal day (two windows; the game's day runs 08:00–20:00, so the morning
  window gives one hour, the evening window two). On a Tap Drought day, one window, or none.
- Sump: standpipes, 07:00–08:00, queue.
Outside the tap hours the tap gives nothing, so anything that draws water every hour (grow beds, insect bins) needs a
**storage vessel** filled in tap hours. Storage costs squares. Squares cost rent.

### Where the water goes

The Company publishes allocations quarterly (the **Supply Statement**, posted in the tap halls). The Archivists keep the
back issues. A typical recent quarter, in round figures:

| Band | Share of published output | Per person, per day (Archivists' estimate) |
|---|---|---|
| The Crown | 36% | 400 to 500 L (baths, pools, gardens, the Skywater bottling hall's rinse lines, "landscape features") |
| The Terraces | 27% | 120 to 160 L |
| The Middle | 21% | 35 to 50 L |
| The Mills | 11% | 8 to 12 L (tap); more from everything else in this file |
| The Sump | 2% | 3 to 5 L (standpipes) |
| "System losses" | 3% | — |

These figures exclude Process Line 3, which the Supply Statement does not mention.

### Down: drains and the Outfall

Everything that goes down the drains of the Stack goes to the **Outfall**, a founding-era culvert below the Sump that
opens into the bay on the south side. The Sump lives on top of it. Things that go down the drains above (rings, cutlery,
cell casings, pills, a surprising number of small expensive objects from the Crown) end in the Outfall's grates. **Grate
pickers** in the Sump comb them every morning. The water itself goes out to the bay, feeds the Red in summer, and comes
back in through the Intake. There is a joke about this: "In the Stack, every drop gets a second chance."

## 3. The Water Licence

### The Ordinance

The Water Licence comes from the **Public Wellbeing (Water) Ordinance, Y58**, issued by the Halden Company the year
after the Drowning, "in response to the unprecedented public health emergency arising from the storm and from the
contamination of Calder's groundwater by unregulated private wells". (The groundwater was contaminated by the Works. The
"unregulated private wells" were the residents' own, dug in the decade before the Drowning when the city's mains turned
brackish. See file 01.)

The Ordinance is posted in every tap hall, in a frame, behind glass that is cleaned more often than the taps. Its
operative parts, as people in the Mills know them by heart, more or less:

> **Section 2. Prohibition.** No person shall, within the Halden Envelope or its curtilage, treat, purify, distil,
> filter, condition, recover or otherwise render water fit or apparently fit for human consumption, or offer, sell,
> barter, gift or distribute such water, save under and in accordance with a Water Licence issued by the Company.
>
> **Section 3. Purpose.** This Ordinance is made for public wellbeing. Untested water endangers the residents who drink
> it and the reputation of the water on which their health depends.
>
> **Section 4. Definitions.** "Water" includes any beverage of which the principal constituent is water and which has an
> alcoholic strength not exceeding two per centum by volume. "Halden Envelope" means the structure of the Stack, its
> facades, roofs, gutters, shafts, fixtures and the air within them. "Recover" includes condensation from the air of the
> Envelope by any device. "Gift" includes distribution without charge in any quantity exceeding one quarter-litre per
> recipient per day.
>
> **Section 9. Atmospheric moisture.** Moisture held in the air of the Envelope is maintained by the Company's climate
> systems at Company expense and is a Company resource. Recovery of such moisture by private devices is permitted only on
> registration of the device and payment of the Atmospheric Recovery Levy.
>
> **Section 11. Precipitation.** Precipitation falling upon the Envelope is collected by Company infrastructure and is a
> Company resource from the moment of contact.
>
> **Section 14. Wellbeing Minimum.** No residential tenant in good standing or in arrears shall be supplied with less than
> three litres of potable water per registered occupant per day, save during a declared Supply Event.

Everyone has a favourite clause. The Rain Church's is Section 11 ("from the moment of contact": they preach that the
rain is innocent until it touches Halden). The kvass brewers' is Section 4's two per centum. The Mills Co-op's is Section 14,
for the last five words.

**Hook:** the word "curtilage" in Section 2 has never been tested. If the Flats are the Stack's curtilage, the Tide Folk's
rain tarps are illegal; if they are not, the Flats are outside the Ordinance entirely and anyone could set up a licence-free
water works there at low tide. Both the Company and the Tide Folk prefer not to ask the Arbiter. (File 07 has the Flats.)

### Classes and fees

The licence comes in three classes. The fees are set by the Company's **Water Wellbeing Office** (WWO, floor 46), which
issues licences, sets fees, employs the water inspectors and hears nothing.

| | Class P: Producer | Class D: Provider | Class H: Household Device |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who | Bottlers and bulk works (Skywater, a handful of Middle bottlers) | A stall, a shop, a tank that sells by the litre or cup | One device treating water for one registered household's own use |
| Volume | Unlimited | Up to 400 L/day | Up to 4 L/day per registered occupant; **may not sell or gift** |
| Application fee | 12,000 cr | 600 cr | 40 cr |
| Continuing Certification (monthly) | 2,000 cr | 150 cr | 8 cr |
| Device Seal (per device, yearly) | 300 cr | 75 cr | 15 cr |
| Wellbeing Levy | 0.10 cr per litre sold | 0.30 cr per litre sold | n/a |
| Inspection fee (paid by the inspected) | 120 cr/visit | 40 cr/visit, minimum 2 visits a month | 20 cr/visit, at the inspector's discretion |
| Testing | Lab test weekly at WWO, 200 cr | One PS-4 strip per batch, logged | None required; **PS-4 strips may not be held** |
| Display | Brand mark under licence | **Certified Safe Provider** plaque, numbered, renewed yearly (25 cr) | Device Seal tag on the device |

Two things to notice about the table, which everyone in the Mills notices.

First, the Class D provider pays about 7 cr a day before they have sold a litre (Continuing Certification, two inspections
a month, the plaque, the seal), then 0.30 cr on every litre, then the strips. At Mills prices, a provider has to sell about
40 litres a day just to stand still. That is more than one ledge still makes. Most certified providers run two or three
stills or buy unlicensed water to resell under their plaque, which is exactly what the licence exists to stop and exactly
what it encourages.

Second, the Class H household licence cheapens the household's water and costs it its privacy. A registered device is on
the WWO register, which the meter office reads. A household that registers an air-well and then shows a drop in tap
consumption is sent a **Consumption Review** letter, and frequently an inspector.

**In play:** licences are items, not menu entries. A **Water Licence (Class D)** is a card that sits on your counter or
in a frame on your wall (a 1x1 special slot). With it in place, the water you sell can carry the **Certified** mark and
sells to Middle and upper contracts. Without it, you can still sell water, to lower floors and at the counter, at risk. The
plaque must be renewed (an item that arrives by drone from floor 46 each year with a bill). The **Device Seal** is a small
tag item that you attach to a machine by packing it into the machine's seal slot; an unsealed still is a contraband still.

### Inspections

Water inspectors are WWO employees, floor 60-something people working the floors below 30 on a quota. They wear
grey-blue. They carry a PS-4 kit, a sample case of twelve vials, a seal gun and a tablet. The best-known in the Mills is
**Inspector Teo Saxby**, who has done the Mills for nineteen years and who is, everyone agrees, fair, by which they mean
he takes the same bribe from everyone.

How an inspection goes for a Class D provider:
1. The inspector arrives without notice (a certified provider gets a two-hour warning by message; this is a perk of
   certification nobody advertises).
2. He checks the plaque, the device seals, the batch log and the PS-4 strip log.
3. He takes two samples: one from the provider's sale vessel, one "from source", meaning whatever is in the still's pot.
4. He tests one on the spot and takes one to WWO.
5. He charges the inspection fee, on the spot, card or scrip.
6. If the spot test fails: suspension of the licence until a re-test (re-test fee 80 cr), and the batch is seized for
   "safe disposal" (it is poured into a Company tank, and some of the seized water reappears as Halden Pure bottled stock).

How an inspection goes for an unlicensed household: the inspector is let in or isn't. If he isn't, he notes the door and
an HPS team comes back with a warden. If he is, he looks for stills, air-wells, fog-net cord, PS-4 strips, salt-line
hoses that go somewhere other than the toilet, and blue-banded pipe that wasn't installed by Halden.

**In play:** **Inspection Sweep** is an event. A slip arrives in the morning ("Water Wellbeing Office: routine inspection,
floors 15–19, today") and the inspector visits at a random hour. Anything on your field that is contraband (an unsealed
still, a soft-line tap, a sack of PS-4 strips, unlicensed water stock beyond your household allowance) is found unless it
is **inside a closed container** at the moment of the visit. So the sweep is a packing puzzle: fold the still away,
crate the stock, hide the strips. A running still can't go in a crate. Hiding takes squares you would rather use. A
"tip" (a bribe, an item of value placed in the inspector's satchel, a special container that appears during the visit)
makes him miss one thing.

### "Public wellbeing"

The phrase is everywhere: on tap halls, on the plaque, on inspection receipts, on the poster that announces an
unlicensed provider's conviction. The Company's Wellbeing Office (it shares a floor with WWO) runs **Wellbeing Week**
once a year: free cups of Halden Pure at the tap halls, a survey about how residents feel about their water, children's
drawings of happy droplets pinned up on the tap hall walls, and the year's largest sweep against stinkers.

**Wellbeing Information Rewards**: a resident who reports an unlicensed treatment device receives 30 cr or 20 litres of
meter credit, whichever the Company chooses. Certified providers report their unlicensed competition more than anyone
else does. Floor wardens report the rest.

## 4. Stinkers

### Why the name

"Stinker" is the Company's word, from WWO's internal slang for an unlicensed treatment site (an "STK site", a
**suspected treatment kit**), and from the smell. A stinker's still smells of trike when the first cup comes over,
of hot brine and scorched slurry when the pot runs low, of the charcoal they polish with. It gets in their clothes and
hair. In the lifts, people step away from a stinker. In the Mills, nobody does.

The word stuck and the stinkers took it. A stinker who has been doing it twenty years calls themselves "an old stink". A
good still is "sweet-running". "Stinker's honour" is the rule that you never sell water you wouldn't give your own child,
which is broken often enough that people still say it.

### A stinker's kit

The commonest kit in the Mills, sometimes called a **three-pot**:
1. **The settler**: a drum (often a founding-era chemical drum, scrubbed, which is a choice) of salt-line water, left a
   day so the silt drops. The top two-thirds is drawn off; the bottom goes to the drain.
2. **The pot still**: a pressure cooker, a cell-plant reaction vessel, or anything metal with a lid, heated by an electric
   coil on the socket (metered) or a char stove. A copper or steel coil through a bucket of salt-line water as the
   condenser. Founding-era lab glass if you're lucky.
3. **The polisher**: a column (a pipe, a bottle with the bottom cut off) packed with charcoal over sand over cloth.
   Distillate goes through slowly.

Plus a set of rules no one writes down: throw the first cup, leave the last finger, slow fire, change the charcoal every
forty litres, cress-test every new batch of charcoal, never sell the first batch from a new pot.

A three-pot on the socket makes about **2 litres an hour** on a slow run and draws about **1.5 kWh per litre**, which on
the Mills power meter (file 04, file 09) is the real cost of stinker water. Most stinkers run on stolen or shared power;
a still on a legitimate meter is how inspectors find them (the socket's draw spikes in the same pattern every day).

**In play:** the chain on the field:
- **Salt-line outlet** (free brack) → **settling drum** (container, 6 hours: brack becomes **settled brack**, plus a
  **silt** byproduct that blocks the drum if not tipped out)
- → **pot still** (machine: settled brack in; heads, heart and tails out by the hour; power drawn from the socket per hour)
- → **charcoal column** (machine: heart in, **clean water** out at a slower rate; each charcoal fill has a number of litres
  left before it's spent)
- → **test** (any of the tests above)
- → **bottling**: into whatever vessels you have; labelled or not.
Each step is a machine or container, each takes squares, each is a choice about time and risk. The finished water is
**Clean** grade (see Grades): chemically as good as Halden Pure, legally worthless above floor 30 without a licence.

### From stinker to certified safe provider

The story everyone in floors 12–15 knows is **Ilka Stroud's**.

Ilka ran a three-pot on floor 13 for eleven years, in a back room behind a sublet baron's hot-bunk rows. She sold by the
cup at a hatch in the wall. She was a careful stinker: a nose (her brother, Abe) checked every first cup, she cress-tested
every batch, she threw out more than she sold. Nobody got sweet-sick from Ilka Stroud's water.

In Y83 her floor got a new warden and her hatch got a WWO letter. The letter was not a summons. It was an invitation,
printed on cream card with the Halden mark:

> *Dear Resident,*
>
> *The Water Wellbeing Office has become aware that you may be providing water to your neighbours. We recognise the
> important role that community providers play in the wellbeing of the Mills. We would like to invite you to join the
> Certified Safe Provider Programme.*
>
> *Certification offers peace of mind to your customers, protection for your livelihood, and access to Halden-quality
> testing supplies.*
>
> *Should you prefer not to join the Programme, we will be obliged to treat your activity as unlicensed treatment under
> Section 2 of the Public Wellbeing (Water) Ordinance, Y58.*
>
> *We look forward to welcoming you.*

The path she took, which is the standard path:
1. **Application** (600 cr). She didn't have it. The letter came with a flyer for the **Provider Start-Up Loan** from
   Halden Credit, at 2% a week.
2. **Pre-certification inspection** (40 cr). Saxby came. He looked at the same three-pot he could have arrested her for
   the week before. He told her to replace the drum with a Halden-approved vessel (the **WWO-approved Treatment Drum**,
   180 cr, from the company store; identical to the old one but white).
3. **Device Seals** (75 cr each × 2: the still and the column). Saxby fired brass seal-tags onto both with his gun.
4. **First lab test** at WWO (included in the application, but the sample had to be delivered to floor 46, which meant a
   licensed-lane drone: 30 cr).
5. **Plaque** (25 cr): *Certified Safe Provider No. D-0412*. She screwed it next to the hatch.

Same still. Same brother's nose. Same cup through the same hatch. Her water was now "certified safe" and cost 1.20 cr a
cup instead of 0.50, because the certification, the levy and the loan had to be paid. Her customers kept coming for two
weeks. Then most of them went to **Carrow Lane** (a stinker on floor 14) instead, until Carrow Lane's still was reported
under the Wellbeing Information Reward. Nobody knows who reported it. Everyone on floor 13 is sure they know.

Ilka is still certified. She now buys stinker water from two families on floor 11 at 0.30 cr a litre, runs it through her
own column, tests it with her legal strips, and sells it under her plaque. She is, by the Ordinance's own standards, the
safest water on her floor. She has not slept well since Y83.

**Alternative:** Ilka refused. She let the letter sit, moved the three-pot twice, and was caught on the third move. Seizure,
a 900 cr fine, and the still was **voided**: Saxby's team cut the coil in three places and stamped the pot **VOID** in
red before it went down the chute. A VOID-stamped still from the chute is now a known item in the Sump; the Voided weld
them back. (See file 06 on the Voided, file 10 on voided goods.)

**Hook:** D-0412 is not the first number on floor 13. D-0002 was issued in Y58, to a provider whose name is redacted in
the WWO register the Archivists hold a copy of. The address is the room Ilka runs from. Abe swears the still is older than
he is.

**In play:** **certification is a choice offered by letter**. After your still has run for a while, a cream-card slip
arrives in the morning: join or don't. Joining costs the fees above (or a loan, which lowers your credit rating's headroom),
gives you the Licence item, the plaque and the seals, unlocks Certified-grade water contracts to the Middle and Terraces,
and adds inspections and the levy. Refusing keeps the old life with a rising chance of a sweep. There is no right answer;
both are styles.

**In play:** certified providers are also **clients**. A provider who can't keep up with her own sales buys Clean water
from you in bulk, unlabelled, at a low price, no questions. The contract slip is hand-written on the back of a WWO
inspection receipt.

### The informant economy

Water is the one trade where your competition can have you arrested for doing exactly what they do. The result is a
particular kind of floor politics:
- Certified providers report stinkers (Wellbeing Information Reward plus less competition).
- Stinkers keep **dirt files** on certified providers: proof that the provider resells unlicensed water. A dirt file is a
  deterrent, not a weapon; using it ends both of you.
- Floor wardens report everybody and are paid by the report.
- Sublet barons protect the stinkers in their buildings for a cut (a still behind a hot-bunk wall is invisible from the
  corridor) and report them the day they stop paying.
- The Mills Co-op runs its own shared stills and denies it.

**In play:** a **dirt file** is an item (a folded bundle of receipts, 1x2) that a stinker client might give you as
payment, or that you might assemble yourself by buying from a provider and testing what you buy. Holding one against a
certified rival on your floor stops them reporting you. Selling one to HPS ends their licence and pays well once.

## 5. The Company's water

### Halden Pure

Halden Pure is the water from the Waterhouse, sold three ways:

1. **By the tap**: the sweet line, metered by the litre (see below). Most of it.
2. **Bottled**: from the bottling line on floor 8, next to the Waterhouse. **Halden Pure 1 L** (clear plastic, blue cap,
   0.50 cr deposit on the bottle), **Halden Pure Family Cask** (10 L, white jerrycan with a tap, 3 cr deposit),
   **Halden Pure Conditioned** (1 L, "with added minerals for taste and wellbeing", which are a pinch of salts and a 40%
   premium). Sold at the company store for credits or scrip.
3. **By ration**: the Foundation's "Clean Start" water vouchers, which buy Halden Pure at the company store at the full
   price and are given to people the Foundation has assessed. (File 06 for the Foundation.)

Halden Pure is, chemically, very good. It is also the reference against which everything else is judged. "As good as
Pure" is the highest praise a stinker can earn and the phrase most likely to bring an inspector.

The bottle deposits are a small economy: **bottle runners** (often vent kids) collect empties from the chute, the
corridors and the bins of the Middle and return them for the deposit. The company store only takes bottles with intact caps
and seals, so empties with the cap lost are worth nothing to the Company and something to everyone else, as vessels.

**In play:** Halden Pure bottles and casks are vessels. A full one carries the **Pure** grade and a batch stamp. An empty
one is a vessel that refunds a deposit if returned with its cap (a small contract to the company store), or can be refilled
with your own water, which is legal for your own use and **counterfeiting** if sold with the Halden label on it.

### Skywater

Skywater is rain. That is the whole product, and it is the most expensive water in Calder.

The Crown's roof (and the roofs of its terraced gardens, which step down the top floors like a wedding cake) is a single
collection surface of about two hectares, drained by founding-era gutters into the **roof cisterns** and, separately, into
the **Skywater Hall** on floor 88, where it is filtered, UV-treated, bottled in heavy green glass and sold under licence by
**Varne & Daughters**, a Crown family that has held the Skywater licence since the Drowning. Old Senna Varne is said to
drink nothing else and to be a hundred and four.

What makes Skywater precious is not purity. Chemically, Halden Pure is cleaner; rain over Calder Bay carries salt spray,
soot from the Flats' fires and whatever the gulls leave on the roof. What makes it precious is that it is **real**: it fell
from the sky, it was never in the bay, no machine made it. The Crown pays for the story. In grade terms (see section 7),
Skywater is the one water that carries the **Real** grade.

Skywater products:
- **Skywater Still** (750 ml, green glass, wax-sealed): 38 cr in the Terraces, 60 cr in a Crown restaurant.
- **Skywater First Rain** (the first collection of autumn, labelled with the date): sold by subscription only; Crown
  families give it at weddings.
- **Skywater Ice** (cubes frozen from rain, served in the Crown's bars): a party staple. See Ice below.
- **Skywater for Gardens**: Crown residents' own gardens get roof cistern water free; Terraces gardeners buy Skywater by
  the cask for orchids and seedlings because "tap water is so hard on them".

Before an empty Skywater bottle leaves the Crown, it is **voided**: the Skywater Hall's return service collects empties and
**snaps the neck** before they go down the chute, so that nobody can refill them. (Brand protection; see file 05 and 10.)
Necks are snapped clean, so a bottle with a snapped neck can be fitted with a cork and a wax seal by someone who knows how.
Counterfeit Skywater, rain stolen from the Crown's gutters in a re-necked Skywater bottle, is sold in the Middle to
people who want to give something nice. Very occasionally it **is** rain from the Crown roof. Usually it's Clean water from
a Mills still with a pinch of sea salt for "taste of the sky".

**In play:** **Skywater bottle (voided)** is a chute item: a green glass bottle with a snapped neck, 1x2. Repaired with a
cork and wax (a small repair job), it becomes a vessel. Filled with rain (stolen, or caught in a Rain Day event) and sealed,
it can pass as Skywater to a Middle buyer; filled with anything else, it passes unless the buyer tests it. Counterfeit is a
risky, well-paid line; a brand protection agent may come along with a contract on the board that is a sting.

### The metered tap

Every lease below the Terraces has a metered sweet-line tap. Most are the water heads of founding-era **Sorrel meters**, which the Mills call "Sevens" after the Model 7 stamp on the brass:
founding-era meters with a brass body, a glass dial and a magnetic coupling between the water wheel inside and the counter
outside. They were built to last a century and mostly have. They cannot be made anymore. When one fails, the Company fits a
**Model 12** (a cheap Vantage-made smart meter with a screen and a radio), which reads accurately, reports hourly, and
cannot be fooled with a magnet. People in the Mills do not want their Seven to fail.

How the tap bills:
- **Tariff by band.** The price per litre rises as you go down the tower (section 8). The Company's explanation, in the
  Supply Statement: "Smaller accounts carry proportionally higher service, metering and collection costs." The Archivists'
  explanation: the Crown's water is included in their service charge, and the service charge is negotiated.
- **Prepay or post-pay.** Tenants with a credit rating above a threshold are billed monthly after reading. Tenants below it
  are fitted with a **prepay meter** (a Model 12 with a credit slot): you buy meter credit at the company store in advance,
  with a **convenience fee** of 8% on every top-up. Prepay water is dearer water, for the people with the least money.
- **Reading day.** Sevens are read by a meter reader once a month. If the dial can't be read (fogged glass, a crate in
  the way, "nobody home"), the bill is an **estimated read**: the Company's estimate of your consumption, based on "comparable
  premises", which always turns out to be more than you used. Estimated reads are corrected on the next actual reading,
  and the correction is refunded as meter credit, not credits.
- **Standing charge.** 0.6 cr a day for the privilege of having a tap, whether it runs or not.

**In play:** the tap is a machine on your field, 1x1, fixed (you cannot move or sell it; it belongs to Halden). Put a
vessel under it in tap hours and it fills at the tap's flow rate (a Seven on a normal day in the Mills: about 6 litres an
hour). Every litre goes on the bill, shown as a running number on the meter's dial. The bill is due with the rent. Your
credit rating sets prepay or post-pay; prepay means you load the tap with credit (a coin-slot) before it runs.

**In play: the slow stone.** A Seven can be made to under-read by strapping a strong magnet to the counter housing, which
drags the coupling. A **slow stone** (a founding-era magnet from a dead motor, wrapped in tape) makes the Seven read about
80% of what flows. On reading day there is a chance the reader notices (a scuffed housing, a dial that stutters); the
penalty is an Interference charge and your Seven is replaced with a Model 12, forever. A **meter-friendly** variant: the
Old Hands can fit a slow stone inside the housing so it leaves no marks. They charge for it in a way that means they'll
come to you for a favour later.

### Arrears: the button and the red tag

Late rent is late water. The Company's arrears procedure, from the tenancy handbook, with what it means:

1. **Day 1 late**: an arrears notice slip. Credit rating falls.
2. **Day 1 late** (with the notice): a fitter visits and installs a **Flow Restrictor** in the tap. This is a brass disc with a pinhole,
   called **the button**. It limits the tap to the **Wellbeing Flow**: exactly enough to deliver the Section 14 minimum (3
   litres per registered occupant per day) during tap hours, and no more. Being "on the button" or "on the trickle" is
   how people in the Mills say they are behind on rent.
3. **Day 8 late** (second rent day, the lockout). A **red tag** goes on the valve: a plastic seal through the handle, numbered. The
   Wellbeing Minimum is now delivered as **Halden Pure 1 L bottles**, three per occupant per day, collected at the tap hall
   in person, logged, charged to the arrears at bottled prices plus the deposit.
4. **Reconnection**: arrears plus a reconnection fee (80 cr) plus the fitter's call-out (30 cr). The button is removed. The
   button is kept on file.

Breaking a red tag is a crime with its own name, **tag-breaking**. Red tags are numbered, so a broken tag can't be
replaced by one bought from a market stall. Unless it can. **Tag-makers** in the Sump print red tags with matching
numbers if you bring them the stub.

**In play:** the button is an item the Company puts into your tap's modifier slot. Your tap fills at a trickle rate (a few
hundred ml an hour) until the button is removed. Grow beds can't live on the button; insect bins barely can. The red tag
blocks the tap entirely. These are the most direct ways the game's rent pressure reaches the production chains: miss rent,
and the hourly water draw of every machine on your field becomes your problem.

## 6. Every other way to get a drink

The tap gives the Mills about ten litres a person a day, when it runs. People need three to drink, more to cook, more to
wash, and the grow beds and insect bins that feed the tower need far more. The rest comes from the methods below. For each:
how it physically works, what it removes and what it doesn't, where it stands in law, and how it surfaces in play.

### Air-wells

**How it works.** An air-well is a dehumidifier with ambitions: a fan pulls air over a cold coil, water condenses, drips
into a tray. The Mills' air is wet (the bay is a few floors down, the walls sweat, the insect farms breathe, the
hot-bunk rooms hold three shifts of sleepers), so a decent unit pulls **8 to 15 litres a day** in the Mills, more in the
Sump, much less in the Terraces, where the air is conditioned dry by the Company's climate plant.

**What it removes.** Condensate starts nearly pure. But it falls through the unit, which grows mould and slime in its
tray within days, and picks up whatever is in the air: old factory dust, insect-farm dander, cooking oil. It needs a
**charcoal column** after it and a **wipe-down** every few days, or it becomes "mould tea". Air-well water is the
cleanest workaround water in the Mills when the unit is kept.

**Law.** Section 9: air-wells must be registered (Class H) and pay the **Atmospheric Recovery Levy** (0.15 cr per litre,
estimated from the unit's rated capacity, not its actual output). Unregistered air-wells are unlicensed treatment.
Registered ones trigger Consumption Reviews.

**Kinds you might buy** (machines are bought, each different):
- **Halden Breathe-2**: the company store's model. Registered on sale (the seal is fitted at the counter). 8 L/day.
  Draws a lot of power. Reliable. Its tray is shaped so you can't fit a hose to it: "drip tray must be emptied by hand
  for wellbeing reasons."
- **Drybox**: a refurbished office dehumidifier from the Terraces, rescued from the chute (voided with a cut cord,
  respliced). 10 L/day, noisy, unregistered, no seal slot (it was never meant to make drinking water). Cheap from the Voided.
- **Atmos Mk I**: founding-era, the Works' own clean-room humidity unit. 30 L/day for the power of a Breathe-2. Heavy (2x3),
  rare, and every Old Hand who sees one wants to know where you got it. One of the few founding-era machines that makes
  something people need every day, which is why the Company buys any it finds and scraps them.

**In play:** an air-well is a machine with a power draw per hour and an output tray (a vessel slot). Each hour it adds
water to the vessel (more in wet weather events, less in the dry season). Every few days its **tray fouls** (a status on
the machine): output turns from **Clean** to **Grey** until you spend an hour cleaning it (it's off for that hour). The
Atmospheric Recovery Levy, if registered, goes on the bill as a fixed daily charge. The air-well is the canonical
"pay the tower or spend space" trade from `IDEAS.md`: the tap costs credits per litre; the air-well costs squares, power
and upkeep.

**Hook:** the tower's own climate plant dehumidifies the Mills air through the founding-era vents, and the condensate is
supposed to flow down to the Waterhouse for recovery. The vent kids know the **drip lines**: places in the crawlspaces
where the climate plant's condensate pipes have rusted through and leak, steadily, clean-ish water. Every drip line has an
owner. Some have had the same family owner for twenty years.

### Fog nets

**How it works.** In autumn and early spring, Calder fog rolls in off the bay at dawn and wraps the tower up to about the
fortieth floor. Fine mesh stretched across a window or a ledge catches fog droplets, which bead, run down and drip into
a gutter at the bottom. A good net on a Mills ledge gives **3 to 10 litres a morning** in fog season, nothing at all in
summer.

**What it removes.** Nothing: it *collects* the bay's skin. Fog is born at the surface, so it carries salt (a little),
the Slick (some) and pyrol film (some). Fog water needs **distilling or a long charcoal run**; straight fog water is
"sweet-sick in a cup" in a bad week. During the Red, fog water is toxic.

**Law.** Fog nets hang on the **facade**, which is part of the Envelope, so they are an "unauthorised facade fixture" and
a Section 11-adjacent theft at once (the Company counts fog as precipitation when convenient). Facade wardens with long
poles cut nets on sight in the Middle. In the Mills they cut the ones on the main facade and ignore the courtyard sides.

**Who makes them.** Net-weavers in the Mills, mostly older women who worked the Works' filter-mesh lines, knot fine nylon
from chute-salvaged netting and stockings. **Marrowby mesh** (after a weaver family on floor 19) is the most prized: a
double knot that holds beads better and doesn't sag.

**In play:** a fog net needs a **window square** on your lease (if your lease has an exterior wall: a property of the
lease, and a reason to rent the dearer outside-wall squares). It produces only in the **Fog Season** event and on fog
mornings (an hour-window at 06:00–09:00, so in game terms the first hour of the day). Its output is **Brack (fog)**,
gentler than salt-line brack (less salt and metal, still solvent): it skips the settler and goes straight to the still,
and the still yields more Clean per hour from it. Nets wear out (a durability counter); a facade warden event can cut one.

### Ledge stills

**How it works.** A solar still: a shallow black tray of dirty water under a sloped pane of glass or clear plastic. Sun
heats the tray, water evaporates, condenses on the underside of the pane, runs down to a channel at the low edge, and
drips into a bottle. No power, no fire, no smell. Slow: **1 to 4 litres a day per square metre** in good sun.

**What it removes.** Salt and metals stay in the tray (the tray crusts and needs scraping). Trike comes over, more slowly
than in a pot still, so the first hour's drip is still the worst. Pyrol mostly stays. A ledge still's water is decent if
its tray is kept and the morning drip is thrown.

**Where it works.** Only on sunny ledges. The Stack's south face gets sun from late morning to evening at Mills height; the
north face never. Mills ledges on the south face rent at a premium for this alone. Ledge stills are the Mills' most visible
water source: on a sunny day, the south face below floor 30 glitters with hundreds of tilted panes, "the Mills' teeth".

**Law.** A ledge still is a treatment device (it distils) and a facade fixture (it's on the ledge). Doubly illegal,
universally ignored below floor 25, because there are too many to cut and the inspectors would have to lean out of
windows.

**In play:** a ledge still is a cheap machine (bought from a glazier, or a chute pane and a tray) that needs a window square
and produces only in **sunny hours** (weather is a daily roll; a clear day gives the afternoon hours). Output is low but
power-free. It is the beginner's still: put salt-line brack in, get a trickle of Clean out, and learn to throw the first
hour's drip.

### Pot stills on the salt line

Covered above under stinkers: a still fed from the salt line. The difference from a ledge still is power and smell. A pot
still is fast and visible; a ledge still is slow and invisible. Many Mills households run both: ledge in summer, pot in
winter.

### Rain theft from the Crown's gutters

**How it works.** It rains on Calder about one day in three in winter, one in eight in summer. Rain falls on the Crown's
two hectares of roof and terraces and runs into gutters, then into **downpipes** that drop through four internal shafts to
the roof cisterns and the Skywater Hall. The downpipes are founding-era: cast pipe the width of a person's thigh, bolted in
sections, accessible from service panels on most floors of the Terraces and some of the Middle (they were meant to be
inspected).

The Rain Church's method is **spiling**: drill a small hole in a downpipe through a service panel, drive in a hollow
**spile** (a tapered tube, wood or brass, with a stopcock), and on rain days open it into a bucket. A spile in a downpipe
during a Calder downpour gives **a litre a minute** for as long as the rain lasts. Close it, plug the outer panel, leave.
Spile holes are tiny and leak a little, so the downpipes of the Stack are crusted with lime where old spiles were driven
and pulled, decades of them, like the trunk of a tapped tree.

Below the Crown, rain also runs down the **outside** of the tower: the facade sheet. Mills gutter-catchers fix angled
**ledge gutters** to catch the sheet. This water is dirtier (it's washed the whole facade, every pigeon and every ledge
still) but it is closer and nobody has to break into a shaft.

**The weepers.** When the roof cisterns are full (after two days of heavy rain), the excess flows to **overflow pipes**
that empty down the north face from about floor 85. They were built to throw the water clear of the building; in Y70 the
Company had them shortened (the spray was "dampening the Terraces' outlook"). Now they "weep" down the facade and the
Middle and Mills balconies below get the water in sheets. On a **weeping day**, every bucket in the north-side Mills is
out on a ledge.

**What it removes.** Rain is the cleanest raw water in Calder: low salt, no metals, a little soot and Slick from the air.
Downpipe rain after the first flush (the first ten minutes wash the roof) is drinkable after a cloth filter, and better
after a charcoal column. Facade rain needs a still.

**Law.** Section 11: rain is a Company resource "from the moment of contact". Spiling is theft of Company property and
damage to Company infrastructure. Catching weeper water off your own balcony is, technically, also theft, but the Company
has never prosecuted it because that would mean admitting the weepers exist.

**In play:** **Rain Day** is a weather event. On a Rain Day:
- a ledge gutter (if fitted on a window square) fills any vessel in its slot each hour;
- a **spiling contract** may appear on the board from the Rain Church (a slip printed on cheap blue paper: "The Cup needs
  carrying. Floor 64 panel 3. Bring two casks."). You send a drone with empty casks and a spile; it comes back with rain
  (Real grade) or, if the shaft check rolls badly, without the casks and with a fine slip;
- weeper water arrives if you have a north-face window square, two days after heavy rain.
Rain is the only **Real** water a Mills player can get. It sells up, to Terraces gardeners, or as counterfeit Skywater, or
to the Rain Church back as a gift (which raises your standing with them).

### Crown grey and the soft line

**How it works.** The soft line (section 2) carries the Crown's bath and basin water down its own green-banded stack. It is
water that was Halden Pure an hour ago, with soap, skin oil, perfume, hair and the occasional earring in it. Tapping the
soft line means finding where the stack runs past your lease (it passes through the Mills in two places, behind the
walls of floors 11–28 on the east side), drilling it, and fitting a valve. **Soft tappers** do this for a fee; the Old Hands
know where the stack runs because they installed half of it.

**What it removes.** Nothing; it's grey water. But it's *clean* grey water: no salt, no metals, no solvent. Settled and run
through sand and charcoal it is good washing water; distilled it is excellent drinking water. A still fed with Crown grey
makes twice the Clean per hour of a still fed with brack and never needs the first cup thrown. Every stinker wants a soft
tap.

**Law.** Theft of Company water and damage to infrastructure. Soft taps are found when the stack's flow sensor (founding
era, still working on some sections) notes a loss; the Company sends a fitter with HPS.

**Culture.** Crown grey has a rumour economy. People claim to know whose bath it is ("this is the Varne bath, smell the
lilies"). A soft tap below a Crown floor known for parties gets champagne in it on a good night and, once, a famous
diamond ring that the Sump grate pickers would have had if a soft tapper on floor 19 hadn't caught it in her strainer.

**In play:** a **soft tap** is an illegal machine on your field. It gives **Grey (soft)** water free, a few litres an hour,
around the clock, with a random **catch** item in its strainer slot now and then (an earring, a bottle cap, a pill, a
hairpin, rarely something worth a week's rent). Each day carries a small chance of discovery by flow sensor; the chance
rises with how much you draw. Soft grey is the best still feedstock and the best grow-bed water (no salt). It also fits
the IDEAS principle that the bottom lives off the top's waste: the Crown's bathwater as a production input.

### Recycled body water

**How it works.** The human body is mostly water and loses about two litres a day of it as urine and more as sweat and
breath. A **reclaimer** is a still for urine: collect, settle, distil (urine distils well; the ammonia is the main
problem, and a vinegar trap fixes it), polish with charcoal. Founding-era plant clinics had vapour-compression reclaimers
(for workers in the clean rooms, who couldn't leave for the toilet); a few survive.

Breath and sweat are recovered by the **hot-bunk houses**: a room of thirty sleepers on a shift exhales a litre an hour into
a closed room. Hot-bunk barons fit air-wells in every sleeping room and sell the water back to the sleepers in the morning
at the hatch. Sleepers in hot-bunk houses say they "buy their own breath".

**The yellow trade.** Urine is also the Mills' best fertiliser (nitrogen) for the grow beds and an ingredient in old
tanning and dyeing. **Yellow runners** collect sealed urine pails from households each morning and sell them to grow
co-ops and reclaimers. A household on the yellow route gets a few credits a week or a litre-chit for its pail.

**Law.** Reclaiming water from urine is "recovery" (Section 4) and needs a licence. Collecting urine is legal. Selling
urine is legal. Selling the water distilled from it is not. The fertiliser is fine.

**In play:** **urine pail** is a household byproduct item: a sealed 1x1 pail that accrues on your field each morning if
you keep a household (or comes in by contract from neighbours). It feeds two chains: (1) the grow bed's **fertiliser
slot** (faster growth), or (2) a **reclaimer** machine (Clean water out, slowly, with **ammonia** byproduct that goes back
to the grow bed). Choosing which is a real trade-off: water now or food later. Hot-bunk houses are clients: they want
air-well servicing and buy reclaimers second-hand.

**Hook:** the Company's **Remains Recovery** service, contracted to process the bodies of residents who die without a
paid funeral plan, lists among its outputs "recovered process water, returned to the tower system". The Archivists have
the contract. The Supply Statement does not mention it. Neither does anyone at a funeral.

### Ferments and small beer

**How it works.** Before anyone in Calder had heard of germs, people drank small beer because brewing made water safe:
boiling the mash kills germs, the yeast's alcohol and acid stop new ones. It doesn't remove salt or metals, so in the Stack
the water going in must already be decent (tap water, or Clean, or Crown grey). What brewing does is make **any doubtful
decent water** into a safe drink that keeps for a week instead of a day, and it **changes the law** that applies.

The Mills' main ferment is **crust**: a kvass made from stale bread crusts from the garbage chute (the Crown and Terraces
throw away a lot of bread; the bread arrives in the Mills stale, stamped or broken, but bread). Crusts are toasted dark,
steeped in hot water a day, strained, sugared with whatever sweet scraps the chute brings (fruit peel, jam dregs,
candied things), fermented two or three days. It comes out cloudy, brown, slightly sour, fizzy, at about **2%**. Every
household recipe differs. Crust is breakfast for half the Mills.

Other ferments:
- **Peel water**: fruit peels from the Crown (chute), water and sugar; a light sour wine-like drink at 1–3%.
- **Mill ale**: real small beer from grain, rare because grain is a grown crop and dear. Co-op ale for celebrations.
- **Sour**: a vinegar-sharp drink from a mother culture (a jelly disc passed between households for generations; to be
  given a mother is a sign of trust). Below 1%.

**The two-and-a-tenth.** Section 4 defines any drink of 2% alcohol or less as "water" for licensing. A drink of 2.1%
is a beverage, licensed under the **Beverage Licence**, which is cheaper and inspected by the food inspectors instead of
WWO (who are stricter, and have the PS-4 strips). So every crust brewer in the Mills brews to just over two per cent, and
"two-and-a-tenth" means small beer, and by extension anything that is technically legal: "it's a two-and-a-tenth sort of
arrangement." The food inspectors carry a hydrometer. A brew they read at 1.9% is unlicensed water. Brewers add a spoon of
extra sugar to be sure, so Mills crust is slightly stronger, and the Mills slightly drunker, than it would be under any
other law.

**In play:** a **brew crock** is a container-machine: water + crusts (or peel) + sugar in, and over 48–72 hours (it runs
overnight happily: a night process from the day/night idea) it becomes **crust** (or peel water), a drink item in jugs or
bottles. It keeps for days (water keeps one day before going **Stale** in an open vessel). Crust sells down and sideways
(Mills and Sump contracts, hot-bunk houses buy it by the cask), and it is the main way to turn the chute's bread into
something worth more. The crock has a **strength** reading: under 2% it's unlicensed water, over 2% it's a beverage
(needs the cheaper licence for upper-floor sales). The sugar slot decides which.

### Ice from above

**How it works.** Two kinds.
- **Party ice.** The Crown and the Terraces use ice extravagantly: bars, buffets, ice sculptures, cold-chain deliveries,
  bathtubs of ice for the Pleasure Pier's bottles. Leftover ice goes down the chute in bags, and ice sculptures go down
  whole after parties, sometimes still swan-shaped. By the time it reaches the Mills it is half melted. Party ice is often
  Skywater Ice: **Real** water.
- **Ice fall.** In the coldest weeks of winter, the upper floors (300 metres up, in the wind) grow icicles from the
  weepers, gutters and balcony rails. When the sun comes out, they fall. The Company's **Falling Object Advisory** keeps the
  Middle's balconies closed. People in the Mills, further down and further out from the base of the tower, stretch
  **ice nets** from ledges and catch what falls, and nobody wants to be on a ledge doing it.

**What it removes.** Ice is frozen water; it's as good as what froze. Party ice is mostly Pure or Skywater. Icicles are
rain, with a little facade grime.

**Law.** Chute contents are "waste transferred to the tenant" and legal to keep. Ice fall is precipitation: Section 11,
technically. Nobody has ever been fined for catching ice, because the Company does not want an official record that ice
falls on people.

**In play:** **ice** is a chute item that **melts** over hours: a 2x2 bag of ice becomes a 2x2 puddle-in-a-bag (a vessel of
water) in a few hours, and if it's not in a vessel or a cold container by then, it's lost as a spill. A time-pressure
item: sort it out of the chute first thing, put it in a vessel. An **ice sculpture** is a big odd-shaped chute item
(a 3x3 swan) that yields a lot of Real water if you have room to let it melt in a tub. In winter the **Ice Fall** event gives
an ice-catching window: fit an **ice net** to a window square and it drops ice items in at random hours.

### Hydrogel

**How it works.** Hydrogel is a polymer that drinks hundreds of times its weight in water and holds it as a jelly. It comes
into the Mills three ways:
- **Cold packs**: Greenhold produce and Crown food deliveries arrive in insulated boxes with gel cold packs. Used packs go
  down the chute. The gel is water locked in a polymer with a preservative: **not drinkable**. Dried in a low oven or by a
  still, it gives up its water (distillable to Clean) and leaves **gel sand**, the dry polymer crystals, which can be
  re-wetted forever.
- **Vey HydraSachets**: Vey's medical hydration gel, sold in the Middle to workers on long shifts ("Hydration on the go.
  For wellbeing."). Edible, slightly salty, lemon-flavoured. Damaged boxes go down the chute; voided sachets are slit. A
  slit sachet must be eaten that day.
- **Gel sand from the Co-op**: the Mills Co-op buys gel sand by the sack and mixes it into grow beds.

**What it's for.** Two practical jobs:
1. **Grow beds hold water.** A bed mixed with gel sand loses less water to the air and draws less per hour. The plants
   still need water, but the bed can coast through a tap gap.
2. **Water that doesn't slosh.** Water gelled with gel sand packs like a solid: it can't spill, it can be cut, it doesn't
   need a sealed container. A **gel brick** of a few litres is how water travels in a cheap drone without a sealed bay.
   Recovering it means drying it (a still or a char oven), so gelled water costs fuel to get back, but it travels.

**Law.** Hydrogel isn't water. Gel bricks aren't water. A gel brick moving through a licensed lane carrying 4 litres of
unlicensed Clean water is a **"hydration product"**, and the WWO has argued for nine years that it should be covered by
Section 4. The Arbiter hasn't ruled.

**In play:** gel sand is an additive item for grow beds (a slot: reduces the bed's hourly draw). **Gel bricks** are made
in a press or a tray (water + gel sand → solid 1x2 brick, overnight). They pack like solids, so they can go in any drone bay;
liquid water needs a **sealed bay** or sealed vessels. This makes water delivery a real packing choice: bottles and casks
(liquid, need a sealed bay or upright vessels, heavy), or gel bricks (pack anywhere, buyer has to recover the water,
pay less). Cold packs from the chute are a free gel-sand source after drying.

### Charcoal and sand

**How it works.** The polishers of the water trade.
- **Slow sand filter**: a drum of fine washed sand. Water percolates down slowly. Within two weeks of use, a slimy living
  layer grows on top of the sand, called **the skin** by Mills filter-keepers, and that layer is what actually eats the
  germs. A new sand filter is nearly useless; a **ripe** one is very good against germs and silt. Scrape the top finger of
  skin when it clogs, and it ripens again in a few days. Removes: germs, silt, some metals bound to silt. Not salt, not
  dissolved metals, not solvents. It's for rain, grey water and fog, not bay water.
- **Charcoal column**: crushed charcoal in a pipe, water trickles through. Charcoal grabs solvents (trike, pyrol), the
  Slick, bad tastes, chlorine-like smells, and some metals. Not salt. Charcoal fills up and stops working without warning;
  stinkers change it by the litre count.

**Where charcoal comes from.** Wood is the scarcest thing in the Stack after daylight. So:
- **Crate char**: charcoal burned from real-wood crates, pallets and broken furniture thrown away by the Crown and Terraces
  (down the chute, usually voided: smashed, legs sawn off). Char burners in the Sump burn it in sealed drums.
- **Shell char**: from the Flats' mussel and oyster shells and insect chitin from the protein farms. Worse, but plentiful.
- **Spent cartridge char**: founding-era filter cartridges from the Waterhouse (spent, sold off as scrap) contain activated
  carbon of a quality nobody can make. Baked in a char oven, it **reactivates**. Waterhouse scrap is the best charcoal in
  Calder, and a lot of it "falls off" the Waterhouse's scrap barge.
- **Halden FilterPak**: a licensed charcoal cartridge for registered devices, 12 cr, 60 litres. Fits only Halden housings.

**Where sand comes from.** The Flats. Flats folk wash bay sand (a brutal, wet job) and sell it up the tower by the sack.
Washed sand is "**Flats white**"; unwashed is useless and dangerous. (File 07 on the Flats.)

**In play:** sand filter and charcoal column are both cheap machines. The sand filter has a **ripeness** gauge (rises
over days of use; falls when you scrape it): a visible, steerable, slow process, the kind the hour/day timescale suits. The
charcoal column holds a charcoal fill item with a litre counter; when it's spent, swap it. Spent charcoal goes to a char
oven to reactivate (overnight) or to the grow bed as soil conditioner. Different chars are different items with different
litre counts: crate char 30 L, shell char 15 L, cartridge char 120 L.

### Water as wages

Water is money in the Mills, and some employers pay in it directly.
- **Litre-chits**: the Mills Co-op pays part of its workers' shares in paper chits good for one litre at a Co-op tank.
  Chits circulate as small change on Co-op floors; a cup of crust costs "half a chit". Co-op chits are stamped with a
  grow-bed sprout and a floor number. Forged chits exist; the Co-op tank-keepers know every tank-keeper's handwriting.
- **Hydration Allowance**: the Company's term for meter credit loaded directly onto a worker's prepay tap, paid instead
  of part of a wage by Halden-contracted employers (the Waterhouse's own cleaning crews, the chute sorters). Meter credit
  can only be spent at that tap, can't be withdrawn as credits, and expires at the end of the lease.
- **The wet shift**: in the Sump, runners and grate pickers are paid at the end of the day in water from the gang's tank.
  A "wet shift" is a day paid in water; a "dry shift" is one paid in nothing, which happens.
- **The cup with the bunk**: hot-bunk houses include a quarter-litre cup with every shift's bunk (Section 4's gift limit,
  exactly).

**In play:** some contracts **pay in water**: litre-chits (a currency item spendable at Co-op contracts and tanks), meter
credit (applied to your tap's bill automatically), or full casks in the drone's bay on return. A Co-op contract might pay
40 cr, or 15 cr and 30 litre-chits; the chits are worth more if your grow beds are thirsty.

### What removes what

The cheat sheet every Mills water-maker knows, here as a table:

| Method | Salt | Metals | Trike | Pyrol | The Slick | Germs | Silt | Output (Mills, typical) | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Boiling | no | no (concentrates) | partly | no | no | **yes** | no | — | fuel |
| Settling | no | partly (with silt) | no | no | no | no | **yes** | — | a day, a drum |
| Pot still | **yes** | **yes** | yes if first cup thrown | yes if last finger left | mostly | **yes** | **yes** | 2 L/h | power, smell |
| Ledge still | **yes** | **yes** | yes if first drip thrown | mostly | mostly | **yes** | **yes** | 1–4 L/day per m² | a window, sun |
| Sand filter (ripe) | no | some | no | no | no | **yes** | **yes** | 1 L/h | sand, patience |

The rule that comes out of it: **only distillation (or the Waterhouse) beats the bay.** Everything else cleans water that

salt line, though free, keeps the stinkers busy.

## 7. Water grades

Water in the game should carry a grade the way goods carry real/synthetic grades in `IDEAS.md`. The tower already has the
words.

| Grade | Tower word | What it is | Who accepts it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Real** | "sky" | Rain, Skywater, Skywater Ice, icicles. Never touched the bay or a machine | The Crown pays for it. The Terraces want it for gardens and gifts. The Rain Church gives it |
| **Pure** | "blue" (the cap) | Halden Pure, from the tap or bottle, batch-stamped | Everyone. Upper-floor contracts take nothing less, except Real |
| **Certified** | "plated" (from the plaque) | Clean water sold under a Class D licence with a logged PS-4 strip | The Middle. Clinics in the Terraces for cleaning, never for patients |
| **Clean** | "clear" | Treated, tested, safe. Chemically as good as Pure. No licence | The Mills, the Sump, the Flats. Never on paper above floor 30 |
| **Grey** | "grey" / "soft" if Crown grey | Wash water, used water, untested water, fouled air-well water | Washing, grow beds (soft grey only: brack-grey kills them), stills as feed |
| **Brack** | "brack" | Bay water, salt-line water, fog water | Stills, sprinklers, toilets. Nobody drinks it |
| **Stale** | — | Any drinkable water left in an open vessel past a day | Drops one step (Clean → Grey); sealed vessels don't stale |

**In play:** the grade is the item's mark. A contract slip from the Middle reads "20 L Certified or better"; from the
Terraces, "12 L Pure, sealed, batch stamped"; from the Crown, "6 × 750 ml Real, glass"; from the Sump, "40 L, drinkable,
any". Each grade sets the price ceiling. A drink has a grade the way a vegetable is real or synthetic, and the
production chain decides how high a grade you can reach.

### Price swings

## 9. Water, band by band

Short; file 02 has the floors. This is only how each band drinks.

- **The Crown (90–104).** Roof cisterns, gravity-fed, always full. Pools, private baths, a lap pool on 101 that is
  emptied and refilled weekly "for freshness". Gardens on Skywater. Ice everywhere. A Crown household's water is
  invisible: nobody has ever turned a tap and got nothing. The Crown's conversation about water is about taste: "too
  hard", "a little flat this season", "the Varnes' First Rain was exceptional".
- **The Terraces (60–89).** Plentiful Pure, cheap per litre. Private clinics run their own Class P polishing loops. Terraces
  gardeners buy Real for orchids. The Terraces notice water only in Membrane Week, when the pressure drops for a day and the
  building management sends apologies with a bottle of Skywater.
- **The Middle (30–59).** Metered, careful, anxious. Households keep a **water book**: a ledger of daily use against the
  Thoughtful Use threshold. Children are taught to turn the tap off while soaping. Families keep a Family Cask under the
  bed. The Middle is where the counterfeit Skywater sells (gifts that look like the Crown), where certified providers sell
  to people who don't trust the tap's lead, and where reporting a neighbour's air-well is a neighbourly thing to do.
- **The Mills (10–29).** Tap hours, the button, stinkers, ledge stills on the south face, fog nets, soft taps, crust,
  litre-chits, the Co-op tanks. Everyone is a little bit in the water trade. Everyone is a little bit guilty. The Mills
  wash with grey: **wash halls** (Halden Wash Halls, 2 cr for five minutes of warm Pure; the Co-op's "drip rooms", a bucket
  of warm soft grey for 0.50 cr) and the **cloth bath** (a basin, a cloth, a litre).
- **The Sump (4–9).** Standpipes one hour a day; the Waterhouse's leaks, which the gangs own; grate picking at the
  Outfall; the wet shift; drip lines in the crawlspaces. Sump water bosses keep **wet ledgers** (see below). Brack is
  everywhere: the Sump's floors are wet with it. The Sump's air-wells give the most water in the tower because the Sump's air
  is the wettest.
- **The Flats.** No tap, no pipe. Barrel water bought from the Sump's gangs or the Waterhouse's back door at triple Mills
  prices, often paid in salvage. Rain tarps (Section 2's curtilage question). Tide stills: black tarps over pits of bay
  water, sun-stills on the mud. The Flats folk know the bay better than anyone and drink the worst water in Calder. (File 07.)

## 10. Water in culture

### Sayings

- **"Sweet water, short life."** Trike tastes sweet. Don't trust a sweet cup.
- **"First cup for the bay."** Throw the heads. Also: the first try at anything goes wrong; don't sell it.
- **"Leave the last finger."** Don't run anything to the bottom: a still, a debt, a friendship, a favour.
- **"The tower drinks from the top."** Whoever's above you gets it first. Said about water, jobs, news, everything.
- **"Rain doesn't know whose roof it is."** Rain Church; also used by anyone caught with something they shouldn't have.
- **"Two-and-a-tenth."** Technically legal.
- **"On the button."** Behind on rent. **"Red-tagged"**: cut off, out of chances, finished.
- **"Dry"** means broke. **"Wet"** means flush ("she's wet this week, ask her"). **"To tap someone"** is to ask for a loan.
- **"Pure as a strip"**: honest, checked, beyond suspicion (sarcastic, usually).
- **"Plated"**: certified, and by extension anyone who's gone respectable by paying for it. "He's plated now" = he's sold out.
- **"Buy your own breath"**: pay for something you made yourself. Hot-bunk origin.
- **"Every drop gets a second chance"**: things come back around; what you threw away will be sold back to you.
- **"Blue cap, blue gums"**: the tap's water through the old pipes; also, the Company's help makes you sick.
- **"Who's in your pot?"**: whose water are you drinking, i.e. who do you owe, whose side are you on.

### Manners

- **The wet.** A guest in a Mills home is offered a **wet**: a small cup (a thimble or a cap) of whatever the house drinks.
  Accepting a sip is polite; draining it is rude in a poor house and expected in a wealthy one. Refusing is an insult unless
  you say "I'm wet today, thank you", meaning you've had enough, leave it for the house.
- **Never pour back.** Once water has been poured into a cup, it doesn't go back into the vessel. Leftovers go to the
  plants. "Pour it to the plants" is a gentle way to end a conversation.
- **The dregs.** The last of a cup goes to the grow bed, the cress tray or the pot on the windowsill. A household with no
  plant to give its dregs to is a household that's given up.
- **Asking.** You never ask how much water someone has. You ask "dry hours?" (how was your tap today?) and they answer
  "wet enough" (it's fine) or "on the button" (it's not).
- **Lids.** A covered vessel is private property; an uncovered one is a household's for the day. Touching someone's
  covered cask is like opening their mail.
- **First water.** A baby's first drink of plain water is given from the best water the family can find, Real if possible.
  Mills families save a bottle of rain or a melted chunk of party ice for it for months. Godparents bring "first water" as a
  gift.
- **Wedding cup.** A couple drinks from one cup, then pours the dregs into a pot with a seedling. The plant is the
  marriage's luck; when it dies, someone always says something.
- **Funerals.** The Tide Folk pour a cup into the bay "to give back what the body borrowed". The Mills, more practical, give
  away the dead person's stored water to the neighbours who carried the body down.

### The Rain Church and water

(File 06 has the Church as a faction. This is what it does with water.)

The Church's doctrine fits on a tap hall wall: **the Roof was stolen**. Rain is the one thing that falls on everyone, rich
and poor, and the Crown built a lid over the sky and put a price on what comes through it. Every litre caught on the roof is
a litre owed to the people below, who were meant to receive it. The Church has worked out how much, from Calder's rainfall
records (kept by the Sounding, bought every year at a price that strains the collection plate): the roof catches about
**fourteen million litres a year**, which the Church divides among the tower's residents and calls **the Owed**. Its
sermons read out the Owed per person for the year, a litre figure. People who've never been inside a Rain Church know their
Owed.

Practices:
- **Spiling** (see section 6), done by the **Pourers**, the Church's lay clergy, who carry brass spiles on cords
  around their necks.
- **The fonts**: hidden cisterns in old service shafts, filled on rain days, opened to the faithful and anyone else on the
  Church's open days. The Church's water is **given, never sold**: one cup, the **Cup**, per person per visit, a quarter
  litre, which is the exact amount Section 4 allows as a gift without a licence. The Church did not choose a quarter litre.
  The Company did.
- **Rain Days are holy.** On a Rain Day the Church holds **the Opening**: every spile in every downpipe it knows is opened at
  once, every font filled, and the Litany of the Downpipe is chanted in the shafts, where the downpipes roar. ("It falls on
  the just. It falls on the unjust. It falls on the roof. It is taken at the roof. It is returned.")
- **First flush to the Crown**: the first ten minutes of roof rain, which wash the roof, are never spiled. "First cup for
  the Crown." The Crown can have its pigeon dirt.
- **The Cup as currency of trust.** To give someone a cup from a font is to vouch for them. A Pourer who gives you a cup in
  front of others is telling the floor you're under the Church's eye.

**In play:** the Rain Church is a client and a source. Its contracts: carry casks to and from a spile site; deliver
charcoal and sand to a font; carry Real water down to the Sump on a Rain Day (pays in standing, a little credit, and the
Church's blessing, which is a reputation stat with the faithful that makes Mills clients trust your water). It gives you a
free cup on your floor's open day (a small Real-water item). Selling Real water to the Terraces is legal in the Church's
eyes only if a tithe goes back to a font: a choice that touches your reputation, not your bill.

**Hook:** the Church's estimate of the Owed is fourteen million litres. The Skywater Hall's published bottling and the
roof cisterns' published capacity together account for about nine. The Church says the Crown's pools take the rest. The
Old Hands say there is a fifth downpipe on the founding blueprints that nobody has found.

### Sharing rules

Most floors in the Mills run some unwritten version of these:
- **Children and the sick first.** In a tap drought, the Co-op tanks serve children's cups before anyone else's.
- **No hoarding past a week.** A household with more than a week's water stored during a drought is a hoarder; neighbours
  will ask for it, and refusing is remembered for years. (The Company has its own hoarding rule; see crimes.)
- **The block tank.** Many corridors keep a shared tank, filled in turns during tap hours by whoever's tap is running
  (sometimes through a hose from one tap under another's meter, which is billing fraud under Halden rules and good
  neighbourliness under the Mills'). A **tank-keeper** keeps the key and the list.
- **Grey goes down, never sideways.** Grey water passes to the floor below (through buckets, sometimes through deliberate
  holes in floors) for washing and beds. Passing grey sideways, to a neighbour on your own floor, is an insult: it means
  you think they're beneath you.

### Water debts

Water debt is older than credit in the Mills, and it doesn't appear on any credit rating.
- A **wet debt** is owed in litres, not credits. Borrowed in a drought, repaid after. The standard interest is **a litre a
  week on ten**. In a long drought, people borrow water at 10% a week to keep children drinking, and pay it back for a year.
- Water debts **pass to family**. A dead person's wet debts are owed by whoever took their water stock at the funeral.
- **Wet ledgers**: Sump water bosses keep ledgers of who owes them how many litres. The best-known, **Mag Tolley**, who
  owns the biggest Waterhouse leak in the Sump (a cracked founding-era return pipe on floor 6 that her grandfather found),
  has a wet ledger going back thirty years with names of people who have long since moved up to the Middle and still send
  her water once a year at Midwinter because her ledger says so and because she knows where they live.
- **Paying a water debt in credits** is an insult to the lender (it implies they were trading, not helping) unless the
  lender asks.

**In play:** **wet debt** can be a contract type. A neighbour lends you 20 litres in a drought (a cask in your field on
the morning of a Tap Drought); a slip says you owe 22 litres within two weeks. Repay in water (pack the litres in a drone to
their floor). Missing it doesn't hit your credit rating; it hits your standing on the floor, and next drought nobody lends.
Mag Tolley's ledger is a late-game client: she'll lend large amounts of water against odd collateral and collect in favours.

## 11. Water crimes and punishments

The Company prosecutes; the Arbiter decides; HPS enforces (by subscription, so prosecutors of water crimes are prompt and
victims of them are not). File 05 has law in general. Here are the water-specific offences, with what actually happens.

| Offence (Company term) | What it is | Penalty (as charged) | What actually happens |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Unlicensed Treatment** (Section 2) | Running a still, filter, air-well or reclaimer without a licence | Fine 300–1,500 cr; seizure and destruction of devices | Devices voided (coil cut, stamped VOID) and sent down the chute; fine added to rent; credit rating drop; a "certification invitation" six months later |
| **Unlicensed Distribution** | Selling or giving away water without a licence, more than a quarter-litre a day per recipient | Fine 200–800 cr per instance | Mostly used against hatches and the Rain Church's fonts |
| **Atmospheric Diversion** (Section 9) | An unregistered air-well | Fine 150 cr + back-levy for "the period of operation" (estimated) | The estimate always goes back to the start of the lease |
| **Precipitation Theft** (Section 11) | Spiling, ledge gutters, rain catching | Fine 100–500 cr; damage to infrastructure charged at cost | Pourers caught spiling get **remediation labour** (below); ledge gutters get cut down |
| **Facade Interference** | Fog nets, ledge stills, anything on the facade | Removal at tenant's cost (40 cr) | Below floor 25, nearly never enforced |
| **Meter Interference** | Slow stones, bypasses, reversed meters | Interference charge (estimated loss × 3); Model 12 fitted | Model 12 is permanent; credit rating flagged "metering risk", forcing prepay |
| **Tag-breaking** | Breaking a red tag | Fine 500 cr; lease termination at the Company's discretion | The most likely water crime to end in eviction |
| **Soft-line Theft** | Tapping Crown grey | Fine 800 cr; damage at cost; "water taken" estimated and billed at Pure tariff | Crown grey is billed at the Pure price, because it was Pure an hour ago |
| **Counterfeit Brand Water** | Refilled Skywater or Halden bottles sold as genuine | Brand protection penalty: 2,000 cr+ | Brand protection agents prefer to recruit counterfeiters as informants |
| **Hoarding During a Supply Event** | Storing more than 20 L per registered occupant during a declared Supply Event | Confiscation of excess "for redistribution" | Confiscated water is sold back at Supply Event pricing |
| **Wellbeing Endangerment** | Someone gets sick from water you provided | Fine up to 5,000 cr; lease termination; the Arbiter may impose remediation labour | The rare crime the Mills also punishes: a stinker who sickens a child is finished on their floor whatever the Arbiter says |
| **Unauthorised Testing Materials** | Holding PS-4 strips without a licence | Fine 50 cr per strip | Strips are evidence of intent: an unlicensed person with strips is charged with treatment even without a still |

### Remediation labour

The penalty the Mills fear most. A convicted water offender who can't pay is assigned to **remediation labour at the
Waterhouse**: cleaning the membrane trains during Membrane Week and regenerating ion beds with acid and caustic, in the
founding-era halls on floors 5 to 8, at a credited rate of 15 cr a day against the fine. The work is wet, chemical,
twelve-hour shifts, and the protective gear is the Company's, which is to say worn. People come back with burns, bad
lungs, the shakes. They also come back knowing exactly how the Waterhouse works, which membranes are failing, where the
valves are, and which guards look the other way. Half the Sump's best water engineers learned the trade serving a fine.

**Hook:** a remediation crew from Y79 noticed that one membrane train, Train E, is cleaned every Membrane Week but never
put back into the tap supply. Its output valve is labelled PL3.

### Mills justice

The Mills Co-op and the floors keep their own rules alongside the Company's:
- **Selling sweet water** (badly cut, making people sick): your still is broken by your neighbours, publicly. Repeat: your
  floor's tank-keepers refuse you. That's a death sentence in a drought, so people leave.
- **Informing on a stinker**: never proven, always suspected. Informers find their tap's Seven mysteriously jammed with
  a slow stone so their bill drops, then get a Meter Interference charge.
- **Hoarding in a drought**: named at the block tank. The hoarded water is "borrowed" by the floor and the debt
  written in the tank-keeper's book.
- **Stealing from a block tank**: the worst of all. The thief's name goes on the tank's lid in paint.

## 12. Events

Water events for the day's slip, the price board and the contract board. Each with what happens on the field.

- **Tap Drought** (a low-output stretch, common in summer and when membranes foul): the Mills tap hours shrink to one
  window or none for 2–5 days. Stinker and Certified prices rise. Co-op litre-chits gain value. Contracts for water
  delivery to Mills and Sump floors flood the board at good pay. Wet-debt offers appear. The tap hall board posts "SUPPLY
  EVENT IN EFFECT" and bottled Halden Pure goes to ×2.
- **Membrane Week** (quarterly, announced a week ahead): the Waterhouse shuts trains for cleaning. Two days of no tap below
  the Middle; reduced elsewhere. Remediation labour contracts. A chance of Waterhouse scrap (spent cartridge char) appearing
  for sale in the Sump.
- **Boil Notice** (contamination in the sweet line): "Residents are advised to boil tap water before use as a precaution."
  The tap's output drops to **Grey** grade for a day or two; tested, it shows metals, not germs. Contracts that require Pure
  can't be filled from the tap. Stinkers busy.
- **Rain Day**: rain. Ledge gutters fill, spiling contracts, the Opening, Real water floods the Mills market and prices fall.
  Ledge stills produce nothing (no sun). Fog nets nothing.
- **Weeping Day** (two days after heavy rain): the weepers overflow down the north face. Free Real water for north-face window
  squares.
- **Fog Season** (autumn, several weeks): fog mornings; fog nets produce every first hour; Clean prices fall a little; damp
  rises (air-wells produce more; mould fouls their trays faster).
- **Ice Fall** (deep winter, a few days): ice items fall into ice nets; party ice in the chute melts slower (cold); a chance
  of a facade injury in the news.
- **The Red** (summer bloom, a week or two): brack and fog water carry a **toxin** tag that only a still followed by a full
  charcoal run removes; charcoal use doubles; a fog net's output during the Red is worthless.
- **Inspection Sweep**: see section 3. The packing puzzle of hiding your stills.
- **Wellbeing Week** (yearly): free Halden Pure cups at the tap hall (a free 250 ml Pure item each day for a week), a survey
  slip (fill it in for a small scrip reward; your answers go in your file), and a sweep with double the inspectors.
- **Meter Reading Day** (monthly): the Seven is read. Bills arrive. Slow stones roll for detection. If your tap is
  blocked by stacked goods on the reading hour, you get an estimated read.
- **Salt Line Burst**: a salt-line pipe bursts on your floor. A free flood of brack (fill every vessel you have, for stills),
  and any item stored in a **floor-level container** marked open gets **brined** (salt-damaged: metals rust, food spoils,
  cloth stains) unless it's in a sealed container. The event is a reason for sealed crates.
- **Sprinkler Trip**: a fire somewhere on your floor sets off the salt-line sprinklers. Everything on open shelves gets
  brined. Shop furniture with lids protects its contents. The Company bills the floor for "fire suppression water".
- **Dark Floor Night** (hook-driven, recurring): the Mills tap runs weak in the evening window, a third down, every few
  nights, nobody says why. If the player has the Archivists' blueprint, the pattern is visible.
- **Red Tag on the Block**: a neighbour is cut off. They come to your counter asking for water on a wet debt, or to buy your
  tap's hours (run a hose from your tap to their cask, billed to your meter). Yes, no, or a price.
- **Pipe Fever** (rare): a stretch of old sweet-line pipe in the Mills starts leaching lead; the tap's grade drops to Grey on
  floors 15–19 for weeks. The Company announces a "pipe renewal programme" for the Mills, scheduled for Y91.

## 14. A litre's round trip

To see how it all connects, follow one litre.

It starts in the bay below the south face, in the body water, where the Lagoon's metals hang and the pyrol film floats
above. The Intake on floor 3 pulls it in through a screen crusted with mussels. It passes the pre-filters, the membrane
trains (Train B, one of the good ones), the ion beds, the UV. It leaves the Waterhouse as Halden Pure, so clean it would
strip a copper pipe, and climbs the riser to the roof cisterns on 90.

On a Tuesday it goes to a bathtub on floor 97. Someone lies in it for forty minutes with a book and a glass of Skywater.
They pull the plug. The litre, now with lily soap in it and a few hairs, goes down the green-banded soft stack.

Behind a wall on floor 19, a soft tap catches it. The soft tapper, a widow named **Bett Corrin**, sells it by the bucket to
a stinker two doors down, who runs it through a three-pot (no first cup to throw: soft grey has no trike) and a crate-char
column, and tests it with a cress twist. Two days later the cress is green. The litre is Clean.

The stinker sells it, a cup at a time through a hatch, to a woman from floor 17 who runs a little workshop-shop. She
drinks most of it. The last finger she pours, as one does, into the grow bed by her window: a tomato plant, grown from a
seed savers' seed, watered every hour from a cask she fills in tap hours and tops up from the stinker on the button
days.

The tomato ripens. It is **real** produce. She packs it into a drone with eleven others, a contract slip on embossed card,
and launches it up a licensed lane to floor 96, where a Crown cook slices it into a salad for a christening, served with
six bottles of rain in green glass.

The salad is not finished. The leftovers go down the chute.

## 15. Loose threads

More hooks, unattached, to pan through.

- **Hook:** the Atmos Mk I units were all accounted for in the Works' inventory: 300 of them. The Company has scrapped 41
  that it found in the tower since Y60. That leaves 259. The Old Hands say a lot of them went to the Dark Floors.
- **Hook:** Halden Pure tastes slightly different on certain days. Noses say it's the remineralising salts. Mag Tolley says
  it's when the Waterhouse is short and the Company tops up the sweet line from somewhere else.
- **Hook:** Skywater's licence was granted to Varne & Daughters by the Company in Y57, the year of the Drowning, eleven
  weeks **before** the storm, in the First Month. The licence document is in the Archivists' collection. The Varnes say the date is a
  clerical error.
- **Hook:** a Rain Church Pourer has been found dead in a downpipe shaft on floor 68, drowned in a dry shaft. The Church
  says the Crown opened a valve. HPS says it was an accident during an act of Precipitation Theft.
- **Hook:** Greenhold (the agricultural tower) has offered the Mills Co-op a "water partnership": Greenhold's patented
  drought-resistant seed, which needs a third less water, in exchange for exclusive rights to whatever the Co-op's grow
  beds produce. The seed savers say Greenhold's seed doesn't come true from saved seed and must be rebought each year.
- **Hook:** Vantage, the rival tower, is rumoured to make its own membranes. If true, a single Vantage membrane delivered to
  the Old Hands could double the Mills' tap hours. The Company would pay a great deal to make sure nobody tries.
- **Hook:** the Sounding sells rain forecasts. The Rain Church buys them. So, it turns out, does the Skywater Hall, which
  closes its downpipe service panels on the morning of a forecast rain. Someone at the Sounding is selling the Church's
  orders back to the Varnes, or the other way round.
- **Hook:** in Y61, the **Dry Year**, when a third of the membranes failed at once, the Mills had no tap for nine weeks. The
  Mills Co-op, a year old, grew up that year around the first block tanks. Every Co-op tank-keeper's key is a copy of a key cut in
  Y61. Nobody knows where the original lock is.
- **Hook:** a child on floor 16 drinks only rain, has done since birth, and has perfect teeth. People come from other
  floors to look at her teeth. Her mother won't say where the rain comes from.
- **Alternative (on the poison):** the Drowning didn't spread the Lagoon's sludge: the Company spread it afterwards,
  deliberately, by dumping the remaining reservoir into the flooded city at night "to prevent a second collapse", because
  disposing of it properly would have cost more than the Works earned in a year. The divers of the Flats know there's a
  layer of slurry on top of old Calder's streets that's newer than the flood silt underneath. (A variant for file 01 to
  take or leave.)
- **Alternative (on the Licence):** there is no Water Licence as a document at all: just the meters. Water is legal if it
  passed through a Halden meter and illegal if it didn't, and the "licence" is a meter you rent for your still that bills
  you for the water it makes. Simpler, crueller, and a single mechanic (every source you own carries its own meter if
  you're legal).
