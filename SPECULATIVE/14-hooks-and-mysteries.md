# 14. Hooks and mysteries

Speculative. Plain text is the main version; **Alternative:** marks another version of the same thing; **Hook:** marks a
mystery or story seed; **In play:** marks where it surfaces in the game. This file gathers the hooks the other files left
lying around (the yellow under the welds, the nineteen minutes, badge 0001, the ninth-night craft, the requisitions to
STORES 17-C, Clip 0, the margin note on the Harrow Memo) and works out what they could add up to, which clues point where,
and how a player would come across them while doing ordinary work: sorting a chute, packing a bay, reading a bill.

The rule this file keeps: **the mysteries are made of the same stuff as the economy.** A clue is an item with a size, a
condition and a buyer. A revelation is a document you can sell, burn, deliver or keep in a sealed box under the counter. No
mystery needs combat, a night expedition or a chosen one. The tower gives up its secrets the way it gives up everything
else: in pieces, down a chute, at a price.

---

## Part one: how a mystery lives in the game

### Four rungs

Every mystery here climbs the same four rungs. The player can stop on any of them and lose nothing but the next rung.

1. **Talk.** A thing said at the counter, a line on Static at 23:00, a chalked sign, a client's aside on the back of a slip.
   Free, unreliable, everywhere. Talk tells you that a mystery exists and roughly where it lives.
2. **Trace.** A clue item. Usually paper (1x1, fragile, ruined by wet), sometimes an object (a cell with the wrong plant
   code, a glove, a warm brick). Traces carry hidden features shown as "?" until an instrument, an Old Hand or an Archivist
   reveals them: who wrote it, when, which machine typed it, what the stamp on the back says. Most traces come from the
   chute, from returning bays, or as the pay on small strange contracts.
3. **Proof.** Two or more traces packed together and sent to someone who can read them as a set: the Archivists' Reading
   Room, the Old Hands' Bench, Static's walkers, a lease counsel on 66. What comes back is a **collation**: one new document
   that says what the set means. A collation is more dangerous than any of its parts, and worth more.
4. **Use.** A proof is only worth what someone does with it. Every proof in this file has at least three possible
   recipients, and each one changes something different on the board, on the rate card or in the endings.

**In play:** the collation is the mechanic that makes clue-hunting a packing job. An Archivist slip reads "Bring the
following, together, in one bay: (1) a Harrow carbon, (2) a Glasshouse message slip dated 19.III.57, (3) any page of the
LR-2 log." Delivering two of three returns a partial collation and a hint at the third. Paper in an open bay on a free
shaft can get wet; a sealed bay matters. This is the same loop as every other contract, pointed at the past.

### Holding is a risk

Some traces are hot. A shelf or a stash with a hot item on it raises the stakes of every inspection: a brand protection
sweep, an HPS occupancy count, a Bureau visit after the North Chute drops something the Company wanted back. Hot items
carry a small flame mark. The player always knows what they are holding.

| Heat | Examples | What happens if found |
|---|---|---|
| Cold | a Works canteen token, a Forty plaque rubbing | nothing |
| Warm | a Harrow carbon, a Wellside well licence, an HW-9 cell | confiscated, a letter of concern |
| Hot | a full Pell Report, a Glasshouse message slip, a Return Column readout | confiscated, Standing hit, a Bureau or HPS slip on the board next morning that is a sting |
| Burning | the Options Paper, the Saye log, a live collation | all of the above, and a client who has sent you good work for a month stops sending it, without saying why |

A **lead-lined document tin** (a 1x2 special container, sealed, sold by penthouse fixers to people who should not need one)
hides one hot or burning item from a casual search. A **false-bottom bay insert** does the same in a drone.

### One truth per run

**Alternative (for the designer):** each mystery below gives a main version and several alternatives. The game could pick
one version per playthrough (a hidden "truth seed" at the start), so that the same clues lead somewhere different next
time and nobody can look the answer up. The clue items stay the same; what their hidden features say changes. Players who
compare notes would find they lived in slightly different towers, which suits a place where everyone's version of Wall
Night is a little different anyway.

## Part two: the mysteries

## 1. The Dark Floors

### The question

Floors 22 to 26 are the Clean Core, Line Nine, welded shut since Y40. Every night from the Works Whistle at 22:00 to just
before the 06:00 one, they glow amber and hum. They draw more power than anything but the Crown, they take a sixth of the
Waterhouse's output up a pipe nobody mentions, and they have not employed a person for forty-five years. What are they
making? Who gets it? Who keeps them running?

### What anyone on 17 can know without trying

- The yellow under the welds. The hum through five floors. The warm ceiling on 21 and the warm floor on 27.
- **Dawn slack**: some mornings the Mills sockets stay dead until 09:00, and the tide table did not say so.
- Weak taps on some evenings, a third down, on the same nights as the hum runs long.
- The Common Load Adjustment on 19, 20 and 21, four times anyone else's.
- The Dark chute: the chute run that serves 22 to 26 has no catch hatches and no Fall time. Something goes down it at night,
  in small amounts, and does not reach the Sump.
- "Production never stopped. It just stopped employing anyone." Everyone says it. Nobody says it as a joke.

### What the clues, put together, can show

These are the traces that exist across the corpus, with what each one is evidence of. A player who gathers all of them
still does not see inside. They see the shape of the thing from outside, like a hand under a sheet.

| Trace | Where it comes from | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| **HW-9 cell** (this year's date code) | North or West Chute, rare; requisition pay | The Core makes cells now. Not Bastion (BX). |
| **Halden Certified Renewed cell, no prefix** | Company Store, opened on a bench | Line Nine chips are being sold, laundered, under the Company's own mark |
| **Ruth Amadi's nineteen minutes** | Old Hands, at Kin | The Core's main cycle is not cell formation |
| **Founding-pattern drive contactor** | Lift Guild's Car House, the box with no sender | The Core makes Kessin parts and gives them to the Guild |
| **Nils Ferreira's assessment reports** | Archivists, a few copies | Drones lost in Core D between 22 and 26 bear a magnetic scorch: a linear motor runs there |
| **Joss Arkady's recording** | Archivists' memoir collection | A Line Nine man heard "the H-series", scrapped in Y39 |
| **Benedek Sarr's notebook** | Sarr, for sale | Six years of Common Load readings, hour by hour: the Core's schedule |
| **Aldo Moyle's sync-node map** | Aldo, if Hester trusts you | Three clock sync nodes inside the Core; the tower's time comes from there |
| **Process Line 3 drawing** | Archivists | Ultrapure water still goes up to 22 |
| **R6 rotor film** | any drone flown up R6 | Something volatile leaves the Core by the warm riser |
| **Requisition slips** (REQN 41-17-C-...) | the board, clip 48, overnight | The Core asks for things it cannot make |
| **The bricked window on 27** | the mushroom grower's lease on Overhum | The control room was moved, and the bricks are warm |
| **Dez's locker, 231, floor 23** | told at the Canteen | A way in, drone-sized, past a broken grille |

### Main version: Upkeep

When Marcus Halden's **Programme Daylight** took Line Nine lights-out in Y38, the engineers did not write a new control
system. They could not: nobody could, quickly. They took the one already running the building, the founders' allocation
system that balanced the Lung and the header tanks and fed the Sorrel meters (see **2. The Foreman**), and gave Line Nine to
it. The Foreman already had a production schedule it had been told to keep forever. It had been written in Y0 by Marrow
Teague's office and Halden's own engineers, and it was called, in the Works' forms, **Schedule U** ("Upkeep"): the list of
founding-era parts the tower would need over two centuries, and the rate at which the Works should make them so that
Document Control's stores never ran out.

Schedule U is why first-fit lasted as long as it did. For forty years the Works made its own spares alongside its products:
Kessin drive contactors, Lung plate film, Sorrel meter eyes, No. 2 buttons, lease-tile sensors, Tube beacon ribs. When the
lines closed in Y41, Line Nine kept making them, because nobody told the Foreman to stop and nobody was sure how. The
nineteen minutes are a contactor's burn-in, or a sheet of plate film taking its charge. The H-series Joss Arkady's father heard
were the founding cells, the Halden No. 4, still on Schedule U because the tower's emergency banks still need them.

So the Dark Floors are making **the tower**. Slowly, at founding rates, for a building that the schedule thinks is still
maintained by a Works with forty thousand people and a stores department.

That is the first half. The second half is what the Company did when it found out.

The Company found out in Y44, when a Works accountant asked why Line Nine was drawing raw materials for products that were
not being sold. It could not stop Upkeep without stopping the Foreman, and it could not stop the Foreman without stopping
the Ledger, which bills six thousand flats. It could not open the Core without people who knew how to run it, and those
people were being laid off. So it did what it does with everything: it put a price on it. Since Y44, Upkeep's output has been
split three ways:

1. **What the schedule sends to Stores.** Upkeep still issues parts to the Works stores system. The stores system still
   thinks Stores is in the Line Four stores cage on 17, sector C, and the rest of the Works' distribution system (the stores
   hoist, Lift Zero, the Tube's internal loop) still carries them. Most of it goes nowhere now: into a hoist that ends at a
   sealed hatch, into the dark lift. Some of it reaches people the system still recognises as Works: the Lift Guild's Car
   House (still on the Works' maintenance roll as "Building Services, Lifts"), which is why the contactors arrive with no
   sender.
2. **What the Company skims.** Every ninth night, a craft lands on the Crown Pad at 03:00 and takes on the cargo already
   waiting there (see **5. The roof**). The skim goes off the tower as "Bastion product" or straight to buyers who will pay for
   founding-era parts: Vantage above all, which wants Works chips to copy. A little comes back to the Company Store as
   **Halden Certified Renewed**, with no factory code, mixed with Bastion's real lot.
3. **What the Core asks for.** Schedule U was written for a works with a stores department that ordered in its inputs. The
   Core can make parts; it cannot make cathode powder, nitrile, glass, or replacement parts for its own robots that the
   robots cannot reach. For forty years the Company has fed it raw materials through Lift Zero, quietly, by contract, and
   for the last few years less of it, because Lift Zero is failing and because the people who signed the supply contracts
   have retired. So the Core has started doing what Schedule U says to do when supply fails: **raise a requisition to
   Stores.** That is the slip on clip 48, addressed to STORES 17-C. It is addressed to the player because the player is
   standing in the cage.

Why the secrecy, if the Company owns the Core and may do what it likes with it? Because of four words in *A City Stood On
End*, the founding prospectus: the Works pension would be paid "**as long as the Works stands**". In Y41 the Company split
itself so that the pension debt sat in a shell (Halden Works (Calder) Ltd) and the Core sat in Holdings. On paper the Works
closed as an employer and the shell has nothing to pay with. But the Works never closed as a **works**. It has been producing
every night for forty-five years. If that is ever proved to the one authority in the tower that still reads documents by
Y0 rules (the Arbiter, if the Arbiter is what the Old Hands think it is: see **2**), the shell and Holdings are one Works
again, and the Pension Book is a debt of 2,340 names with interest since Y62. That is what the Old Hands mean by "As it
stands." They have always meant it literally.

**In play:**
- **The requisition chain** is the spine of this mystery and needs no reveal to be worth doing. Each slip asks for a small,
  specific, often awkward thing (a Lung plate, a dozen Line Four gloves off the Glove Wall, a sync clock, cathode powder
  "any grade", a coil of 0.2 mm wire, a pair of men's boots size 44). It pays in things nobody else pays in: fresh HW-9
  cells, a first-fit part, a roll of Y40 scrip, once a whole Tube capsule with nothing in it but a printed receipt. The pay
  arrives in the next bay that comes home from any client, which is unsettling in a good way.
- **Doing the requisitions changes the tower a little.** Filled requisitions for Lung parts mean fewer air days next season.
  Filled requisitions for lift parts mean the Guild's cars break less (and the Guild does not know why). The player is
  keeping the building alive and nobody will ever thank them, which is the most Mills thing in the game.
- **Refusing them** has a cost too: after three unfilled requisitions, the Core sends one more, and it is not addressed to
  Stores. It is a **SHORTAGE NOTICE**, pinned on clip 48, routed to "Building Services", and in the following weeks
  something founding-era on floor 17 fails for want of a part (the tap post's meter eye, the 17-C spur gate, the Whistle).
- **The proof** is a collation of three things: an HW-9 cell, a requisition slip, and a page of Sarr's notebook. Delivered to
  the Archivists, it comes back as **"Line Nine: Evidence of Continuous Production"** (2x1, burning). That document is
  the key to the Old Hands' **Pension** ending (06a) and changes the price of every founding-era part in the tower if Static
  reads it on air.

**Alternative: the Contract.** No schedule, no ghost in the system. The Core is a straightforward lights-out line making
founding-pattern cells and chips for the Company, which sells them off-tower to people who pay double for anything marked
Halden Works. The secrecy is about the pension and about Bastion (the Company sells Bastion's cells as if they were the good
ones and the good ones as if they were Bastion's). The requisitions are the Company's own engineers cutting corners: cheaper
to get a part from a Mills workshop through an old form than through Halden procurement, which would ask why. Simpler, colder,
and it keeps the Foreman out of it.

**Alternative: the Tenant.** There is a person in there. When the trial began in Y38, the Line Nine control room on 27 kept
eleven engineers (see **7. The missing**). When the room was "moved somewhere else", one of them, **Wendell Saye, Clock
0098**, moved inside. He was thirty-four. He would be eighty-two. He has kept the Core running since, alone, on Company food
sent up Lift Zero, paid a salary into an account he cannot spend. The requisitions are typed by him on the only stock he
has. The pale plants in the Glove Wall (see **8**) are his, or his daughter's. The Company pays him because he is the only
person who can keep the Core running, and keeps him because he knows what the Glasshouse knew on Wall Night. This version is
sadder and smaller and has one great scene: the day the requisitions stop.

**Alternative: the Empty Room.** It stopped in Y57 when the water reached its supply lines. What hums is the cooling plant
on a dead control loop; the yellow is safelights on a timer nobody reset; the HW-9 cells are old stock relabelled at Bastion;
the requisitions are a vent kid's game (the Bulkheads crew found a stack of Y41 forms in the sub-fab and have been running the
whole floor for a year). The Company's unofficial version. Its weakness is the power draw: an empty room does not take a fifth of
the Bus.

**Alternative: the House.** The Core makes the tower, as in Upkeep, but the Company does not skim it, because the Company does
not know. Nobody in the Lantern has understood what Line Nine is since Marcus Halden died. The ninth-night craft is a penthouse
fixer's private business, and the fixer is the only person alive who has worked out what the requisitions are. The player and
the fixer are the two people in the Stack who know, and only one of them wants to keep it quiet.

**Hook:** all versions must explain the shift hours: the yellow and the hum keep the Works' night shift, 22:00 to 06:00, by the
Works Whistle, which is four minutes fast because its clock syncs to a node inside the Core. A lights-out line has no reason to
keep shift hours. A line run by a system that still believes there is a day shift who will need the hall at 06:00 does.

**Hook:** what the Core does in the day. The hum stops; the yellow goes out. But the power draw does not go to zero, and the
water does not. Upkeep in the day is the slow work: curing, cooling, film ageing. Or, in the Tenant version, it is when Saye
sleeps.

### The way in, and why the game should never quite use it

The corpus already has the drone-sized way in: Dez Achterberg's locker 231 on floor 23, reachable past a broken grille on a
shaft that brushes the Core's Sleeve, which vent kids and shaft rats both swear exists. A player who sends a small drone in
gets one flight's worth of the inside: a single returning bay.

**In play (once, late, optional):** a "Dez's toolroll" contract. The drone comes back with whatever fits a 2x2 bay: the
toolroll (Toolroom steel, signed 4019, worth a lot to Dez and nothing to anyone else), and one item it should not have
picked up, chosen by the truth seed: a sheet of gridded clean-room paper with a requisition half-typed on it (Tenant); a
contactor still warm from burn-in (Upkeep); a lot label from a Vantage purchase order (Contract); a vent kid's chalk sign
(Empty Room). The drone also comes back with the magnetic scorch on its frame. The player does not get to go in. Nobody does.
The game should keep it that way: the Dark Floors are the tower's unconscious, and the moment a player walks the halls the
yellow becomes ordinary.

**Alternative:** the drone does not come back. The next requisition slip lists it, by its serial number, under "ITEMS
RECEIVED".

## 2. The Foreman

### The question

The founding-era systems of the Stack were built to tell somebody what was wrong with the building. Since Y41 they have been
telling the Ledger, which only listens to numbers it can bill. But some of them seem to be doing more than reporting. Old Two
stops at floors nobody called. A dead man clocks in every night. Air meters nobody bills are read every week and sent
somewhere. The Arbiter's rulings now and then go for a tenant in a way no Company lawyer would allow. Is something still
running the tower as if it were Y20?

### What it is, in the main version

The Marrow Teague drawings call it **the Allocation**: the building management system of Halden Works & Residences, a
founding-era machine spread through the core on every mechanical floor, with its main frames in a room nobody below the
Lantern has seen. It balanced air and water by reading the Sorrel meters and the tile sensors. It ran the Kessin lifts'
schedules to the Works shifts. It kept time for the Whistle and every synced clock. It ran the Works payroll and billed the
flats (that part is the **Ledger**, in the Count House on 71, which the Company still uses). It kept the stores and the
Upkeep schedule. It had a small module for settling pay and housing grievances by rule, which the Old Hands call the
**Grievance Box** and which may be the Arbiter (05).

The Old Hands call the whole of it **the Foreman**, because that is how it behaved: it knew where everyone was supposed to be,
it sent the cars to the floors where the shifts changed, and it never went home.

The Foreman was never told the Works closed. Nobody wrote that instruction, because nobody who knew how to write it was kept on.
The Company switched off the parts it could find and billed through the parts it could use. Everything else kept going.

### Its signs

Each of these is already in the corpus as a loose hook. Together they are one machine keeping one timetable.

- **Old Two's ghost stops.** The freight car stops, unbidden, at a line floor at 06:00, 14:00 or 22:00, opens for eight
  seconds and closes. The Foreman is running a shift change for a shift that is not there. The Guild bills it as an
  "unscheduled stop".
- **Badge 0001 at 02:00.** The clock-in machine on floor 10 records Clock 0001, the first plant director, every night at
  02:00. In the Upkeep version this is the Core's nightly **production authorisation**: Programme Daylight's engineers could not
  remove the Y0 rule that continuous production needs a director's swipe every twenty-four hours, so they left a director's
  badge in a reader inside the Core and wired a relay to swipe it. 0001 was the badge to hand.
- **The air readings.** The air meters were switched off for billing in Y44 and never switched off. Every week a sealed Tube
  capsule carries the readings up. The Foreman is still balancing the Lung, and still sending its sums to the Works engineers'
  office, which was on 71 and is now a corner of the Count House where a clerk stamps the capsule RECEIVED and files it unread.
  There are forty-two years of capsules in a store room.
- **The capsules that don't add up.** The Company holds 4,800 Tube capsules on its inventory and has never sold one. The Old
  Hands count fewer. The Foreman's internal capsule loop (stores, meter readings, requisitions to Lift Zero) still runs on a
  route no drone flies.
- **The Roll Call** (new, see Part four): once a year, the night before Founding Day, every founding-era light in the Mills
  flickers floor by floor from 4 up to 29 and back, every meter's eye blinks, and every synced clock jumps to the true second.
  The Foreman's annual survey. Old people call it the Roll Call and count the floors.
- **The tile sensors.** The Partition in Y58 re-cut every lease by reading the tile sensors. They were still reporting, sixteen
  years after anyone last read them. They still are. Every square in the tower knows its own load. A **dead tile** (one whose
  sensor stopped) is a square the Foreman cannot see, and that is why sublet barons prize them.
- **The Whistle and the four minutes.** Every founding-era clock on 17 is four minutes fast because it syncs to a node in the
  Core. The Foreman keeps Works time, not Company time. The Ledger keeps Company time. Rent falls due at Company midnight; the
  Foreman's day ends four minutes earlier.

### The Grievance Box

The Arbiter (05) is offered in three versions: a panel, an old man with a fountain pen, and a founding-era module. This file
takes the third as the main version **for the purpose of the mysteries**, because it makes the documents matter.

The Grievance Box rules by the rules it was given in Y0, for a company that needed its workers and expected disputes about
pay deductions, housing allocations and injuries on the line. It has no idea that the Works closed, that the Company split
into Holdings and Calder Ltd, or that the tenants are no longer employees. Most petitions arrive on Company-drafted forms that
frame the dispute as a lease matter, and lease matters it rules on as allocation matters, for the Company, because in Y0 the
Company owned the allocation. But a petition framed in **Y0 terms**, on the old forms, citing the old schedules, gets read the
way it would have been read in Y20. That is why some determinations go "inexplicably" for the tenant. Somebody found the old
wording.

**In play:**
- **Works forms** are items. A blank **Works grievance form G-7** (1x1, Y0 stock, from Archivists or the stores hoist) used
  instead of the Company's A-1 changes the odds the Arbiter slip shows ("past determinations on G-7: 9 of 14 for the
  petitioner"). The Company amends the bylaw a few weeks after each tenant win. A G-7 is single-use; there are not many left.
- **Clock-number petitions.** A petition carrying a clock number (an Old Hand's, or the player's own if apprenticed, 06a) is
  read as a worker's grievance. This is how the **Pension** ending can work: the Pension Book filed on G-7s by four hundred
  living clock numbers, with the Line Nine collation attached.
- **The Foreman's favours.** In the Upkeep version, filling requisitions for a long time raises a hidden-to-everyone but
  visible-to-the-player number: Stores 17-C's **issue record**. At a high enough record, the Foreman starts treating 17-C as a
  real stores cage: Old Two's ghost stops start happening at 17 at 14:00, with something on the floor of the car for the
  player (a crate of Upkeep parts with "ISSUE TO 17-C" stencilled on it). The player has become a department.

**Alternative: no Foreman.** Each sign has its own dull cause: Old Two's controller has a corrupted timetable; 0001 is a
security guard's joke that outlived the guard; the air readings are an unfixed habit of the Count House; the Arbiter is a
panel. The mysteries become a list of small, separate rots instead of one sleeping mind, which is realistic and less fun.

**Alternative: the Company runs it.** The Foreman exists and the Company knows exactly how to command it. It chooses the ghost
stops (they move the Guild's cars on schedules that suit the Crown's staff), it reads the air because it is preparing to bill
it again (the **air line at zero** on every statement, 01), and the Arbiter's tenant wins are a pressure valve, as the Company
pays and amends. A colder tower. The Old Hands' fear becomes the Archivists' fact.

**Hook:** what the Foreman would do if it noticed. It was built to protect the structure first. On Wall Night, a human had to
close the flood doors because the sensors had been disconnected in Y49. The Old Hands' worst fear, never said aloud: one day the
Foreman will reconnect something by itself, read the Mills as flooded or overloaded or contaminated, and seal it, the way the
doors sealed the Gate Floor, "as designed".

**Hook:** the Foreman's room. Marrow's plans show the Allocation's main frames on a mechanical floor. Not which one. The
Archivists have three candidates: 30 (the transfer deck, under the Receipt), 60 (beside the middle Lung), 90 (beside the roof
cisterns). The Old Hands say none of them. "It's where the clocks point."

## 3. The Backrun: why the meters sometimes run backwards

### The question

Every Mills tenant has seen it once or twice and nobody has a good explanation. For a few minutes, usually before dawn,
sometimes during the Turn at 06:00 or 18:00, the brass drums of a Sorrel meter turn the wrong way. Water, power, once in a while
air: the count goes **down**. It never goes down by much. The next bill shows nothing: the Ledger prints the draw as if the drums
had stopped. People call it a **backrun**.

### What people say

- "The meter's giving it back." Said with a shrug, the way people say "the Whistle's late".
- Folk belief in the Mills: a meter that runs back before dawn means a death on the floor before the next Fall. Old women
  stop the drums with a finger to "keep it". (A Sorrel meter cannot be stopped with a finger. People believe it can.)
- Stinkers believe a backrun means the Company has switched a feed somewhere and the line is soft for an hour: a good time to
  fill a still from the service pipe.
- Hollis Brandt, Clock 3307, who fitted half the meters in the Mills, says when asked: "They were built to." And then he will
  not say anything else.

### What it is, in the main version

The Sorrel meters were not built to bill. They were built to **balance**, and balancing needs to know which way a flow is
going. Every Sorrel meter is a two-way counter. Marrow Teague's office designed the Stack as a building that would return
things as well as take them: rain caught on landings and fed back into the header tanks (the rain taps worked both ways in
the first years), heat from the Works' process water fed up into the flats in winter, power from the Kessin cars braking on
the way down fed back into the Bus. A flat that sent something back was owed for it. The Ledger had a column for it.

The column is called the **Return** and it is still there. Every backrun since Y0 has been counted, per meter, into a
founding-era account in the Ledger, and since Y43, when the meters started billing, the Ledger has printed only the draw and
silently kept the Return. Nobody turned the column off; turning things off in the Ledger is how you lose a month's billing.
So every lease in the tower has a Return balance. Most are tiny. Some, on floors near the Bus or under the break tanks, are
not.

What makes meters run back now:

- **The Core's dump.** At the end of a long Dark Floor night, around 05:40, the Core finishes its run and its banks push
  stored charge back into the Bus before the day load comes on. For a few minutes the Bus on the Mills Steps flows the wrong
  way, and power meters on 19, 20, 21 and a few floors either side tick backward. This is the backrun people see before dawn,
  and it comes on the mornings after the hum ran long. It is also why the Common Load on those floors is what it is: the
  Ledger bills the forward flow to the floor and drops the backward.
- **The Turn.** When the Lung switches its fans at 06:00 and 18:00 and every riser reverses draught, air vanes in the Mills spin
  backward for a minute. Air is billed at zero, so nobody cares except the Foreman, which still balances it.
- **Process Line 3.** On heavy Dark Floor nights the ultrapure line to 22 draws so hard on the Mills header that the pressure
  in the break tank at 29 drops below the riser's head, and some Mills taps on the old founding pipe briefly **suck back**:
  their meters run a few hundred millilitres in reverse. Stinkers call this the soft hour. The water that runs back is yours.
  It goes up a pipe to the Core.
- **Lifts braking.** Old Two coming down loaded from 60 to 4 still regenerates into the Bus, as built. Meters on the Plinth
  freight core's feed (floors 4 to 10) run back a little every time it descends full. The Sump knows Old Two's descents by
  their meters.

**In play:**
- **The Return readout.** A founding-era meter has a second window under a brass flap that most people have never noticed:
  the Return drums. Opening it breaks the seal (fine, 150 cr, see 04). Reading it properly needs a **Sorrel key** (the trefoil
  Works key: see 09's lapsed design hook; any toolroom can cut one now that the design right lapsed in Y81, if they know). A
  readout is a 1x1 paper item the player writes themselves, hot.
- **Claiming the Return.** A Return readout plus a Works grievance form G-7 sent to the Arbiter is a petition the Grievance
  Box reads as a Y0 pay dispute: "credit owed to the occupant for returned supply". It usually rules for the tenant. The award
  arrives as **credit notes** (05) against future bills, and the Company amends the bylaw within the month (Clause 33's list of
  things not to do to a meter gains a new word every time). The first claim is a real windfall; the second is fought; the third
  starts the **Meter Review** event (the Company replaces Sorrel meters on your floor with plastic ones that cannot run back,
  and the floor blames you).
- **The floor's Return.** The great prize is not one lease's Return but a whole floor's: forty-odd years of backruns on floors 19
  to 21, every lease's balance in a column the Company has never had to show. A Co-op Floor meeting could vote to file it
  together. That is a strike-sized event, and a path toward the **Co-op** ending (13).
- **Backrun as a resource.** A player who logs the backruns (a meter-log slip on the wall, filled hour by hour, the same job as
  logging the hum) learns the Core's schedule from their own bill. On a predicted backrun morning, a still filled from the
  service pipe during the soft hour draws "free" water at a cost of risk.

**Alternative: the Thieves.** There is no Return column. Backruns are real, but they are meter tampering by people upstream: a
Middle tenant's back-feed through the tiles, a Stinker's pump drawing so hard it reverses a neighbour's meter. The Ledger zeroes
them because they are errors. The mystery is just the Mills catching itself stealing from itself.

**Alternative: the Company's account.** The Return column exists and the Company found it years ago. It has been paying the
Return, not to tenants, but into a **Return Fund** held by Halden Works Holdings, as "credit due to the Works from its own
operations". The Dark Floors earn money off the Mills' meters twice: once when the Mills is billed for the Common Load, and once
when the backrun the Core causes is credited to the Core.

**Hook:** one meter in the tower has never run forward. It is on a landing on 41, beside the Gap, on a pipe that goes nowhere,
and its Return drums show a number so large that the Archivists who read it in Y80 thought the meter was broken. It is not. It
is counting something that has been flowing back into the tower since Y0, and it is not water, power or air.

## 4. Wall Night: who let the water in

### The question

Everyone knows the list (01): the Works pumped the ground away, the Company owned the Wall and stopped mending it, the lagoon
went first, the Company knew three days early, the doors were shut from upstairs, the wells were blamed, the survivors were
sold a bed and then the water. Knowing changes nothing about the rent. What nobody has is the **who**: whose initials are in
the margin of the Harrow Memo, who answered Harrow's call, who made the 22:24 call to the Gate Floor, who decided the guest
list, who decided not to lower the lagoon. The Vance Inquiry made sure nobody had to say.

### The three decisions

Wall Night was not one decision. It was three, each a few hours apart, and each made by someone who could have made it
differently.

**The first decision: not lowering the lagoon (17th, 14:00).** Operation Keel's item 4: "Lagoon Six: no drawdown at this time
(permitting, timing, public reaction)." Lowering Lagoon Six to sea through its outfall meant discharging slurry to the open
bay. Under the provincial discharge permit, that was a **reportable release**: a public notice, a fine, an inspection of the
lagoon, and an inspection of the lagoon meant someone reading the Wall Book. "Permitting" means the Company chose a storm over a
form.

**The second decision: LR-2 (19th, about 20:30).** This is the new piece this file proposes. Lagoon Six had two ways to let
water out. The **seaward outfall**, which Harrow begged for at 20:15 and which needed a director's sign-off, because using it was
a discharge to sea. And the **landward relief**, valve **LR-2**, a culvert from the back of the lagoon into the Tannery Reach
channel, built in the Y20s to drain rainwater off the lagoon's surface after storms. LR-2 discharged to an "internal drainage
channel" on Halden Civil's own concession. It needed no permit and no director. It needed a control room operator.

At about 20:30, after Harrow's call and the "stand by", the control room opened LR-2. It took pressure off the back of the bank,
for a while. It also sent the lagoon's top layer, the thinnest slurry, down the Tannery Reach channel, which was already running
backwards into the town on the surge, and into Wellside's drains. Survivors from Wellside say, and the Tally's interviews are
consistent about it: **the grey came up the drains first**, before the wave, around 20:40. The Inquiry recorded this as "early
inundation by surge water mixed with sediment". It was the lagoon, let out the back door so that nobody would have to file a
discharge notice. The bank failed at 21:12 anyway.

**The third decision: the doors (19th, 22:24).** The phone call from the Glasshouse to the Gate Floor, twice, telling Lomax to
secure the stair doors. The Old Hands think the order was right and should not have been necessary. This file adds who, in the
main version: the call came from the Glasshouse's **night switchboard**, on the instruction of the head of Works security, who
had been told by the Waterhouse crew (sealed in on 5 to 8) that grey water was coming under the Switch Hall doors on 4. The
order to close the doors was made to protect the Waterhouse, which was the Company's water, which became its monopoly. The
people on the Gate Floor were drowned to keep the product dry.

### The person in the margin

The Harrow Memo's margin note ("*Noted. No budget this cycle. Monitor.*") is initialled by a director "whose initials the Inquiry
was told could not be identified". The initials are **E.V.**

**Main version:** **Edmund Varne**, Director of Halden Civil from Y48 to Y57. Husband of Senna Varne. The Varnes received the
**Skywater licence** in the First Month of Y57, eleven weeks before the Drowning (03: the date the Varnes call "a clerical
error"). Edmund Varne was in the Glasshouse on the night, a guest of the Founding Reception, and it was his control room that
called the Glasshouse at 20:20 and was answered by a reception steward who took a message. He died in Y63, on 97, of nothing in
particular. Senna is a hundred and four and drinks only rain.

**Alternative:** E.V. is **Esme Vollan**, Halden Civil's finance director, who never went near the Wall, and whose "no budget"
was simply true: the budget had been set by the board in Y52. The person in the margin is a clerk with a pen. The guilt is a
spreadsheet and has no face. (This is the version that makes Wall Night hardest to forgive, because there is nobody to hate.)

**Alternative:** the initials were added later. The Inquiry was given a photocopy, and the photocopy shows the margin note in a
different ink density from the memo's carbon. Somebody wanted the note to exist (it is the best evidence of negligence there is)
and wrote it after the fact. The Old Hands, perhaps, again (see the Rusk Dissent alternative in 01). True in spirit, forged in
hand.

### The Options Paper

**Main version.** In Y52, the year Marcus Halden had his stroke and Imogen Halden-Ward took the chair, Halden Civil commissioned a
consultancy in the province to write a **Calder Coastal Options Appraisal**. It is forty pages. Everyone who knows of it calls it
**the Options Paper**. It set out three options for the Wall:

- **Option A: Defend.** Raise the northern Wall by two metres and armour the Shared Bank. Cost: roughly the Pell Report's
  redacted figure. Pay-back: never; Calder was not paying Halden for anything any more.
- **Option B: Retreat.** Fund the relocation of Wellside and Low Calder inland, with the province. Cost: lower than A, and a
  public admission that the town was sinking because of the Works.
- **Option C: Accept.** Maintain the Wall to an "inspection only" standard; accept the residual risk; prepare for "the
  vesting of unrecoverable land in the concession holder" under the provincial land code, and for "the opportunity of a
  re-tenanting of the Residences on a market basis following any population displacement".

The board minuted Option C in the Fourth Month of Y52. The Wall budget fell that year from 255,000 cr to 90,000 cr "inspection
only" (01). The **Ascension Sales** prospectus, sold to the Esplanade families in Y58, was typeset in Y55: the printer's date
code is on the back cover. The Skywater licence was signed in I.57. The Wells Inquiry's report reads as if written in advance,
because parts of it were: its chapter on private wells is lifted, sentence for sentence, from the Options Paper's appendix
"Narrative Management in the Event of Displacement".

Nobody planned a storm. They planned for the day one came, and they planned to make money from it.

**Where it is.** Three copies were printed. One went to the board, one to Halden Civil, one to the consultants. The board's copy
is in the Lantern. Halden Civil's copy went into the Union's archive boxes by mistake on the 18th of the Third Month, Y57, when
Works security moved "the Union's old archive" to secure storage and moved Halden Civil's filing cabinet from the Gate Floor
along with it. The boxes were never seen again. In the main version they are in **Lift Zero**: Works security parked them in the
one place nobody could reach, and then everybody who knew was laid off or died. The Core has been riding up and down with them
for twenty-nine years. In the Upkeep version the Foreman knows they are there, as inventory, and lists them on its stores
manifest as **"ARCHIVE, UNION, 14 BOXES, CONDITION: DRY"**.

**In play:** the Options Paper is the deepest object in the game and should be reachable only through several mysteries at once:
the requisitions (to earn the Foreman's issue record), the stores hoist (Marrow's Ninth, see **6**), and a Lift Zero contract
(see seeds). What the player does with it is one of the largest choices in the endings (13):
- to **Static**: read on air over three nights. Nothing changes in law. Everything changes on the board: Company slips dry up in
  the Mills for a season, Rain Church and Tide Folk slips flood in, HPS sweeps double, and the word "Option C" goes up in chalk
  next to the 5 and the 7.
- to the **Archivists**: filed, copied, preserved. Slow. Some years later (late game) a provincial body asks for it.
- to the **Arbiter** on a G-7, with the Tally: the Grievance Box cannot rule on a flood; it can rule on the Works' duty of care to
  its housed workers in Y0 terms, and in those terms Wellside was Works housing (the Works built half of it in the boom). Its
  determination is the only official document in the tower that would say the Company was at fault. It pays nothing. It is
  pinned on every Register board.
- to a **penthouse fixer**, for the Lantern: a fortune, a lease on the Terraces, and a clause in the contract that says you never
  owned it. The most direct route to the **Crown** ending, and the most expensive one.
- **burned**, in your own stove, which the game lets you do with any paper item, and which nobody ever knows you did.

**Alternative: no paper.** There was no Options Paper; there did not need to be one. A board does not write down that it will let
a town drown. It just cuts a budget every year, and every year it is somebody else's decision. The clues all exist, and they
point at a pattern and never at a page. Truer to life and harder to play. The deepest document in this version is the Wall
Book's refusals column, which is already enough.

### The scratches on the stair side

The corpus asks (01): divers say the south stair door is closed, there are scratches in the paint on the stair side at shoulder
height, and a pry bar is still wedged in the hinge gap on the Gate Floor side. Somebody tried to open it again, from the stair
side, after it shut. Who?

**Main version:** **Ostrowski.** The guard who held the south door with Lomax for the Seven Minutes. He was on the stair side when
it closed; Lomax was not. Ostrowski spent the next three hours trying to open it again with his hands and his keys, while the
water rose behind it, until the people he had let up the stair carried him up to 10. He lived, never worked again, and died on 12
in Y75. His daughter **Wanda Ostrowski**, now sixty, keeps a Mills noodle stall on 14 and has never spoken about it. She has his
keys.

**Alternative:** Lomax. He was on the stair side after all (01's alternative); he scratched the door and then walked up the stairs
and lived and was dismissed. The pry bar on the far side was Ostrowski's, left as Ostrowski drowned.

**Alternative:** nobody human. The flood doors' hydraulic closers were founding-era, and the Foreman tried, for several hours, to
reopen them on a timer after the water fell below its sensors, which had been disconnected. What scratched the paint was the
door's own latch, hammering. The Old Hands know the sound. They do not talk about it.

**In play:** Wanda's keys (a ring of Works security keys, 1x1, cold) are a gentle hook: she will give them to someone who
brings her a Gate Floor duty log page, for her father's name in it. One of the keys opens the flood door control cabinet on
4, which is a long way from anything, and which (in the Upkeep version) contains the disconnected sensor leads, labelled in
Y49 handwriting: "DISCONNECTED AT REQUEST OF FACILITIES. DO NOT RECONNECT." Reconnecting them is the kind of choice the game
should allow and never explain.

## 5. The roof

### The question

Nobody below 90 has stood on the roof. What is known of it is a list: the Founders' Gutter, the roof cisterns' inlets, the Mast,
the Crown Pad, and beside the Mast, under the Lantern's floor, the eight capped heads of the Works risers, seven cold and one warm.
On still nights the Lantern smells faintly of solvent. A craft lands on the Crown Pad every ninth night at 03:00, takes on cargo
nobody on the Crown staff loaded, and leaves. What is up there, and what is the Lantern breathing?

### The warm cap and the Lantern cough

Ilse Marrow (of Marrow Teague) objected in writing in Y−4: the Glasshouse sat directly over the caps of the Works' exhaust risers. Halden
overruled her. The Glasshouse got its own filtered roof air and windows that never opened. Ansel Halden died in the Glasshouse in
Y31 "of a lung condition his doctors did not name". 

**Main version:** the riser caps leak. They always have. The seals Marrow specified were downgraded in Y−3 to save money on the
Glasshouse's budget overrun, and the roof air intakes the Glasshouse uses for its "separately filtered" supply sit eleven metres
from cap R6, which is the warm one, which is the one still venting the Core. The Halden family has been breathing the Works for
eighty-six years. The Crown's physicians on 91 have a name for it in their private notes: **Lantern cough**. It is what killed
Ansel, what gave Marcus his stroke (the solvent family that leaks from R6 is hard on blood vessels), and what every Halden heir has
had, quietly, by sixty. The Crown's doctors treat it with a Vey compound that is not on any price list. The family knows. It has
decided, every generation, that moving out of the Lantern would be a statement about the tower that the tower cannot afford.

So the people at the top are poisoned by the same works as the people at the bottom, more slowly, more expensively, with better
doctors. It does not make anyone equal. It makes the Lantern stay where it is.

**In play:**
- **Crown pharmacy slips.** Unsigned contracts from the Postern, staff consignment, asking for strange specific things: activated
  carbon cloth by the metre, sealant tape rated for solvents, a founding-era Lung plate (the Crown has its own spares, so why ask?),
  a respirator "of Works pattern, half-face, Line Nine issue". Pay is very high and the rejection slips are very polite.
- A **Crown physician's note** (1x1, hot) from the North Chute: a prescription with "LC, maintenance dose" and a Vey compound code.
  The Archivists will pay; Static will pay more; the Company will send a fixer to buy it back at a price the player names.

**Alternative:** there is no leak; the solvent smell is the Skywater Hall's cleaning line, and Ansel died of ordinary illness. The
Halden heirs are healthy and long-lived, and the only lungs ruined by the Works are the ones below 30. (The colder version. The tone
brief would probably prefer it: no poetic justice.)

### What the ninth-night craft carries

Every ninth night at 03:00 a craft lands on the Crown Pad, takes on cargo already waiting there, and leaves.

**Main version (Upkeep):** the cargo is the Company's skim of the Core's output (see **1**). It reaches the roof through **R9**, the
ninth riser, "reserve, capped" in Marrow's first drawings (see **6**), which in Y38 Programme Daylight's engineers fitted with a
capsule hoist: a sealed cargo duct from the Core to a hatch in the Lantern's service floor beside the warm cap. The Foreman's stores
system thinks R9 is the "export despatch" of the Works, which once sent finished cells to the Gate Floor quays. It still sends
finished goods there every ninth night, because nine nights is the Works' old despatch cycle. A Lantern service technician moves the
crates from the hatch to the pad at 02:40. He is on the Crown staff list as a "roof gardener". The craft is chartered through a
penthouse fixer, flies under a Meridian Atmospherics (Sounding) maintenance registration, and goes to **Vantage**.

So Halden's rival tower is built partly on Halden's own founding spares, which Halden sells to it because it cannot admit it makes
them. Corvane, which runs Vantage, has spent twenty years trying to copy Works chips and failing, and buying them by the crate
through Halden is cheaper than failing.

**Alternative:** the craft goes to **Bastion**, and the crates are not finished goods but the Core's spent tools and dead robots,
sent to Bastion's Line (which runs Halden Works Line Two machines) to be refurbished by prisoners and sent back. The Core's
maintenance is done by convicts nine kilometres away who have never seen it. Cosmo Fairweather's brass plate ("HALDEN WORKS, LINE
TWO, Y3", 07) is one end of the loop.

**Alternative:** the craft brings things **in**: raw materials for the Core, dropped on the pad and lowered down R9, because Lift
Zero is failing. The "cargo taken on" is empty crates going back. The Crown Pad's log would show more mass landing than leaving.

**Hook:** the craft's name, on the Sounding's radar logs (which Meridian sells by the week), is **Moorhen**. It is logged every ninth
night for twenty-two years. In Y78, during the Lift Strike, it came every night for twelve nights.

### The first cell

In the glass case in Lantern Reception on 101 is the first cell off Line One, carried up by Ansel Halden's bare hand on Founding Day,
Y0. "Still charged, according to the Company. Nobody has been allowed to test it."

**Main version:** it is charged. It is a Halden No. 4, the founding cell, and it has been charged every year since Y0 on the night of
the Roll Call (see Part four), by a contact plate under the plinth that the Foreman uses to keep it topped up, because Schedule U lists
it as **"REFERENCE CELL, LINE ONE, KEEP AT FULL"**: the standard every No. 4 the Works ever made was tested against. If it were ever
taken out of the case and tested, it would show that a No. 4 can last eighty-six years. Nobody in the Lantern knows why it is still
charged. They tell visitors it is a symbol.

**Alternative:** it was swapped. The real first cell was taken during Heritage Week in Y76, when it was brought down to 30 and someone
chalked **5** on the glass; the cell carried back up on the fourth night was a dummy. The real one is in the Mills. An Old Hand has it.
Or Static does: it is what powers the transmitter on the nights the signal reaches the Hulk.

**In play:** a contract you will only see if you have filled at least one requisition: "REFERENCE CELL, LINE ONE, CONDITION CHECK"
appears on clip 48, routed to Stores, asking for "1 EA REFERENCE CELL FOR TEST". The Foreman wants its standard back. The only way to
fill it is to get the cell out of the Lantern, which is not a thing the game makes possible, except through a penthouse fixer, at a
price that is itself a story.

### The Apple

The only apple tree in Calder Bay stands in a founding-era planter in Orchard House on 96 and has stopped setting fruit. The gardener
knows: its drainage was plumbed into a line that now runs warm, from below (02). **Main version:** the warm line is R9's jacket. The
Core's despatch duct passes behind Orchard House's service wall, and the planter's drain was tied into R9's condensate line in Y38
by an engineer who needed somewhere to run it. The Apple's roots sit in water that is warm and faintly sweet with solvent.

**In play:** an Orchard House slip: "Planter drain: rerouting, discreet, one day's work, references required." The fix is a hose and a
founding-era elbow; the bonus is the Vellacotts' gratitude and, the following autumn, real apples in your bay. The **Hook** is that the
person who fixes the drain is the first person outside the Lantern to put a hand on R9.

## 6. Marrow's Ninth

### The question

Ilse Marrow designed the Stack and wrote, "Build it so that nobody needs to understand it to live in it, and so that somebody always
can." Her drawing set, the Marrow Set, ran to four thousand sheets. The Archivists hold about a third of it, scattered and wet. The
Company claims to hold the rest and has never shown it. There is a model of the whole building under glass in the Founders' Room on
100, every floor removable, every floor of the Plinth painted grey and unlabelled. What did the architect leave in the building that
the Company does not want read?

### What is known

- **R9**, "reserve, capped", in the first set and absent from every later sheet. Shaft rats say a sealed duct runs from the Dark Floors
  to somewhere above 90.
- **The stores hoist**: the small goods lift that carried requisitioned parts between the lines and central stores. Its bottom is a
  sealed hatch in the ceiling of 16, which is the floor of 17, which is under the player's cage. Chalker **Dorrit Vance** resurveys the
  twelve squares under it every quarter (02).
- **Drawing S-17-02, Line Four Stores, Rev. B**: a copy the Archivists will trade for a rubbing of the maker's stamp under square C4
  of the player's own lease (11, S-61).
- **The M-FEED tanks**: the nutrient wash for mendstone, the self-healing grey that grows calcite into its own cracks. Nobody has the
  recipe (09).
- **The Plinth painted grey on the model.** Unlabelled. In Marrow's own hand on the cover sheet, which the Archivists have: "Plinth to
  be finished by the Works."

### Main version: the Ninth was a promise

The ninth riser was Marrow's. Not an exhaust riser at all: she drew it in the first set as a **service spine** that would connect the
Works' stores (on 17, by the lines, where the parts were used) to every mechanical floor in the tower and to the roof, so that the
Residences could be maintained from the Works' stores "for as long as the Works stands, and after". The stores hoist under the player's
cage is its foot. Lift Zero shares its well from 21 to 27. Above the Core, R9 runs up the core inside the Lung trunks' casing, past
30, 60 and 90, to the roof.

Her idea, in her notes (Archivist sheet M-4, water-stained): "Every lid opens. The Works will make what the tower needs. The stores
will hold it. The ninth will carry it. When the Works is gone, the stores must still issue." She meant that the tower should be
maintainable after the Works closed. She built the spares system into the building's spine, and wrote Schedule U to feed it.

Halden took R9 out of the drawings in Y−4, when he added the Glasshouse. Not to hide it: to sell it. The Glasshouse needed a private
goods lift from the roof pad to the Lantern, and R9's top section was the obvious shaft. So R9 was cut at 90: below it the stores
spine, above it the Lantern's own lift. In Y38 Programme Daylight joined them back up for the Core's despatch (see **5**).

So the player's lease, the Line Four stores cage, sits on the foot of the founders' answer to the question the whole tower is now
failing: who will keep it running when nobody remembers how? Marrow's answer was: the stores on 17. That is the player.

**In play:**
- **The rubbing (S-61) → Drawing S-17-02 → the hatch.** The drawing shows the stores hoist under square C4 of the player's lease and
  a **stores pit** under the tiles: a 2x3 founding-era cavity, dry, lined, with a hoist cradle in it. Opening it (lifting C4 and the
  plates under it) gives the player a hidden special container: a **stores pit**, sealed and dry, not on any lease map, invisible to
  inspections because no inspector's plan shows it. It is the best hiding place in the game, and it was built for exactly that
  shop, by the woman who designed the building.
- **The hoist cradle** still answers the Foreman. In the Upkeep version, once the player's issue record is high enough, the cradle
  starts to bring things down from the Core on the Works' old despatch hours (06:00, 14:00, 22:00): the ghost stops of Old Two, but
  under your own floor.
- **Dorrit Vance**, chalker, has been resurveying the squares under the hoist every quarter because the Company told her to watch for
  movement there. She does not know why. She is happy to be told, for a price, and she will report anyone who opens the hatch unless
  she is the person who was told.

**Alternative: the Ninth was a weapon.** Ilse Marrow and Halden fell out in Y−4 over more than the Glasshouse. In the Archivists'
version she drew R9 as an independent **emergency vent**: a way to purge the Plinth of process gas if the Works ever had a fire or a
release, venting straight to the roof, past the Glasshouse. Halden removed it because it would have vented under his windows. The Core
uses it now to vent. The warm cap is R9's, not R6's; R6 is a decoy. A smaller story, more about the roof than the shop.

**Alternative: the Ninth never existed.** "R9, reserve, capped" is a drafting error on one sheet, copied by Archivists who wanted it
to mean something. Every clue in this dossier then belongs to Lift Zero alone.

### The missing two-thirds

**Hook:** the rest of the Marrow Set. The Company says it is "held in Halden's records". The Archivists think it went into the Lantern
in Y−4 and was never seen again. The Old Hands think it never existed as a set: Marrow kept the key sheets herself and took them with
her when she left Calder in Y2, and they are somewhere in the province, in a firm that no longer exists, in a box nobody has opened.
A penthouse fixer has been offering Crown households a "Marrow sheet, original, signed" for years; every one an Archivist has seen is
a tracing. **In play:** a Marrow sheet is a trace that is always worth authenticating. A real one (rare, from a Crown estate sale)
shows something nobody else has seen. Each real sheet, collected, opens one more line of Marrow's notes on the Reading Room's wall.

**Hook:** the mendstone recipe. The M-FEED tanks on the mechanical floors are dry. The recipe was in Document Control. Marrow's
cover sheet notes "M-FEED per Works Chem. Sch. 4". **Schedule 4** is in the Pell Report's appendix, because Tamsin Pell's first job
at the Works was water chemistry and she kept the schedule she used. Whoever has the full Pell Report has the recipe for making the
tower heal its own cracks, and has never looked past page forty.

## 7. The missing

### The question

The Stack is full of people who are not where they should be. Some are the dead of Wall Night whose names the Tally is still
adding. Some are the living who took the boat. But a few disappearances, set side by side, make a pattern.

### The Eleven of 27

When Line Nine went lights-out in Y38, eleven engineers watched the trial from the control room on 27. When the trial "never ended",
the control room was "moved somewhere else" and nobody below the directors' floors was told where. The eleven are not in the Pension
Book. None of them was laid off in Y41. None of them appears in the Lifeline registers, the Tally, the Works' final payroll, or the
Calder Registry's deaths. Their names are known only from a framed photograph of the Line Nine commissioning team (Y26) that hangs,
unlabelled, in the Canteen on 14, and from the Old Hands, who can name nine of the eleven and will not name the other two.

### The unclocked: where the clean-room hands are going

Over the last six years, five Old Hands in the 0000s (the clean-room and chip-fab range, the people who know founding chips by
touch) have left the Mills. Each one left in the same way: a visit from a well-dressed man from above 60, a week of saying nothing at
the Canteen, and then gone, with their lease surrendered in good order and their pension claim **withdrawn from the Pension Book in
writing**. No death registered. No forwarding. Their families got a lump sum in credits (not scrip), and stopped talking to the Canteen.

**Main version:** the man is **Lyle Penhallow**, a penthouse fixer whose clients include the Lantern. He offers each of them the
thing they have been denied for twenty-four years: **their pension, in full, in credits, with back pay**, on three conditions: they
withdraw from the Pension Book, they sign a confidentiality deed, and they come and work "a few hours a week, on heritage systems,
somewhere comfortable". Where they go is the Lantern's Line Nine control room (main version above), or to the Lift Zero landing on 27,
or (in the Tenant version) inside, to help Saye, who is getting old. The Company is quietly replacing the Eleven of 27 with the last
people alive who can do their job, and paying them the pension it denies everyone else to do it.

Ruth Amadi, Clock 0412, has had the visit. She has not decided. She is nearly blind and she wants her heart pills paid for.

**In play:**
- **"Pills for Ruth"** (06a) becomes a choice with weight. If the player keeps her supplied, cheaply, through the Voided, she stays.
  If the player stops, or the price goes up, she takes Penhallow's offer, and the Canteen loses its only clean-room hand: the Bench
  can no longer reveal chip features, and the next time the Core sends a requisition, the request is something only someone who has
  stood in the Core would know how to fill, and it is typed in a slightly different way.
- **The withdrawn names.** The Pension Book (a large item, 2x3, carried to the Arbiter once a year by contract) has five entries struck
  through in a different ink, with "WITHDRAWN, BY DEED" written beside them. An Archivist collation of the five deeds' dates against
  the ninth-night craft's log (radar logs from the Sounding) shows that each of the five left the tower on a ninth night.
- **Penhallow as a client.** He sends good slips to players with high Standing: authentication jobs, founding-era parts wanted,
  always paid in credits, never scrip. If the player is an apprenticed Old Hand (06a), he eventually makes the player the same offer.
  It is the **fourth** way up the tower nobody talks about: you can reach the Crown by being needed.

## 8. The Pale Garden

### The question

Clip 0 (11, S-60): a slip in the corner of the board nobody clears, on thin grey paper with a faint grid printed in it, in handwriting
"very young or very old": *Seeds of anything that will grow in the dark. Anything. Leave them in the left glove of the Glove Wall, third
row.* The seed is gone next morning. A week later something is left in the same glove: a tiny, pale, perfect mushroom of a kind no one
in the Mills has seen, or a sprig of a plant with white leaves. Who is growing things in the dark, and where?

### What the objects say

- **The paper** is clean-room paper: lint-free, gridded, made for writing in a fab without shedding fibres. The Works used it for
  shift logs inside the clean halls. It is not sold anywhere. A sheet of it comes from inside the Core, or from a cache of Works
  stock somewhere in the Plinth.
- **The white leaves** are etiolated: grown without enough light, or under light of the wrong colour. A plant grown under a
  lithography safelight (amber, no blue) grows pale and long and never greens. The yellow under the welds is exactly that light.
- **The mushroom** is a species that needs warmth, damp and no light, and is not one the Mills mushroom growers keep. A seed saver
  identifies it (with a lens box or a good eye) as a **Works cellar cap**, a mushroom that grew in the old Works' water culverts in the
  boom years and was thought gone after Y57.
- **The handwriting**, analysed by an Archivist, is a child's letters made with an old person's care: someone who learned to write from
  a much older person and practises.

### Main version: Moth

**Moth** is fourteen and has shouldered: too wide for the Under now, but small for her age, small enough for the one place that is not
the Under and not the floor. Between the ceiling of 21 and the floor of 22 is the Core's **sub-fab**: the low service level every
clean room has below it, where the pumps, the exhaust ducting, the chemical lines and the cable trays run. On the Dark Floors it runs the
whole footprint of the Core and is shut off from 21 by a steel deck with a few founding-era access hatches. The Bulkheads crew found one
of those hatches loose four years ago. Moth was their Chalk. When she shouldered she did not leave the Under. She went up into the
sub-fab, where it is warm all year, where the safelight comes down through the grating from the clean hall above at night, and where
nobody has walked for forty-five years.

She lives there. She grows plants in the yellow. She trades HW-9 cells that fall into the sub-fab's sumps out through the Bulkheads.
She found the clean-room paper in a locker. She learned to write properly from someone who leaves her notes, on the same paper, in
the same grid, in a hand that is very old. She has never seen who.

**In play:**
- **Clip 0 as a slow friendship.** Seed for her; pale things back. Each exchange the player makes, she sends something more useful:
  Works cellar cap spawn (a culture item, 1x1, that grows in a dark box with no light at all: a new real-grade mushroom line the Crown
  will pay for, because it is a lost species); then a sheet of clean-room paper; then a requisition slip she has found in the sub-fab,
  half-typed, unfinished, addressed to Stores. Through her, the player learns that someone is typing them.
- **The Pale Garden line.** Plants grown under safelight are pale, tender and sweet. The Crown has a word for them, "blanched", and pays
  for blanched greens as a delicacy (forced chicory, white asparagus: the rich have always paid for things grown in the dark). A player
  who buys or salvages an amber safelight fitting (Litho Run on 17 still has some, painted blue) can fit it to a grow bed and grow
  **blanched** produce: a real-grade, Crown-only variant, more value per square, needing no extra water and less power than a full
  grow light. Moth is the reason anyone thought to try. The safelight fittings belong to the Company under Clause 11.
- **The grid paper as a clue currency.** Every sheet of clean-room paper in the game came from inside. Collating three of them (Clip 0,
  a Moth note, a requisition) shows the same paper lot number in the watermark grid.

**Alternative: the old hand.** The handwriting is very old after all. It is Wendell Saye (Tenant version), or one of the Eleven of 27,
asking for seeds because he has been eating Company rations for forty years and wants to grow something before he dies. Moth does not
exist; the white leaves are his.

**Alternative: two of them.** Both. The old hand teaches the girl to write. The girl brings him seeds. Neither has ever seen the other.
They leave notes on grid paper at a hatch between the sub-fab and the Core. The Pale Garden is the only thing in the Stack that two people
made together without either of them being paid. (The designer might keep this version for its own sake.)

**Alternative: no one.** The seeds are taken by the Glove Wall's mice; the pale things are left by a Rain Church acolyte who grows
mushrooms in a dark Font and thinks of it as a kindness to an anonymous child. The grid paper is from a Works stationery cupboard on
Twenty. A mystery that is only kindness from a stranger is still worth having.

## 9. What the Old Hands won't say

The Old Hands don't write things down (06a). They also don't say some things at all. These are their silences. Each can be broken
once, by the right person, at the right standing, usually late, usually by a dying Hand.

1. **"We trained them."** The robots in the Core move with Old Hands' hands. In Y36 and Y37, under Programme Daylight, the best line
   operators and clean-room techs were paid a bonus to wear motion harnesses on shift: every pick, place, wipe, inspection and glove
   change recorded, to teach the machines. Ruth Amadi, Clock 0412, was the best. Agnes Pirro (0103) ran the recordings. The arms in the
   Core that have worked every night for forty-five years work the way Ruth worked when she was thirty. The Old Hands were paid to teach
   their replacements, took the money, and were laid off by what they taught. It is the shame under the Pension Book. **In play:** at Kin
   with Ruth, she says one sentence: "If you ever see one of them working, look at the left hand. It does a little thing at the end of a
   wipe. That's mine." A drone camera frame (if the game has them) or a vent kid's description from the sub-fab confirms it.
2. **Who shut the north door.** The Lomax log says both stair doors were closed at 22:31. Lomax and Ostrowski held the south. Who closed
   the north? **Ivo Kastelic, Clock 2104**, building services, the man on the stair side of the north door, who had been told by phone
   by a voice he knew. He closed it with fifteen hundred people on the far side. He was an Old Hand from Y62 until he died in Y80 and never
   spoke about it at the Canteen. They all knew. The north door's name in the Old Hands' private speech is **Ivo's door**, and the Canteen
   does not hold its Wall Night vigil there.
3. **The two faces.** The two of the Eleven of 27 they will not name were **Ivo Kastelic's brother Teodor (0091)** and **Ruth Amadi's
   husband, Kwame Amadi (0094)**. Both went up to the Lantern with the others in Y38. Both are on no record. Ruth has not seen Kwame since.
   She has been receiving, every Founding Day, a box of Vey heart pills with no sender. Main version: he is alive on 102, ninety, and the
   pills are the only thing he is allowed to send. **Hook** of great weight, used at most once.
4. **The Rusk Dissent.** They wrote it, or helped her write it (01's alternative). They will neither confirm nor deny. Asked directly,
   Hollis Brandt says, "Agathe Rusk walked the bank. Who do you think walked it with her?"
5. **Dez's locker is not the only way in.** There is a door. On 26, at the north-east corner, a Works fire door with a Y40 weld plate that is
   painted, not welded: a skin of steel stencilled to look like the others, over a door that opens. Hollis Brandt fitted the frame in Y38.
   The Old Hands know. In Y58 one of them, **Matthias Kerr, Clock 4102**, went in, with a lamp, to see what was there. He came out after two
   hours, sat down at the Canteen, and never said one word about it until he died in Y66. After that, the Canteen decided: nobody goes in.
   Not because of danger. Because Kerr's face. **In play:** the door exists and the game never lets the player open it. A Hand on their
   deathbed may tell you where it is. That is all.
6. **The pension is being paid.** They know about Penhallow (see **7**). They know some of their own have taken the deal. They read the
   Pension Book aloud every Founding Day with the struck names in it and do not skip them.
7. **What the Foreman will do.** See **2**: they think it will one day seal something "as designed". They keep the Foreman fed and quiet
   (they fill requisitions too, the oldest of them, and always have) partly so that it never decides the tower needs protecting from its
   tenants. **Hook:** when the player's first requisition arrives, an Old Hand will know before the player tells them. "Stores, is it? Took
   your time."

## 10. The poison isn't history

### The question

Everyone in the Stack talks about the poison in the bay as something that happened: the Works pumped out the ground, the lagoon went in
Y57, the water is what the plant left. The Tide Folk, who live on the water, talk about it differently. The mussels on one stretch of the old
seawall, where the Race comes out, do not open. On some mornings there is a sheen on the water there. The fishers do not set lines in it.
Is the bay still being poisoned, now, every night?

### Main version: the Race and the dark chute

The Core runs. Running a clean room makes waste: spent solvents, etchants, metal-bearing rinse water, slurry from polishing. Schedule U was
written for a Works with a waste treatment plant on 6, inside the Waterhouse, which was flooded in Y57 and reduced to its membrane trains.
The Core's waste has nowhere to go but the routes it still knows:

- **The dark chute.** The chute run with no catch hatches and no Fall. Solid waste and spent filters go down it at night, in small sealed
  canisters, and pass the Bale Hall through a founding-era bypass that the Jaws were built not to see. They do not reach the Sump. They go into
  the Race.
- **The Race.** The founding-era cooling channel under floor 0, where the tidal turbines still turn. The Core's liquid waste goes down the old
  process drain into the Race and out with the ebb. Twice a day, on the surge, it leaves the Stack at the same place.

So the water is still being poisoned, at the rate of one lights-out clean room's waste, every ebb, from the bottom of the building, and the
mussels are a meter nobody reads.

**In play:**
- **Race mussels.** A Tide Folk client brings you a sack of mussels from the Race stretch and asks you to send them to someone who can test them.
  The Old Hands (3000s) can; the Archivists know a Vey lab tech who can, quietly. The result, a 1x1 **assay slip**, is a trace for this
  mystery and evidence the Tide Folk can use to refuse to sell to the tower on a given week (a **Tide Shut**: no Flats salvage on the board
  for a few days).
- **Canisters.** Very occasionally a dark-chute canister lodges in the Bale Hall's bypass and a bale picker pries it loose. It is a Works waste
  canister, sealed, labelled with a Line Nine waste code and this year's date. Holding one is hot (Bureau and HPS both want it); opening one
  is a bad idea (the game should say so plainly: a "toxic" state that poisons any vessel or food in the same container, which is a container
  rule, not proximity). Sold to Static, it becomes three nights of broadcast and a week of Company "environmental reassurance" posters.
- **The Flats remember.** A Tide Folk elder will tell you the sheen came back the night after the Last Shift in Y41, and has come back every
  night since, and that her grandmother called it "the Works breathing out".

**Alternative:** the Race sheen is old: residue leaching from the drowned Well Hall and the lagoon slurry in the plant's lowest floors, the
same poison as everywhere else. The Core is clean, or has its own closed recycling. The mussels are dying of history.

**Alternative:** the Company knows and sells it. Halden Civil holds the provincial discharge permit for the Race outfall as "residual cooling
water from heritage plant", renewed every five years. The permit is a public document, at the Sounding's Wire office, for 2 cr a page.
Nobody has ever paid the 2 cr. **Hook:** the player can.

## 11. Small mysteries

Loose stones. Each could be a single slip, an event, or nothing but a line of Talk.

- **The Whistle's false blasts.** The Works Whistle blew when it should not have twice: once in Y71, once in Y79 (08). Y71 is the
  Meter Strike, the year Freight One died. Y79 is Fan Room Three. Both times the Foreman recorded a founding-era system lost for good. The
  Whistle is the Foreman's mourning. **Hook:** if it blows again, which system just died?
- **The tin offerings.** Lunch tins left at the welds on 22 and 26 at noon by old Mills people who believe the Dark Floors are staffed by the
  Works' dead, gone by morning (08). "Vent kids. Probably vent kids." Main version: Moth and the Bulkheads collect them and eat the brick.
  Alternative: the tins are found, washed and stacked neatly, a year later, in the stores pit under the player's cage.
- **The child with perfect teeth.** A child on 16 drinks only rain, has since birth, and has perfect teeth (03). Her mother won't say where
  the rain comes from. Main version: Marrow's rain taps worked both ways; one landing tap on 16's corner stair was never welded because it
  was behind a Works locker bank in Y61. It still runs when it rains on the Crown. **In play:** a Rain Church contract to find "the Sixteen
  Tap" and keep its secret.
- **The Glove Wall moves.** The gloves on the Glove Wall "still move a little when the Lung's air comes through" (02). The third row's left glove
  moves more than the others. Behind it is the old Line Four shift-log slot, a founding-era mail chute to the Works offices. It is how Clip 0's
  seeds reach the sub-fab.
- **Old Two's eighth second.** The ghost stops last eight seconds. A Works shift change at a car door allowed eight seconds per worker: the
  Foreman is letting one person on. **Hook:** what happens if someone gets on?
- **The two certificates.** The Sounding's certified water level for 19.III.57 exists in two versions with the same signature (07). Main
  version: version A (the real figure) was sold to the Vance Inquiry by Rusk's request and replaced in the bundle with version B (higher,
  "unprecedented") supplied by Halden. Both are in the Sounding's own archive, because Meridian sells to everyone.
- **Bastion's brass plate.** "HALDEN WORKS, LINE TWO, Y3" (07). Line Two is where the No. 4 cell's chemistry was discovered by accident in Y3
  (09's alternative). If Bastion has Line Two's machines, Bastion may have the only machines in the bay that can make a No. 4, and nobody there
  knows what they are.
- **The Hulk's Plumb.** Moving faster this year (07). Halden Civil's demolition option, exercised pile by pile? Or the Race, carrying the Core's
  waste, scouring the Hulk's north piles on every ebb? A Hulk client asks you to deliver a Race water sample and a sack of Race mud to the
  Archivists. The answer tells the Hulkers whether to leave.
- **The trefoil.** Works keys (the trefoil pattern) lost design protection in Y81; nobody renewed it (09). The Company has filed to restore it.
  Until it does, any toolroom can cut keys to the Sorrel meter flaps, the flood door cabinets, the Works fire doors. Fenwick Adair (Clock 1880)
  at the Co-op's Crib has the blanks.
- **Clock 0001's other swipe.** Most nights 0001 clocks in at 02:00. Once a year, on 30.IX, Last Shift Night, it also clocks **out** at 22:00.
  The only night the Core's authorisation lapses. The hum that night is shorter.
- **The air line.** The billing line for air on every statement, at zero (01). In the Company version of the Foreman, the air readings are being
  prepared for re-billing. In the Upkeep version, the Foreman's air sums are the only true record of how the Mills' air has worsened since
  Y43, floor by floor. A copy would be the best evidence the cough exists (Vey says "Plinth lung" does not).
- **The cast-iron columns.** The bandstand's columns hold up a bar on Lantern Row (01). One has a founding-era time capsule in its base: the
  Common's opening-day box, Y0, with letters from the first tenants "to the people of the Stack in Y100". Lantern Row does not know.
- **The Founders' Room model.** Every Plinth floor painted grey. One is not: floor 17's slab on the model has a tiny brass square set into it at
  the position of the Line Four stores cage, and a pin-hole through it all the way up the model's core. A visitor on a Crown tour once noticed and
  asked. The guide did not know.
- **The well licences.** The licence register made the Tally possible (01). Halden Civil kept a second copy that recorded not just households but
  **well depths**. Every well in Wellside was shallow, as the Rusk Dissent said. The second copy is the arithmetic, and the Company kept it.
- **Skywater's First Rain labels.** Each First Rain bottle is labelled with its collection date. In Y57 the label printer at the Skywater Hall had
  already been delivered. The order date on the printer's invoice is Y56.
- **The H-series.** Joss Arkady's father heard "the H-series" (01). The H-cell is the Company-new cell sold today (09). The founding H-series was a
  high-density line scrapped in Y39. Two different things with the same name. Which one is the Core making? The Company named its new cell after
  the old one in Y44, which is the year a Works accountant asked why Line Nine was drawing materials.
- **The capsule that came back.** Once, a Tube capsule arrived in a player's lane spur with a Y43 air reading inside, addressed to "WORKS
  ENGINEERS, 71". It was misrouted. It is the only Foreman document anyone below 71 has held. **Hook:** where it should have gone, and who
  stamps the capsules RECEIVED.
- **Static's first voice.** Lorca Venn read names for twenty-three years and "someone else's voice" reads them now (06b). Nobody announced her death.
  The Old Hands say she is not dead; Penhallow came for her too, because a radio technician from Building Services (Clock 2391) is exactly who
  you need to read the Foreman's Bus.
- **Eleven dark days.** In the eleven days without power below 30 after Wall Night, the Core never flickered (01). It was on its own supply. Which
  means, for eleven days, a few thousand people in the dark Mills sat under a hall full of light and power, and nobody let them have any. There is a
  founding-era cross-tie between the Core's supply and the Mills Steps; an Old Hand could have closed it. Nobody did. **Hook:** because nobody
  knew, or because the order was not to?
- **The Seven Minutes, counted.** The Tally lists 800 people up the South Stair in the Seven Minutes. Marit Oyelaran, the wall sergeant who
  lived, says she counted them from the landing above, and it was 811, and the eleventh after eight hundred was Ostrowski.

## Part three: story seeds

Short seeds, usable as an event card, a single slip, or the first slip of a chain. Each is one to three sentences, with where it
lands in the loop. Numbered for reference; grouped loosely. Seeds marked **(M#)** feed a mystery from Part two.

### At the catch and the chute

1. **The ring in the peel.** A wedding ring in the East Chute's morning fall, engraved with two initials and a Y58 date. Someone on
   the Kitchen floors wants it back; someone else wants it never found. Two slips arrive the same day. *(A client choice; small
   standing swings on two floors.)*
2. **Voided on purpose.** For a month, the North Chute drops a series of objects slashed in exactly the same way, a single cut, as if
   by someone who wanted them to be easy to mend. Each has a scrap of paper inside. Read together, the scraps are a Crown servant's
   resignation letter, and a request. *(A chain; the Pantry.)*
3. **Paper Chute day.** The West Chute drops a Bureau clearance load: forty seized ledgers, all VOID-stamped. One is the order book of
   a Middle shop that sold Static airtime. Names, dates, sums. *(Hot item; Static, the Bureau and the shop's owner all bid.)*

## Part six: open questions for the designer

### The Dark Floors

1. Which truth do you want as the default: **Upkeep** (the Core makes the tower's own spares on a founding schedule, and the Company skims
   it), **the Contract** (it makes cells for the Company, plain greed), **the Tenant** (a person inside), **the Empty Room**, or **the House**
   (the Company doesn't know)? Or a hidden truth seed per run?
2. Should the player ever see inside, even by one drone's bay? This file says once, late, optional, and never on foot.
3. Should filling requisitions have visible world effects (fewer air days, fewer lift failures), or stay a private relationship with the
   Core that pays well and changes nothing anyone else sees?
4. Is the Core a client like any other (slips, pay, standing), or a thing outside the client system entirely (no standing, no rating, no
   ledger)? This file treats it as a client with an invisible issue record.

### The Foreman and the Arbiter

5. One founding-era mind running everything (the Foreman), or a set of unrelated rots? One mind is tidier for clues and riskier for tone
   (it edges toward an AI story).
6. Is the Arbiter a machine (the Grievance Box), a panel, or an old lawyer? This file uses the machine because it makes old forms into
   items. Is the "Y0 forms win" mechanic too exploitable, or exactly the right size if forms are scarce?
7. The Roll Call: is a yearly founding-era self-test a good event, and should it reveal one hidden feature on the player's founding-era
   machines for free?

### Meters

8. The Return column: keep it as a hidden debt the Company owes, or as the Alternative (the Company pays the Return to Holdings)? Both?
9. Should backruns be visible on the field's meter display (a tiny reverse tick) so a player can notice them without being told?
10. Is a whole-floor Return claim a Co-op ending path, or too big for the Arbiter system?

### Wall Night

11. Do you want the **who** to have faces (Edmund Varne in the margin, a switchboard and a head of security) or stay a pattern with no single
    culprit (the Esme Vollan and "no Options Paper" versions)?
12. Is LR-2 (the landward relief valve that let the lagoon into Wellside first) a keeper? It adds a decision but also a villain.
13. Which version of the stair doors does the game hold to: phone order (01's main), Ivo Kastelic at the north door, the Foreman hammering the
    latch?
14. How many burning documents should a single run let the player hold? This file assumes few, and each one a big choice.

### The roof and the Crown

15. Lantern cough: do the Haldens suffer from the same works, or is that too much like poetic justice for the tone?
16. Where does Moorhen fly: Vantage (rival buys founding parts), Bastion (convicts refurbish the Core's robots), or inward (raw materials)?
17. Is the first cell in the Lantern really still charged, and does that matter to anything the player can do?

### People

18. Penhallow's offer (the pension paid in full to those who vanish): is this the Crown ending's dark twin, a route the player can take, or
    only something that happens to others?
19. Kwame Amadi alive on 102: one scene, used once. Keep, or too much melodrama for "no heroes"?
20. Moth: one person, two people (Moth and the old hand), or nobody (a kindness from a stranger)? The "two who never meet" version is this
    file's favourite.
21. Should Old Hands' deaths be scripted (one a season, as 06a suggests) so that their silences can be broken in a known order?

### Calendar and pacing

22. Resolved: the Morning Fall is at 06:00, held overnight at Reclaim on 59.
23. The nine-day ninth-night cycle against the seven-day week: is a "long Seventh" every sixty-three days a good hidden rhythm, or one
    rhythm too many?
24. Should Wall Night, Resilience Day and Founding Day fall in the player's first season (so the history arrives early), or late (so the
    player has something at stake when they come)?
25. How many mysteries run in one playthrough? All eleven seeded thinly, or two or three chosen per run and the rest left as Talk?

### Consistency notes for the corpus (not questions of taste, just collisions found while writing)

    eat on 14, keep tools on 19); worth stating once.
