# 05: Law and order

> Speculative. Raw material to pan through. Plain text is the main version; **Alternative:** is another version of the
> same thing; **Hook:** is a story seed; **In play:** is how it surfaces in the game. Numbers in credits ("cr") are
> scaled to a working-assumption Mills day wage of about 30 cr and a rent of about 5 cr per square per week on floor 17;
> file 04 (economy) may rescale them, and the ratios matter more than the figures.

---

## 1. Where the law comes from

There is no city. There has been no city since the water came in. What the Stack has instead of law is **a lease**, and
everything that looks like law in the tower is a clause in it, a schedule attached to it, or a service the leaseholder
pays for.

### The Emergency Stewardship Order (Y57)

In the weeks after the Drowning, what was left of Calder's government sat in two rented rooms on floor 63 and called itself
the **Calder Recovery Authority**. It had no tax base (the taxpayers were underwater or on the Stack's stairs), no
police (the precinct house was in old Calder, under six metres of slurry), and no money. It had one asset: the legal
right to govern the land the Stack stood on. On the ninth day it signed the **Emergency Stewardship Order**, which
"delegated to the Halden Company, as sole remaining property-holder of standing, the maintenance of order, sanitation,
supply and public wellbeing within the Halden Residential Structure, for the duration of the emergency."

The Authority dissolved itself in Y59 for lack of funds. Nobody was left with the standing to declare the emergency over.
It is, legally, still the ninth day.

Everything Halden does as a government it does as a **steward**: not because it rules, but because someone has to, and
it was asked. Company documents never say "law". They say "the Occupancy Framework", "residential standards", "wellbeing
measures". A fine is a "standards charge". An arrest is "protective custody". An eviction is a "lease adjustment". The
gallows humour of the tower lives in that vocabulary, and people speak it fluently, the way prisoners learn a guard's
slang.

**Alternative:** the Order was never signed by the Authority at all. The signature page in the Company's copy is a
photocopy, the original "lost in the flood". The Archivists hold a draft with a different clause: stewardship "for a period
not exceeding twenty-four months". Whether that draft was ever the real one is the kind of question the Archivists live on
(see file 06a).

**Hook:** a woman in the Sump claims to be the granddaughter of the Authority's last clerk and to have the clerk's diary,
which says the Authority did not dissolve: it was voted out by a quorum that had been paid to stop coming. She wants
water for it, not money.

### The Occupancy Charter

The lease every tenant signs is the **Halden Occupancy Charter** (tenants just say "the Charter" or "my paper"). It runs
to 212 clauses and eleven schedules. Most people have read the first page, which states the floor, the squares, the rate,
the due day and the tenant's name, and signed the last, which says they have read the rest.

The important parts, the ones the Arbiter cites:

- **Clause 4 (Bylaws):** the tenant agrees to observe the Bylaws "as amended from time to time". The Company can amend the
  Bylaws by posting the amendment in the lift lobby of the tenant's floor. If the lift lobby notice board is full, the
  amendment is "deemed posted".
- **Clause 9 (Rate):** the per-square rate is "subject to periodic review". Review happens every Period (thirty days). It has gone down
  once, in Y72, by a fraction of a credit, on one floor, for one quarter, after which the Company cited it for eleven years
  as evidence that review "operates in both directions".
- **Clause 17 (Metering):** all water and power drawn on the leased squares is the tenant's, whoever draws it. If a
  neighbour taps your line, you pay.
- **Clause 23 (Waste):** the garbage chute serving the lease "conveys to the lessee such material as is discharged from
  above, without warranty". What arrives is yours, and so is the problem of it.
- **Clause 31 (Inspection):** the Company and its "delegated licensors" may enter the lease "at any reasonable hour".
  Schedule F defines "reasonable hour" as "an hour".
- **Clause 41 (Arbitration):** every dispute arising "from or in connection with occupancy" goes to the Arbiter, and the
  tenant waives "all other forums, existing or future". This is the clause everyone has heard of. "Forty-one" is slang for
  having no recourse: *you can forty-one it if you like*.
- **Clause 58 (Household):** everyone sleeping on the lease must be registered as a Registered Occupant. Each costs a
  registration fee and adds to the water allocation baseline.
- **Clause 77 (Continuation):** on the tenant's death, the Charter's debts pass to the Registered Occupants, jointly.
- **Clause 112 (Subletting):** subletting is permitted with a sublet licence, which is how the sublet barons exist (file 06b).
- **Clause 200 (Severability):** if any clause is found unenforceable "by any authority", the rest stand. No authority has
  ever been found.

**In play:** the Charter is a physical item in the player's starting kit: a 2x3 sheaf, bent at the corner. It does nothing
on the field except take space, and it cannot be sold or binned ("Required on the lease at all times. Bylaw 6."). It is
the first lesson in the game's tone. Later, a lease upgrade gives you a **bound Charter** (1x2, leather-backed), which is the
same document, smaller, and costs 40 cr. Quiet joke: on a bigger lease, the paperwork is literally smaller.

**In play:** amendments arrive as **notices** pinned to the lobby board, which the player sees at the start of a day. "From
the first of next week, Bylaw 61 is amended: drones launched from leases below floor 30 shall display a lane transponder
whether or not they use a lane." Each amendment is a rule change for a few in-game weeks: a new fee, a newly banned
item, a new inspection type. This gives the designer a cheap way to vary seasons of play with lore attached.

### Standing: who is a person

The Charter makes four kinds of people, and the Arbiter recognises them in this order:

1. **Leaseholders**: named on a Charter. Full standing. Can petition the Arbiter, can subscribe to HPS, can hold licences.
2. **Registered Occupants**: named on someone else's Charter. Can be petitioned *against*, can petition only "through the
   Leaseholder".
3. **Guests**: present on a lease with the leaseholder's permission, for no more than "one rest period". No standing.
   Their conduct is the leaseholder's liability.
4. **Non-parties**: everyone else. People in hot-bunk beds without registration, Flats people who come up the outer stair,
   vent kids, unregistered children. In Company paper they are "persons not party to the Framework". In speech they are
   **nobodies**, and it is not an insult; it is a status. A nobody cannot be wronged, legally. A nobody can still be fined:
   the fine attaches to the lease where they were found.

**In play:** hirelings (if they come back from the parked ideas) are registered or not. A registered helper costs a
registration fee and raises your water baseline; an unregistered one is free, but a floor warden can report them and the
fine lands on your lease. A real, recurring choice that says something about the world.

## 2. Halden Protective Services

HPS is not police. It says so in its own literature: *Halden Protective Services protects. It does not police. Policing
implies a public, and a public implies a cost no one has agreed to bear.* HPS protects **subscribers and Company
property**. Everything else it does is a courtesy, and courtesies are billed.

### The tiers

HPS is sold by subscription, attached to a lease, renewed weekly with the rent. The tier sets a **target response window**:
the time HPS aims to take from "acceptance of the call" (not from the call) to "attendance" (not to help).

| Tier | Typical band | Weekly fee | Target window | Callout fee | What it includes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Sovereign** | Crown | by negotiation (from about 2,000 cr) | "continuous" | none | officers stationed on the floor; escort on request; incident discretion |
| **Assured** | Terraces | 90 cr | 10 minutes | none | home attendance; recovery service for stolen goods; legal liaison with the Arbiter |
| **Standard** | Middle | 20 cr | 45 minutes | 5 cr | attendance; incident report; recovery "where practicable" |
| **Essential** | Mills | 6 cr | 4 hours | 15 cr | attendance; incident report |
| **Community Cover** | Sump, poorer Mills | 1 cr per registered head, pooled by floor | "as resources allow" | 25 cr, payable by the floor | entry to the Sump in strength when the floor's pool is paid up |

Non-subscribers can call HPS too. HPS will attend if the incident "involves Company property", which includes the walls,
the meters, the lifts, the lanes, the chutes and the Company's licensed brands. So a shop with no subscription that is
robbed of its stock gets no visit; the same shop, if the robbers dented the meter on the way out, gets a visit about the
meter, and the tenant is charged for the dent.

The **incident report** is the real product of Essential cover. It is a printed slip with an incident number, and it is
the only thing the insurance syndicate (file 06b) will look at before declining a claim. Without an incident number, the
syndicate declines on the grounds that no incident occurred.

**Alternative:** the tiers are named for colours rather than words, after the armbands HPS officers wear on each type of
detail: White (Crown), Blue (Terraces), Grey (Middle), Brown (Mills), and no colour for Community, called **Bare** in the
Sump. "Brown cover" is then shorthand for nearly nothing.

**Hook:** the target windows are measured from "acceptance", and HPS dispatch in the Mills accepts calls in batches at the
top of every hour. A former dispatcher, now selling noodles on floor 14, can explain why a stabbing at 13:01 is attended at
18:00 and the books still show it was answered inside four hours. She has the dispatch logs on a cell she will not part with.

### Who they are

HPS officers are called **Protectives**, or **subs** by everyone else (from "subscription"; "the subs are on twelve" means
there is HPS activity on floor 12). They are mostly recruited from the Middle: sons and daughters of skilled workers who
did not get a licensed trade, who live in HPS-subsidised quarters on floors 53 to 55 and pay no rent while they serve. Losing
the job means losing the flat. This is the whole of HPS discipline policy.

Ranks, from the bottom: **Attendant** (the one who comes), **Protective** (the one who writes the report), **Floor
Lead** (one per band post), **Post Captain**, and the **Director of Protective Services**, who lives on 91 and has never
been seen below 60.

Posts, the Company's word for stations:

- **Post Nineteen** in the Mills, a cage-fronted room at the floor 19 lift lobby; four Attendants on a day shift, one on
  nights, and a desk that takes Essential renewals. It is the only HPS post in the Mills band.
- **Post Forty-Four** in the Middle, much bigger, with interview rooms.
- **Station Sixty-One**, headquarters, with dispatch, records and **the Pen**.
- **The Crown Detail**: Sovereign officers are not stationed at posts. They live with the families they guard.
- There is no post in the Sump. There used to be one on floor 8; the room is now a bar called **the Old Post**, and the cage
  front is still there, used to keep drunks from the bottles.

Kit: a grey coverall with a band-colour armband; a **wand** (a short stun baton, cell-powered, which runs flat often
because HPS buys the cheapest cells); a **body lens** whose footage is Company property and is sold, in compilations, to
the Pleasure Pier as entertainment (file 07); a **tag gun** that marks goods with an invisible dye for "recovery"; a
**reader** for lane transponders and licence plates; and a **pad of standards charge forms**, which they are paid a
small commission to fill.

### Custody

HPS does not arrest. It takes people into **protective custody**, for their protection, and the protected person pays for it.

Custody is held in **the Pen** on floor 61: a hall of cells measured in squares, as everything in the Stack is. The
detainee is charged **custody rental** at the floor 61 per-square rate, which is a Terraces rate, for the squares of their
cell (a standard cell is 2x3: six squares), plus a "board charge" for Vitabrick and water. A night in the Pen costs about
what a Mills worker earns in a week. The bill goes to the detainee's lease, or, for nobodies, to whoever comes to collect
them, who must pay it before release.

People who cannot pay are released anyway after the "custody period" (three days) with the debt on their name, and the
debt is sold to a collector (§6).

**In play:** if hirelings exist, a hireling can be taken into custody: they vanish from your roster for a set number of
hours and come back with a custody bill pinned to them, a slip item you must pay at the board. Or not: an unpaid custody
slip becomes a debt that drops your credit rating a little every day until settled. The bill shows the squares: "Cell
61-C-14, 6 sq. at 4.10 = 24.60 cr. Board: 3.00 cr."

### Recovery

What HPS recovers, it holds. Recovered goods are kept in the **Recovery Store** at Station Sixty-One for thirty days, and
can be reclaimed with proof of ownership (a receipt, a provenance slip, a tag-gun mark) and a **storage charge** per square
per day. After thirty days, unreclaimed goods are "retired" through the Company's disposal channel, which means they are
sold at the company store, or, if they are branded goods, stamped VOID and sent down the chute.

Assured subscribers get recovery as part of the service. Standard gets it "where practicable". Essential does not get it.
So a Mills shop that is robbed sees its own stock, a month later, on the company store's discount shelf.

**In play:** a theft event on the player's field (rare, mid-game: "Someone was in the shop overnight") removes one or two
items. If the player has Standard cover or better, there is a chance a **Recovery Notice** slip arrives a few days later
naming the item and the storage charge to date, which climbs daily. Pick it up early, cheap; or let it go. The player might
later see it as a reward in a company-store contract. With Essential cover, all you get is an incident number, which is an
item, which the insurance syndicate will decline.

### Lease enforcement

HPS also carries out **lease adjustments**: evictions, partial or full. A **partial adjustment** removes rows or columns of
squares from a lease in arrears; HPS Attendants come with a **lease line**, a roll of yellow-and-black tape and a stapler, and
mark the new boundary. What is beyond the line becomes "material on unleased squares" and is removed to the Recovery Store,
where storage is charged.

**In play:** this is the field shrinking. If the player falls behind on rent past a certain point (§6), the game takes a row
of their field away, starting at the edge. Whatever stood on the lost row goes to the Recovery Store and can be bought back.
The tape stays on the floor as a visible border, so the player sees the lease they used to have. It is the clearest single
picture of "space is what you pay for" the game could have.

## 3. Licensing inspectors

A licence in the Stack is not permission to do something. It is permission to do something **in the Company's name**, which
makes the same act safe. The licence is what turns a bootlegger into a certified safe provider, a hustler into a licensed
trader, a grower into a registered producer. The inspectors are the people who check that the name is on things.

There are three inspectorates, and only one belongs to Halden directly. The other two are **delegations**: the Company has
delegated the power to inspect to the licensed brands that hold the market. This is the most important fact about
inspection in the tower. **The food inspector works for the food company.**

### The Water Wellbeing Office

Water is Halden's own. The licence is issued by the **Water Wellbeing Office** (WWO), on floor 46, and enforced by its
**Wellbeing Inspectors**. Slang: **tasters**, because the old ones used to taste a sample before they had reagents, and
because they still take a sip, for show, from anything they have just certified.

The water law in one line: *no water may be treated, purified, distilled, condensed, collected or recovered for consumption
within the Structure except by a Licensed Treatment Provider or under a Class D Water Licence.* The definitions
section defines "consumption" to include drinking, cooking, washing food, watering plants that will be eaten, and "any use
which brings water into contact with a person". Which is all uses.

The licence:

- **Application fee:** 150 cr, non-refundable, with a **site survey** (the inspector visits and measures the squares the
  apparatus occupies).
- **Certificate fee:** 40 cr a week per apparatus.
- **Stewardship levy:** a fee per litre produced, read off a sealed **output meter** the WWO fits to the apparatus at the
  owner's cost. The levy is set so that licensed water cannot be sold below Halden Pure's price in the same band.
- **The blue plate:** an enamelled plate riveted to the apparatus, with a number. "Blue-plated" is the slang for legal water.
  A blue-plated still and an unplated one can be the same still. They often are: the cheapest route to a licence is to keep
  your bootleg kit and pay.

An inspection of a licensed provider takes about an hour. The taster checks the plate against the register, reads the
output meter, checks its seal (a lead seal with a WWO stamp), draws a sample into a **reagent card** (a strip that changes
colour for salt, solvents and metals), signs the provider's **water log**, and charges the visit fee.

An inspection of an unlicensed apparatus is shorter. The taster photographs it, writes a standards charge, and fits a red
**Wellbeing Hold** tag. HPS Attendants come later to remove the apparatus to the Recovery Store, where it is held as
evidence for thirty days and then sold. Many stills in the tower have been confiscated, sold at the company store,
re-confiscated, and resold, three or four times. There is a still on floor 13 the tasters have seized five times. Each
time, the same man has bought it back.

The tasters have a phrase for the stinkers they cannot catch: **a dry floor**. A floor where every tenant draws a little
Halden Pure and nobody seems to drink more than that is a floor where somebody is making water. A dry floor gets a
**baseline review**: every lease's draw compared to its registered heads. A lease that draws too little for its headcount
is presumed to be supplementing, and gets inspected.

**In play:** the meter itself becomes evidence. If the player makes their own water (an air-well, a still, a filter rig),
their tap draw drops, and the game can track it. Below a threshold relative to the lease's registered heads and machine
water use, the chance of a **baseline review inspection** rises. So the player is pushed to keep drawing some licensed water
even when they don't need it: paying the tower as camouflage. That is a beautiful, nasty, mechanical consequence of
canon, and it needs no hidden numbers: show the "expected draw" line on the meter.

**In play:** a **Class D Water Licence** is something the player can buy later: a blue plate item that is applied
to one water machine (packed into its slot or fitted like an upgrade). The plated machine produces **licensed water**, a
different item from **unlicensed water** though chemically identical, which the upper contracts accept. It also gets an
output meter, which bills the levy every hour it runs. Choose: free water you can only sell down and must hide, or taxed
water you can sell anywhere.

### The Food Standards Delegation

Food inspection is delegated to **Orrin Provisions** under the **Food Standards Delegation** (Y61). The delegation gives
Orrin's inspectors the Company's power to enter and inspect "any premises where food is grown, bred, processed, stored or
offered", and to "retire" food that does not meet the standards. Orrin writes the standards. Orrin makes Vitabrick.

Orrin inspectors wear yellow oilskin coats with the Orrin wheat-sheaf on the back, and are called **yellowcoats** or
**graders**. They grade: every food product offered for sale in the Stack must carry an Orrin **grade stamp** or be sold
"ungraded", and ungraded food may only be sold to "persons of the same lease". That makes every food sale between
neighbours either an Orrin-stamped sale (with a stamp fee to Orrin) or a crime.

The standards that matter:

- **Protein**: insect protein for human consumption must come from **registered stock** (insect lines licensed from Orrin,
  each bin with a registration tag) and be processed on **registered presses**. Unregistered protein is "unassured protein",
  and Orrin may retire it on sight. Registered producers sell their blocks to Orrin at Orrin's buying price, and Orrin
  stamps them Vitabrick. Unregistered blocks sold under no name are **pressings**, sold by the stack in the Mills and the
  Sump, cheaper than Vitabrick and, everyone agrees, better.
- **Produce**: fresh produce must come from **certified seed**: seed sold by Greenhold under licence (file 07), each packet
  with a lot number. Produce grown from saved seed is "of uncertified provenance". It is not illegal to eat. It is illegal
  to sell above floor 30, and Orrin may "retire" it in the Mills if it is offered for sale. This is the legal ground the
  seed savers (file 06b) stand on and are kicked off.
- **Grade**: anything sold as "real" (grown, not synthetic) must carry an Orrin real-grade stamp. A real tomato without the
  stamp is, in law, an "unverified tomato", and selling it as real is grade fraud.

What a grader does on a visit: checks every bin's registration tag, counts presses, checks seed packets against grow beds,
tests a sample of blocks with a **marker swab** (Orrin stock carries a genetic marker; unregistered insects do not, until they
interbreed, which is a whole legal problem, see §14), stamps what passes, and retires what doesn't. Retired food goes into a
yellow **retirement sack** and down the chute. Graders have no power to fine. They report to the WWO and HPS, who do.

**In play:** the player's insect bin can be **registered** (a tag, a weekly registration fee, Orrin's buying price for the
output) or not. Registered blocks are **Vitabrick (licensed)**, accepted by all contracts and by the company store.
Unregistered blocks are **pressings**, accepted by Mills and Sump contracts at a better price than Vitabrick fetches there,
because people prefer them. A grader visit can retire unregistered stock found in open containers.

**In play:** saved seed versus certified seed is a production choice: certified seed costs more and its packets carry a lot
number item; saved seed is free (you keep some fruit back) but its produce is **uncertified**, and Terraces and Crown
contracts refuse it. Middle contracts accept it at a discount. Some Crown contracts, ironically, ask for "heirloom" produce,
which is saved-seed produce with a seed-saver provenance card and a penthouse fixer in between (file 06b).

### Delegated brand licensing

The third inspectorate is brand protection, which is not called inspection and is not done by inspectors. It gets its own
section (§4).

### What licences cost, side by side

| Licence | Issuer | Up front | Recurring | What it buys |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Class D Water Licence (water) | WWO | 600 cr + inspection | 150 cr/month + 0.30 per litre | Certified Safe Provider plaque (the "blue plate"); sell water up; no seizure |
| Producer Registration (insects) | Orrin | 60 cr per bin | 8 cr/week per bin | registered stock; Vitabrick sale to Orrin; no retirement |
| Grower Certification (produce) | Orrin with Greenhold | 30 cr | certified seed purchases | real-grade stamp; sell produce up |
| Trader's Licence (selling to other leases) | Lease Office | 80 cr | 10 cr/week | a counter that faces the corridor; legal shelf sales |
| Lane Permit (drones) | Lane Authority | by band | per flight fee | licensed lanes; no seizure in scans |
| Sublet Licence | Lease Office | 200 cr | 15% of sublet income | legal hot-bunking |
| Reclamation Licence (restored branded goods) | Brand Integrity Bureau | 500 cr | per-item reclamation fee | sell restored VOID goods as Certified Refurbished |
| Repair Licence (founding-era equipment) | Halden Facilities | 120 cr | annual exam | legally service meters, lifts, Company plant |

**In play:** a **licence wall**. Licences are items: plates, tags, framed certificates, each a small object that must sit
somewhere on the field (a 1x1 plate on a machine, a 2x1 framed certificate hung on a wall container). They take space,
the thing you pay rent for. A shop with every licence has a wall of frames. It's the most legal and least spacious shop.
The licences themselves are the visible cost.

## 4. Brand protection

### The Retirement Ordinance

The **Ordinance on the Retirement of Branded Goods** (Y63, amended many times since) is the law that makes the rich
destroy what they throw away. It says that branded goods leaving a lease as waste must be **retired**: rendered "incapable
of re-entering commerce in a condition likely to mislead a purchaser as to their origin, quality or authorisation". In
practice the brands issue **retirement instructions** for each product line, printed on the packaging in small type:

- **Vey** (medicine): blister packs punctured, ampoules snapped, bottles uncapped and the tablets crushed in the bottle.
  Unopened and in date or not, the instruction is the same. Vey sells a **retirement press** to Terraces clinics for this.
- **Orrin** (food): packaging slit; whole items "denatured" by a blue dye spray from a Company-issued **denaturing can**.
  The dye is harmless. Everyone knows it is harmless. It still makes the food "denatured", and denatured food is not food in
  law; it is feedstock, which is why Mills insect ranchers can legally take it (as feedstock) and illegally eat it (as food).
- **Skywater** (bottled rain): bottles crushed flat and cap seals broken.
- **Halden Pure**: empty canisters returned for deposit; full ones never thrown away, because nobody above throws away
  water; if they did it would be "release of treated water outside the metering framework".
- **Durable goods** (clothes, furniture, tools, appliances from Crown and Terraces suppliers): slashed, snapped, or
  stamped **VOID** with an ink stamp or a hot iron. The VOID mark is required on anything over a certain value that is not
  otherwise destroyed.

The Ordinance makes it an offence to sell, offer, or "possess with intent to sell" any retired branded good that has been
**restored**: patched, resealed, re-dyed, repaired, or had its VOID mark removed. It is not an offence to possess restored
goods for your own use. So the whole Mills wears restored clothes and sleeps on restored mattresses, and every one of them is
legal until someone offers to sell one.

### The Brand Integrity Bureau

The Bureau is a joint office funded by the licensed brands, with a Company delegation identical in form to Orrin's food
delegation. It occupies most of floor 66. Its agents are **Brand Integrity Agents**, called **stampers** (because they carry
a VOID stamp and use it on anything they doubt) or **bibs** (from the Bureau's initials).

Stampers are not paid a salary. They are paid a **recovery commission**: a percentage of the retail value of every
restored or counterfeit item they recover, valued at the brand's own list price. This is the engine of everything they do.
A restored Vey blister pack (worth 2 cr in the Mills) recovered on 17 is valued at 30 cr, its Terraces list price, and the
agent's 20% is 6 cr. A good stamper clears more in a week than a Post Captain.

What a stamper carries:

- a **UV torch**: brands print invisible destruction marks on packaging that glow after retirement, so resealed packets
  show their history under the light;
- a **microdot reader** for serial dots on durable goods, which tell which lease bought the item new, so the agent can look up
  whether it was "retired" and when;
- a **VOID stamp** and an ink pad, plus a hot-iron VOID brand for hard goods;
- **recovery sacks** with tamper seals and a numbered tag;
- and a **schedule of list prices**, which is the agent's bible.

Stampers work alone or in pairs, rarely with HPS. They like the Middle best (people there buy restored goods in quantity and
have something to lose), and the Mills second (where the restorers are). They don't go into the Sump, where restored goods
are the economy, because their commission does not pay for what happens to them there.

**Hook:** a stamper named **Corwin Ashe**, known on 14 to 20 as "Fair Ashe", has a reputation for leaving a shop alone if it
hands him one item a week, his choice, from the shelf, which he then "recovers" and books. Shops call this **feeding Ashe**.
His recovery numbers are steady and the Bureau loves him. Rumour says he resells what he recovers to a penthouse fixer, who
sells it in the Crown as "vintage". Which means the same voided coat has been sold up the tower twice and down the chute
once.

### Counterfeits

A counterfeit is not a restored good; it's a fake one. The Mills and the Sump make them, mostly of things whose brand is a
tax on a cheap object:

- **Vitabrik**: pressings with a hand-cut stamp that copies the Vitabrick wheat-sheaf, close enough at a glance. Sold in
  the Middle, where people want the brand and cannot afford it.
- **Skywatter**: tap water, filtered, in refilled Skywater bottles with a new cap seal. Middle bars buy it by the crate.
- **Vey-pattern** tablets: pressed from crushed retired Vey tablets mixed with chalk and sugar, so they contain some of the
  real drug in unknown amounts. The stampers treat this as the gravest brand offence; the Rain Church treats it as charity.
- **Halden Pure** canisters refilled with unlicensed water: the one counterfeit the Company prosecutes itself, through HPS,
  because it touches the water monopoly.

Penalties for counterfeiting are higher than for restoration, and they include the brand's **dilution damages**, a sum the
brand calculates for "harm to the brand's standing in the market", which is always larger than the counterfeiter's assets
and becomes a debt.

**In play:** item legal status. Every item can carry one of a small set of status marks (a corner icon next to grade):

- **licensed**: carries its brand's mark or a licence; goes anywhere;
- **grey**: no papers but nothing wrong with it (pressings, uncertified produce, unlicensed water); fine below 30, refused above;
- **void**: stamped or slashed; worthless as goods, useful as feedstock or as parts;
- **restored**: void that has been repaired; can be used, can be sold only below 30 or to particular clients;
- **counterfeit**: dressed as licensed; sells as licensed until someone checks.

Status is the hidden-but-visible information the designer wants: you always know what your items are. What you don't know
is whether today's inspector will look. Contracts say which statuses they accept, and the drone route (§15) decides whether
anyone checks on the way.

**In play:** the restoration chain, with the law at every step. A slashed coat comes down the chute (void). On the
workbench, a patch kit makes it restored. Sold on your shelf in the Mills, a restored coat is fine. Packed into a drone for
a Middle client, it rides the risk of a lane scan. With a **Reclamation Licence** (500 cr) and a per-item **reclamation fee**
(20% of list), it becomes **Certified Refurbished**, a licensed item, which goes anywhere, at a margin so thin it only pays
on expensive goods. The brands invented the licence after it became clear they could not stop restoration; they decided to
tax it instead.

## 5. Floor wardens

Every floor below 60 has a **floor warden**. Above 60 the job is done by concierge staff, who are the same thing in a
better uniform.

A warden is a tenant of the floor, appointed by the **Lease Office** for a term of a year, renewable without limit. The
warden is paid three ways:

1. a **rent rebate** of 30% on their own lease;
2. a **report fee** for every "occupancy irregularity" they file that results in a standards charge (a small percentage of
   the charge);
3. a **collection fee** for collecting certain small Company charges in person: registration fees, lobby-board posting
   fees, chute-clearance charges.

The warden's marks of office are a **green armband**, a **Warden's Book** (a ledger of who lives where, who sleeps where,
who has been reported for what), a **key to the lobby board**, and a **whistle**, which they are not supposed to use and
all do.

What a warden reports, from the Lease Office's list of **occupancy irregularities**: unregistered sleepers, unlicensed
trade, sublet without licence, alterations to the lease (shelving bolted to walls, partitions), "excessive" cooking smoke,
apparatus "suggestive of water treatment", "unusual draw patterns" on a neighbour's meter, livestock (anything that is not
registered insect stock), and "assemblies" (more than eight non-household persons on one lease).

Most wardens report less than they could. A warden who reports everything is a warden who gets no help when their own
pipes burst, finds their door glued shut, and discovers that the vent kids have been sleeping in their crawlspace for a
month and filed nothing about it because nobody told them. The art of being a warden is to report enough to keep the
appointment and not enough to be hated. Floors talk about a good warden the way people elsewhere talk about weather.

### Floor 17's warden

**Hester Moyle**, sixty-one, warden of 17 for nineteen years. Former line inspector at the Works (cells, quality control),
laid off in the last round of the automation, at sixteen, and, she says, "never stopped inspecting things". Lives in a two-row lease by the lift
lobby with her brother **Aldo**, who doesn't speak and fixes clocks. She files about four reports a month, almost all against
the same sublet baron, **Gus Tanaka-Breen**, whose hot-bunk house at the far end of 17 sleeps twenty in eight squares. She
has never reported a still. She has, twice, knocked on a door at seven in the morning and said "Tasters are on fifteen,"
and walked on.

Her price is not money. She takes **clocks**: broken ones, from the chute, for Aldo. A shop that sends her a broken clock now
and then finds its irregularities stay in the Warden's Book and never reach the Lease Office.

**In play:** Hester is a client. Her contracts are small and strange ("a spring from a wind-up clock, any condition", "a
watch crystal, 1x1") and pay little, but each one fulfilled lowers the chance that the next inspection on 17 is triggered
by a warden report. The designer can show this plainly: a **Warden's regard** pip next to the lobby board, which goes up
when you do her jobs and down when you ignore her for too long. No hidden number.

**Alternative:** the warden is new. Hester died last winter, and her replacement is **Pim Okafor**, twenty-three, keen,
from a Middle family that lost their lease, who sees the post as the way back up and files everything. A Pim floor is a
hard floor. Pim can be "brought round", slowly, the way any warden is, but not with clocks. Pim wants **good reports**
of his own floor to send up: he will trade a report against you for a report about someone else.

**Hook:** Aldo doesn't fix clocks. He reads them. Founding-era clocks in the tower are synchronised by a signal from
somewhere, and when they're opened up, the backs carry the serial of the floor's **sync node**. Aldo has a map, in pencil, of
every node he has found, and three of them are on floors 22 to 26: the Dark Floors (file 14).

### Warden reports as a system

Each floor's warden files a **report** on a form with three carbons: white to the Lease Office, pink to HPS, yellow kept in
the Book. A report triggers one of: a **letter of concern** (no penalty, but it goes on the lease's file), an **inspection** by
the appropriate office within a week, or, for unregistered occupancy, an **occupancy count**: an HPS Attendant walks in at an
unannounced hour and counts sleepers.

The Lease Office pays a bonus to wardens whose floors show a "healthy irregularity rate". Too few reports is suspicious.
Every warden in the tower knows the number. On 17 it is four a month.

**In play:** a floor-wide event, the **quota week**: near the end of a month, the warden is short of reports. Something has to
give. The player may be offered, by the warden, a choice: be reported for something small (a letter of concern, nothing
more) as a favour, or let her report the neighbour. The neighbour is a client of yours.

## 6. Debt and the people who collect it

Halden does not collect its own debts. Collecting is a cost; selling a debt is income. Every debt owed to the Company that
is more than fourteen days late is **assigned**: sold in bundles to a recovery firm at a fraction of face value. The firm then
owns the debt in full, plus a **recovery charge** (set by Schedule K of the Charter at 25% of the debt, plus "reasonable
costs"), and recovers it however the Charter lets it.

### The arrears ladder

What happens when rent is missed, step by step, from the Lease Office's own procedure (which is posted in every lobby, as
the Charter requires, in a frame that is screwed shut):

| Day | Step | What the tenant sees |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Rent due | the rent slip, as every week |
| 1 | **Arrears Notice** | red slip; 10% late fee; tap to a trickle, socket to half power; Standing -60 |
| 7 | **Assignment** | the debt is sold; a collector's card arrives instead of the Company's; recovery charge added |
| 7+ | **Recovery visit** | the collector tags goods for seizure (yellow tags), with a deadline |
| 8 | **Lockout** | mesh shutter on the frontage (Tolliver Shutter & Seal) |
| 10 | **Lease adjustment order** | the Arbiter (on the papers, no hearing) orders rows removed; HPS tapes the new line |
| 15 | **Termination (forfeiture)** | the Charter ends; everything on the lease goes to the Recovery Store; the debt remains |

The debt remains. That is the line people quote. Termination does not end the debt: the debt follows the person, and under
Clause 77 their Registered Occupants, and it is carried on the **arrears register**, which every lease office in every band
checks before signing a new Charter. There is no bankruptcy in the Stack. The concept was a function of courts.

**In play:** this table is the rent pressure in the game, made procedural. Each step is a visible event with an item or a
change: a pink card (1x1, can be binned, but the 2% stays), a red card, the meter's flow slider dropping (machines that draw
water run slower), a collector arriving, the field losing a row. Every step is recoverable by paying, and every step costs
more. The player always knows exactly where they are on the ladder.

### The firms

- **Brisk & Lowe Recoveries**, floor 48. The biggest buyer of Mills and Middle rent debt. Office style: polite, printed,
  relentless. Their cards are cream with a navy border and say *We are here to help you resolve this.* Their collectors work in
  pairs, one who talks and one who tags. Brisk & Lowe pay the Company about 35 cr for every 100 cr of debt they buy.
- **Nettlefold Settlement**, floor 70. Buys Terraces debt: lapsed private-clinic bills, St. Ober's Spire referral debts,
  failed shop loans. Nettlefold does not visit. Nettlefold writes to employers and has wages paid to it directly.
- **The Varga Books**, floor 6. Buys Sump and lower Mills debt from Brisk & Lowe, at about 8 cr per 100, when Brisk & Lowe give up.
  The Varga Books are a family, the **Varga** family, three generations of collectors, who keep their books on the walls of a dry
  room on 6 in chalk. They're not a gang. They have arrangements with all the gangs (§11). They collect in labour as often
  as in goods: a Varga debt can be paid off in hours on the bilge pumps, in dives, in hauling.

### The yellow tag

A collector cannot take goods on the first visit. The Charter requires a **seizure notice**: the collector walks the lease,
picks items to the value of the debt plus charges, and fixes a **yellow tag** to each, stating the debt and a deadline (usually
48 hours). If the debt is paid by the deadline, the tags come off. If not, the collector returns with HPS and takes the
tagged items. Removing a yellow tag, moving a tagged item off the lease, or selling it is **frustration of recovery**, which
adds a charge and a credit mark.

Collectors choose what to tag by **value per square**, because they have to carry it out: small, dense, valuable goods first.
Cells, founding-era parts, drone frames, sealed medicine, a working machine if it is small enough. Collectors never tag
protein blocks.

People in the Mills call a tagged lease **lemoned**. "They've lemoned the noodle stall."

**In play:** this is a packing puzzle by inversion. A collector visit tags the items with the highest value per square on the
field. The player sees which items are tagged and has a deadline in hours. The options are all the game's existing verbs:
pay the debt (credits); sell other goods fast to pay it (the shelves, the counter, a quick contract); or accept the loss.
Moving a tagged item is allowed by the engine but flagged, and costs a credit mark if the collector notices on return
(certain if it was in the open, unlikely if it went into a drone that is out of the building). The designer can decide
whether "launch it away in a drone" is a legal grey move, a crime, or the beginning of a story.

### Work-off and the boat

When a debtor has nothing left to take, the debt can be converted to a **work-off contract**: the debtor signs to labour for
the debt's buyer at a set daily rate, with board deducted. Brisk & Lowe sell their work-off contracts to labour brokers. The
Varga Books use their own directly.

The worst work-off contracts are held under the **Labour Transfer Agreement** between Halden and **Bastion**, the prison tower
(file 07). Debtors who "elect" transfer (the election is a box on the work-off contract, pre-ticked) are shipped to Bastion,
where the prisoners make cells and chips, the things Halden Works used to make, at a day rate that clears a Mills rent debt
in roughly four years. People call this **taking the boat**. The boat goes once a week, on Thursdays, from the floor 9 dock
in the Sump. Families stand on the outer stair and watch it go.

**Hook:** some come back. Fewer than go, but some, after their four years, on the same boat. They bring Bastion tricks for
making cells, and Bastion scars, and stories about the line they worked on, which used Halden Works machines. One of them
swears the machines at Bastion were shipped there **after** the Drowning, from the Works' upper halls, while the Company was
telling everyone the plant was destroyed.

## 7. The Arbiter

The Charter's Clause 41 sends every dispute to **the Office of the Arbiter**. There is no other forum. Halden calls the
Arbiter "impartial", which it is in the sense that it is equally available to everyone who can pay the filing fee.

### What the Arbiter is

The Office is on floor 77, a long room with a counter at one end and a wall of shelving behind it, and a door in the shelving
that nobody goes through but the clerks. The public deals with the **clerks of the Office**: they accept petitions, check
standing, take fees, and post determinations. The Arbiter itself is not seen. Determinations are signed **A.**, in a
handwriting that has not changed in twenty years.

The official account: the Arbiter is a **panel** of three senior arbitrators, appointed by the Company "from persons of
standing and independence", who sit in private and rule on the papers. The names are withheld "to protect the panel from
improper influence".

**Alternative:** the Arbiter is a single person: **Ambrose Hale-Fennick**, a Crown-born lawyer who has held the post since
Y61, is now very old, and still signs every determination himself, slowly, in fountain pen, in a room on floor 77 he has not
left in years. He is the last person in the tower who studied law in a Calder law school. Everything he knows about justice
is from a city that no longer exists, and he applies it, faithfully, to a lease.

**Alternative:** the Arbiter is a founding-era system. The tower's original administrative core (built Y0, to manage the
leases of thousands of plant workers, the meters and the payroll) included a **dispute resolution module**, designed to settle
pay and housing grievances between the Works and its workers by rule. When the plant stopped employing people, the module
kept running on the leases. The clerks feed it petitions on punched forms and post what it prints. The "A." is a stamp. It
rules by rules written in Y0 for a company that needed its workers, which is why some of its rulings, now and then, go
inexplicably for the tenant, and the Company quietly pays and then amends the bylaw the ruling was based on.

**Hook:** the third version can coexist with the first: a panel that signs for a machine nobody can repair, which the
Company is afraid to switch off because nobody remembers what else is wired to it. The Old Hands know the module as **"the
Grievance Box"**. The Archivists have its manual, or half of it.

### Procedure

1. **Petition.** The petitioner fills a **Form A-1** (four pages, small print, available at the floor 77 counter only, 2 cr per
   form; the Rain Church copies them by hand and gives them away, which is a bylaw offence). The petition names the parties, the
   clause of the Charter in dispute, and the relief sought.
2. **Standing.** The clerk checks the petitioner is a Leaseholder, not in arrears ("a party in default may not seek relief
   under the instrument in default of which it is"), and has paid the **filing fee**: a flat **60 cr** for everyone. Sixty credits
   is two days' Mills wages and less than a Crown resident tips a lift attendant. "Flat" is the Office's word for fair.
3. **Lodgement.** The Company (or the other party) has fourteen days to lodge a response. The Company lodges in thirteen.
4. **Submissions.** Written only. No hearing. No representation is required, but **lease counsel** (lawyers on 60 to 75 who
   specialise in Charter disputes, at 40 cr an hour) can be engaged. Counsel know which past determinations to cite, which is
   the whole skill: the determinations are posted for a week and then filed, and the files are behind the door in the shelving.
   Copies of past determinations are 5 cr a page. A good counsel's office is mostly a library of bought copies.
5. **Determination.** Within the **review period**, sixty days. **Expedited determination** in seven days is 300 cr.
6. **Costs.** The losing party pays both parties' costs. The Company's costs are assessed by the Company's own costs officer,
   and include an hourly rate for the Company's in-house counsel and a share of the Office's running costs. A Mills tenant who
   loses a dispute over a 20 cr meter charge typically owes 400 cr in costs.
7. **Reconsideration.** Either party may seek reconsideration within seven days, with a fee of double the filing fee. Only the
   Company ever does.

Determinations are posted on the **Register board** at every band's lease office and on 77. Each has a number (A/86/0412: the
year, then the sequence), the parties, a one-paragraph "finding", and the order. The Register board on 19 is read like a
newspaper. People laugh at it. They also learn from it.

**In play:** the Arbiter is a slow, expensive, rare choice, which fits it. Some events let the player **petition**: a meter
overcharge, a neighbour's water leak that drowned a grow bed, a collector who tagged goods that were not yours, a brand agent
who seized licensed stock. Petitioning costs the 60 cr fee and an A-1 form (an item, bought at the 77 counter by drone
contract, or given by the Rain Church). The determination arrives as a slip some days later. Most go against you, with costs;
some don't, and pay out. The odds can be shown honestly ("past determinations on this clause: 2 of 31 for the tenant"),
and they can be shifted by buying counsel or copies of precedents, both items that take space. A precedent copy that is
relevant (it names the same clause) is the closest the game has to a legal weapon.

### Sample determinations from the Register

These are real enough to be pinned on a board, and short enough to read in the lift.

**A/79/0031. Petitioner: R. Oduya, lease 17-F. Respondent: Halden Company.**
Finding: water from a burst Company pipe on floor 18 entered the petitioner's lease and destroyed four grow beds of
produce. Under Clause 17, water drawn on the leased squares is the tenant's. Water that crosses a floor boundary is drawn
on the receiving squares when it arrives. The meter on lease 17-F did not register the water because it did not pass the
meter. Order: the volume is estimated at 1,900 litres and billed to the petitioner at the Mills metered rate. The
petitioner's claim for the produce is dismissed: produce from beds watered with unmetered water is of uncertain
provenance. Costs to the respondent.

**A/81/0217. Petitioner: Orrin Provisions. Respondent: members of the Mills Growers' and Workers' Co-operative, bin
complex 4, floor 21.**
Finding: the insect stock in bin complex 4 carries the Orrin genetic marker in 61% of samples. The Co-operative states its
stock was bred from insects that escaped from a registered Orrin bin on floor 21 in Y74 and interbred with unregistered
stock. Insects descended from registered stock are registered stock. Order: bin complex 4 is registered to Orrin
Provisions, with effect from Y74. Registration fees are payable from that date. The Co-operative may continue to tend the
bins as an Orrin registered producer. (Note: this is *In re Bin Four*, below, §14.)

**A/82/0098. Petitioner: J. Halloran, lease 33-B. Respondent: Brisk & Lowe Recoveries.**
Finding: the respondent tagged and seized a drone frame which the petitioner says belonged to his brother and was on the
lease for repair. Goods on a lease are presumed to be the leaseholder's. The petitioner's brother is not a party. Order:
petition dismissed. The respondent's recovery is valid. The petitioner is reminded that goods held for repair should be
held under a Repair Licence, which provides a register of third-party goods. Costs to the respondent.

**A/83/0350. Petitioner: Halden Company. Respondent: lease 8-K (Leaseholder deceased).**
Finding: the leaseholder died on the lease. The remains were removed by Registered Occupants and taken to the Flats "for
burial at low tide". Under Bylaw 88, human remains on a lease are waste and must be discharged by the Company's sanitary
service at the scheduled rate. Order: the sanitary charge of 45 cr is payable by the Registered Occupants under Clause 77,
with a standards charge of 30 cr for "improper discharge of waste outside the Structure". Costs to the petitioner.

**A/84/0122. Petitioner: S. Mbeki-Lund, lease 64-A. Respondent: Halden Company.**
Finding: the petitioner's balcony garden on 64 was shaded for nine hours a day by an extension built on floor 65 under a
Company planning consent. The Charter, Schedule D, guarantees Terraces leases "access to natural daylight". Order: the
Company is to reduce the petitioner's rate by 6% for the duration of the shading, and supply grow lighting at its own cost.
No costs. (This determination is cited by Mills counsel in every daylight petition below 30, without success: Schedule D
appears only in Terraces Charters.)

**A/85/0007. Petitioner: T. Akerlund, lease 12-C. Respondent: Halden Company.**
Finding: the petitioner contends that the per-square rate on floor 12 increased by 40% in three years while the floor's
services declined, and that the Charter's "periodic review" requires the rate to reflect services. The Charter does not
say what the review reviews. Order: petition dismissed. The Office notes that the rate on floor 12 remains below the rate
on floor 13. Costs to the respondent.

**A/85/0291. Petitioner: lease 19-D. Respondent: lease 20-D.**
Finding: the respondent's garbage chute discharge included a quantity of solvent which corroded the petitioner's chute
container and contaminated its contents. Clause 23 conveys chute material "without warranty". Order: petition dismissed.
The petitioner's corroded chute container is a Company fixture; replacement at the petitioner's cost (85 cr). Costs to the
respondent.

**A/86/0019. Petitioner: lease 17-H. Respondent: Halden Company.**
Finding: the Company's lane scan at the floor 30 gate recorded a restored Vey blister pack in a drone launched by the
petitioner, and the drone and all cargo were seized. The petitioner contends the pack was cargo for a client on 28, the
drone was misrouted by a lane fault, and it should never have passed the gate. The Company's lane logs confirm a routing
fault. Order: the drone is to be returned. The cargo is retired. The petitioner is to pay the scan charge. Costs: each
party bears its own. (Notable as a "win" in the Mills. People still talk about it. The petitioner got his drone back.)

**A/86/0204. Petitioner: Halden Company. Respondent: unnamed persons, the Rain Church.**
Finding: rain falling on the roof of the Structure is collected under the Skywater licence. Rain that falls past the roof
and onto the Structure's outer gutters is rain on the Structure. The gutters are Company property. Rain in them is
therefore in the possession of the Company. Order: the respondents are restrained from collecting rain from any external
surface of the Structure. The Office notes it cannot restrain the weather. (Posted on every floor. The Rain Church has the
last sentence painted on its walls.)

**In play:** these determinations can be read as **notices** on the lobby board between days, a quiet way to teach the
player the rules of the world, one at a time, through cases. Each one can shadow a mechanic: the leak case explains why
water through the floor is billed; the drone case explains lane scans and seizure; the chute case explains Clause 23.

## 8. The Bylaws

*Bylaws of the Halden Company concerning the Orderly Occupancy of the Halden Residential Structure. Consolidated
edition, Y84, incorporating amendments posted to Y86. Issued under the Emergency Stewardship Order and Clause 4 of the
Occupancy Charter. These Bylaws are made for the wellbeing of all residents.*

The full text runs to 140 bylaws. The ones below are those that turn up in standards charges, on lobby boards, and in
jokes. Numbers are real (in-world); gaps are bylaws nobody quotes.

### Part I: General

1. These Bylaws apply to every person within the Structure, whether or not a party to the Framework.
2. Ignorance of a Bylaw is not a defence. Posting of a Bylaw in the lift lobby of a floor constitutes notice to every person
   on that floor, and on any floor served by the same lift.
3. Where a Bylaw and the Charter conflict, the provision more protective of the Structure prevails.
4. The Company may waive any Bylaw in any instance. A waiver is not a precedent.
5. The headings of these Bylaws are for convenience and do not form part of them. (People quote this one when a heading
   says "Wellbeing".)
6. Every Leaseholder shall keep a copy of the Charter on the lease at all times. A copy on a screen is not a copy.
7. Every person shall, on request by an officer of the Company, a delegated licensor or a floor warden, state their name,
   lease, and the purpose of their presence. "Living here" is not a purpose.
8. No person shall impersonate an officer of the Company. Persons wearing a grey coverall with a coloured armband are
   presumed to be officers.

### Part II: Occupancy

11. No person shall sleep on a square not registered as residential. A person who closes their eyes for more than twenty
    minutes on a non-residential square is presumed to be sleeping.
12. Each Registered Occupant is allocated not less than two squares of residential floor. Squares occupied by furniture are
    counted.
13. A child under the age of twelve is half a Registered Occupant for the purposes of floor allocation, and one Registered
    Occupant for the purposes of fees.
14. No partition, shelf, hook, rail, bracket or fixture shall be fixed to any wall, floor or ceiling of the Structure.
    Free-standing furniture is permitted. Furniture that remains standing only because it leans against a wall is fixed.
15. Births on a lease shall be registered within seven days. Unregistered children are non-parties and the lease is liable for
    their conduct.
17. Assemblies of more than eight non-household persons on a lease require an Assembly Notice, obtainable from the floor
    warden at a fee. Worship is an assembly. Grief is an assembly.
19. No Leaseholder shall permit any person to occupy the lease by the hour, the shift, or any period shorter than a week,
    except under a Sublet Licence.
22. A Leaseholder who vacates a lease shall leave it "in the condition in which it was let". The condition in which a lease
    was let is recorded by the Lease Office and is not disclosed.

### Part III: Water and power

30. All water consumed within the Structure shall be Halden Pure, Licensed Treatment water, or rainwater bottled under the
    Skywater licence.
31. No person shall treat, purify, distil, condense, collect or recover water for consumption except under a Licensed
    Treatment Certificate.
32. The possession of apparatus capable of treating water is evidence of an intent to treat water. A kettle is not apparatus.
    A kettle with a coil of tubing attached is apparatus.
33. No person shall break, remove, cover, reverse, bypass, interfere with, or "look closely at" a meter seal.
    ("Look closely at" was added in Y79 after a case in which a tenant photographed a seal to report a fault.)
34. A meter is presumed accurate. A tenant who disputes a reading may request a calibration test at a fee. If the meter is
    found to be inaccurate, the fee is not refunded, as the test was performed.
35. Water drawn and not consumed (spilled, leaked, evaporated) is drawn.
36. No person shall release treated water outside the metering framework. Pouring Halden Pure into a vessel owned by another
    lease is a release, unless the other lease pays.
37. Condensation on the walls of a lease belongs to the Company until collected by the Company, which does not collect it.
39. No person shall store more than forty litres of water on a lease below floor 60 without a Storage Notice. ("Stockpiling
    is a threat to supply.")
40. Power drawn from a Company socket shall be used on the lease where the socket is. A cell charged on one lease and
    discharged on another has transferred power, which is resale.
41. Resale of Company power is prohibited.
42. Persons charging cells by means other than a Company socket (solar film, wind vane, hand crank, salvaged generator)
    shall register the apparatus and pay a **standing charge** equal to the power that would have been drawn.

### Part IV: Waste

50. Waste shall be discharged only through the garbage chute serving the lease.
51. The contents of a chute are the property of the lease to which the chute discharges, from the moment of discharge.
    Interception of chute contents between floors is theft from that lease, or, where the chute passes through Company
    squares, from the Company.
52. Branded goods shall be retired before discharge in accordance with the brand's retirement instructions.
53. Persons who retrieve, restore or offer for sale retired goods shall be liable under the Retirement Ordinance. Persons who
    fail to retire goods before discharge shall be reminded.
55. A chute container that is full shall be cleared within twenty-four hours. A chute that cannot discharge into a full
    container shall be closed at the source, and the floor above notified that its chute is closed, at the lower lease's
    cost.
57. No person shall enter a chute.
58. Persons found in a chute are waste for the purposes of Bylaw 51 until they leave it.
88. Human remains on a lease are waste and shall be discharged by the Company's sanitary service at the scheduled rate.
    Remains shall not be discharged by the chute.
89. Discharge of remains outside the Structure (to the water, to the Flats) is improper discharge.

### Part V: Movement

60. The lifts are operated by the Lift Guild under Company licence. Fares, tolls and times of operation are set by the Guild.
61. Drones launched from leases below floor 30 shall display a lane transponder, whether or not they use a lane.
62. No drone shall enter a licensed lane without a lane permit. A drone found in a licensed lane without a permit is
    "unidentified" and may be brought down by lane wardens. Cargo of a downed unidentified drone is retained.
63. The free shafts are unmanaged airspace. The Company accepts no responsibility for anything that occurs within them.
    Goods found within them are abandoned. (Shaft rats read this aloud as a blessing before a run.)
64. No person shall pass a floor-band gate (at floors 30, 60 and 90) without a **band pass** for the band above. Band passes
    for the band below are not required. ("Movement downward is unrestricted.")
65. Day passes to a higher band are issued to persons with a contract, appointment or employment above, valid until 20:00.
66. A person found above their band after 20:00 without a pass is presumed to be loitering.
67. Loitering in a lift lobby is prohibited. Waiting for a lift for more than twenty minutes is loitering.
68. Stairs are for emergency use. Daily use of stairs is not an emergency.
69. The outer stair (from floor 9 to the Flats) is not part of the Structure. Persons on the outer stair are outside.

### Part VI: Trade

70. No goods shall be offered for sale to another lease except under a Trader's Licence.
71. Gifts are permitted. A gift for which anything is returned, including a later gift, is a sale.
72. Prices shall be displayed in credits. Prices may additionally be displayed in scrip. Prices shall not be displayed in
    water, protein blocks, labour or "favours".
73. Food offered for sale shall carry an Orrin grade stamp or be labelled "ungraded", and ungraded food shall be offered only
    to persons of the same lease.
74. No goods shall be described as "real", "grown", "natural" or "fresh" unless they carry the appropriate grade stamp.
    Tomatoes are not exempt.
75. No goods shall bear a mark resembling the mark of a licensed brand.
76. Halden scrip is legal tender at the company store and at any trader who elects to accept it. Traders who accept scrip may
    redeem it at the company store for goods. Scrip may not be redeemed for credits.
77. Barter between leases is a sale and shall be recorded in credits at a value determined by the Company.
79. Pawnbroking requires a Trader's Licence and a Repair Licence, because pawned goods are presumed to need repair.

### Part VII: Conduct

81. Singing is noise.
82. No person shall keep an animal, except registered insect stock and the household cat of a Crown lease, which is not an

83. No person shall broadcast. Receiving a broadcast is not prohibited. Repeating what was heard on a broadcast is

85. No person shall cover their face in a public part of the Structure, except for a mask worn for respiratory reasons,
    which shall be of a type sold at the company store.
86. Smoking is permitted only in designated squares, which are designated by fee.
87. Persons below floor 30 shall not gather on the outer face of the Structure (window ledges, balconies, gutters) for the
    purpose of receiving rain.

### Part VIII: Licensed services

90. Medical treatment may be provided only by a practitioner licensed by Vey or by St. Ober's Spire. First aid is permitted.
    First aid that works is treatment.
91. No person shall sell blood except to a licensed plasma buyer.
92. No person shall teach a trade except under a Trade Instruction Licence. Showing is teaching.
93. No person shall repair a Company fixture (meter, socket, tap, lift, door, chute, lamp) except under a Repair Licence. A
    Company fixture which a tenant has repaired is damaged.
95. Founding-era components recovered from the Structure are Company property. Founding-era components recovered from
    outside the Structure (the Flats, the water, the drowned floors) are salvage, and salvage is subject to the **Salvage Levy**

### Part IX: Penalties

     moderation, a credit note, custody, lease adjustment or any combination.
101. A standards charge not paid in fourteen days is a debt to the Company.

131. These Bylaws may be cited as the Wellbeing Bylaws.
140. The Company thanks all residents for their cooperation.

**In play:** bylaws are a seasoning system. Pick three or four at random each in-game month to be **"under review"**: posted
on the lobby board, enforced more often that month. "Bylaw 39 review this month: water storage checks." The player sees it
and adjusts: fewer water containers, or hide them in drones. Cheap to build (a modifier on inspection weights), full of world.

**In play:** some bylaws are the text on item tooltips or slips. The kettle with a coil of tubing (Bylaw 32) as an item
that the game literally calls "apparatus". A **Storage Notice** (Bylaw 39) as a 1x1 paper the player buys to keep more than 40 l of water on the field, a direct cost-of-space rule with a lore reason.

## 9. Offences and what they cost

The Company does not have crimes. It has **standards matters**, graded A to D by the **Schedule of Standards Charges**
(Schedule M of the Charter). The schedule sets a base charge and lets the officer apply a **band multiplier**, which is
always described as "proportionate": the same offence is charged at a fraction in the Mills and a multiple in the
Terraces, which sounds kind until you work out the multiplier against wages. Crown residents are not charged; their matters
are "referred to counsel" (§10).

Band multipliers: Sump 0.5, Mills 1, Middle 2, Terraces 5, Crown "by referral".

### Credit notes

The credit rating (file 04 owns the mechanics) is where the law bites longest. A standards charge is paid once; a credit note
stays on the rating for a period that depends on the class (A: 90 days; B: 60; C: 30; D: none). Contracts on the board carry
a minimum rating. So a stinking charge doesn't just cost 250 cr; it takes the Middle contracts off the player's board for three
months.

**In play:** the trade-off between fine and rating is the core of legal risk. Some players will happily pay fines; nobody
likes losing the good contracts. The design can show a **standing card** for the lease: the rating, the credit notes on it,
and the date each falls off. This is information, not punishment. Bylaw 103 says so.

### Reassignment

The ultimate penalty below termination is **reassignment** (Bylaw 105): the Company moves a repeatedly-charged lease to a lower
floor. Same number of squares, a lower rate, a worse chute, worse routes. It is presented as a kindness ("a more affordable
environment"), and it is a real kindness to the tenant's bills, which is why it hurts.

**In play:** reassignment is a canon-friendly fail-forward. Instead of game over for a chronically indebted or law-breaking
player, the lease is moved down a few floors: the backdrop darkens, the chute table gets worse, the rent drops, the contract
board shifts. The player keeps the business. They are just lower. Climbing back up is a run in itself. Moving down by
choice should exist too (canon: "moving down is the way out: cheaper rent"), and it should look identical on the paperwork,
except that a voluntary move is called a **relocation** and costs a fee.

## 10. Justice by band

The same act means different things at different heights. The cleanest way to see it is to follow one act up and down the
tower: **a person takes a litre of water that is not theirs.**

### In the Crown (90–104)

A gardener on 96 is found to have filled a bottle from the rain cistern of the family he works for. HPS is not called; the
Sovereign detail on 96 works for the family and reports to the family. The family's **counsel** (a Terraces lawyer retained by
the household) speaks to the gardener's agency, a labour broker on 62. The gardener is dismissed, the agency replaces him by
the afternoon, and the agency deducts the litre (valued at the Crown's notional rate for rain, which is set by Skywater at
40 cr a litre "to reflect its rarity") from his last wages. His band pass is cancelled. He'll find another job, two bands
down.

Crown residents themselves never appear in the Schedule. When a Crown resident does something the Bylaws forbid (and the
Bylaws forbid many things the Crown does daily: keeping animals other than a cat, fixing things to walls, storing water,
assembling, singing), it is a **matter referred to counsel**, which means the family's lawyer and the Company's lawyer have
lunch. The phrase for Crown justice is **settled upstairs**. The Crown has a body called the **Founders' Table**, a dining club of the
old families, which settles disputes between Crown households by agreement; it has no written rules and no record, and its
decisions are obeyed absolutely.

### In the Terraces (60–89)

A clinic nurse on 72 is found to have taken water from the clinic's supply for her own flat. HPS attends in ten minutes
(the clinic has Assured cover). A Protective takes a statement. The clinic files a standards charge at the Terraces
multiplier: around 600 cr for "misappropriation of treated water". The nurse's Charter is reviewed. She has a lease counsel
already (most Terraces families do, on retainer), who negotiates the charge down to a credit note and restitution. She keeps
her flat. She loses her job. The credit note means she can't renew the lease next year at the same rate; she will move to
the 60s, then the Middle. Terraces justice is fast, clean and expensive, and its real penalty is falling.

### In the Middle (30–59)

A licensed noodle cook on 41 is found by a warden to be topping up his licensed water with water from an unplated filter.
This is the band where law is most present, because the Middle is where people have the most to lose and the least to hide
behind. The warden reports, the WWO sends a taster within the week, the filter is tagged and taken, the standards charge is
250 cr at the Middle multiplier (500), and his Trader's Licence is suspended pending review. His name goes up on the lobby
board under Bylaw 104. His customers drift to the cook on 40. He fights it at the Arbiter, loses, owes costs. Eighteen
months later he is cooking on 19 with no licence and a better filter.

The Middle is anxious because it is the band where everything can still be lost and where everyone watches everyone. Most
warden reports in the tower are filed between 30 and 59.

### In the Mills (10–29)

A grower on 21 finds his grow bed's water vessel half-empty in the morning. He doesn't call HPS: Essential cover means four
hours and a 15 cr callout fee to receive an incident number. He calls the **Round** (§12), the Co-op's patrol, who already
know: the neighbour's kid was seen with a jug at 05:00. The Round takes it to a **floor meeting** that evening; the family
pays it back in water over a week, plus a day's work in the Co-op beds, and the kid is put on the bin-cleaning rota for a
month. Nobody files anything. The Company never learns there was a theft, which is the point.

But in the same week, the same floor gets a **baseline review** because three leases are drawing too little Halden Pure, and
the tasters come, and two stills and a filter rig are taken, and nobody on the floor could do anything. The Mills live under
two laws at once: the Company's, which arrives in raids, and their own, which arrives in meetings.

### In the Sump (4–9)

Someone takes a litre from a Pumpmen rain-drum on 7 (§11). There is no HPS; the floor's Community Cover pool hasn't been
paid in two years. The Pumpmen's **bench** sits that evening; the thief stands in the water ("wet time") on 5 for as many hours
as the litre is worth in pump-hours, which is a lot, because a litre in the Sump is worth more than anywhere but the Crown.
If they steal again, they go on the outer stair, which is to say they leave the Structure and become a Flats problem.

Sump justice is harsh and quick and, people say, **fair in its own measure**: it charges for what was taken, not for what
the taking implies about you. The Company's law charges for the implication.

### On the Flats

No law. Bylaw 69: the outer stair is not part of the Structure; the Flats are not part of anything. What happens on the Flats
is settled by the Tide Folk's customs (file 06a) and by whoever is strongest at low tide. HPS has never set foot there. The
Company sends its rubbish there and its debt collectors don't follow, which is why a few people go to the Flats on purpose:
a debt on the arrears register can't touch you where there is no register.

**Hook:** a Flats rumour, believed by many in the Sump: a debt follows you up the tower but not down it, past the waterline. So
a few desperate debtors "go to the water" and live a year on the Flats, and come back up claiming the debt is gone. The
Arbiter has never ruled on this. Brisk & Lowe say it's nonsense. The Varga Books say nothing, and there is a column of names on
their wall on 6 with a line through each one and no amount next to it.

**In play:** band is a modifier, not a separate system. The same inspection, fine, or confiscation event is scaled by the band
the player's lease is in (and, for drones, the band the destination is in). Lower is cheaper but more frequent; higher is
rarer and much more expensive. The Sump has almost no Company enforcement at all, but its own costs (pump tithe, gang
tolls) replace them. A player who moves down to escape inspections finds the inspections are replaced by tithes.

## 11. The Sump's own law

The Sump is not lawless. It has too much law: four gangs, each with its own rules, its own territory and its own reason for
existing, and all of them built around one thing the Company stopped providing. Where the Company left a gap, a gang grew into
its shape. That's why they last: each one is doing a job.

The Sump's floors (4 to 9) are the flooded factory's upper halls: tall, wet, half-dark, with water standing on 4 and 5 and
seeping on 6 and 7. The chutes end here. The outer stair to the Flats starts on 9. The waterline is the first fact of life, and
the first law of the Sump is about it.

### The Pumpmen

The Works had **bilge pumps** in its upper halls, founding-era machines built to keep process water out of the electronics.
After the Drowning they were the only thing keeping 5, 6 and 7 dry enough to live on. The Company did not maintain them (they
are not, the Lease Office says, "residential fixtures"). A group of former Works maintenance men did. Their grandchildren still
do. The **Pumpmen** run the pumps on 5 to 7, keep the cells charged, scavenge parts, and charge every lease on those floors a
**pump tithe**: a weekly fee in credits, cells, or hours of labour on the pumps.

Their law is **the Level**. The Level is the waterline on the gauge the Pumpmen keep painted on a pillar on 5, and everything
in their code is about keeping it down.

- *Keep the Level.* Anything that raises the water (blocking a drain, dumping in the sumps, diverting a pipe) is the worst
  offence there is. It's punished by expulsion to the Flats.
- *The pump comes first.* A pump call (a horn, three blasts) means every able person on 5 to 7 drops what they are doing and
  hauls. Not coming is an offence.
- *Tithe is owed by the floor, not the person.* If one lease doesn't pay, the floor makes it up, and the floor settles with the
  lease. (This makes everyone a debt collector of their neighbour, which is the point.)
- *Water taken is water owed.* Theft of drinkable water is repaid in **wet time**: standing in the water on 5, at the Level,
  for a number of hours set by the bench. The water is cold and poisoned; wet time leaves a rash that takes weeks to clear,
  and the rash is the sentence everyone can see.
- *No one is put in the water who can't stand.* Children, the old, the sick serve their wet time by proxy: someone stands for
  them. Often it's the person who reported them.

The Pumpmen's **bench** is three senior pumpmen who sit on upturned pump housings at the foot of the 5 stairwell. Anyone may
bring a matter. There is no fee. There is no appeal, except to the pump itself: a person who believes they are wronged can
"go to the pump" and work a full shift without being asked, which obliges the bench to hear the matter again the next day. The
current head of the bench is **Old Sefton Marr**, who is not old (he's forty-four) but whose father was.

**In play:** if the player's lease is ever in the Sump, or they take Sump contracts, the pump tithe is the Sump's rent. The
Pumpmen are clients: they need **cells**, **seals**, **bearings**, and **founding-era pump parts**, and pay in labour credits or
in dry storage space (squares on 7 that stay dry, rented to you below the Company's rate). A pump failure event floods the
lowest row of a Sump lease: items on that row get wet (paper ruined, cells shorted). The Pumpmen's horn is a sound cue.

### The Hooks

Every garbage chute in the Stack ends in the Sump, in one of three **drops**: the compactor pits beside the Jaws in the Bale Hall on 8, where whatever no lease
claimed falls (the chutes of empty leases, overflow from full containers, chutes closed at the source). The Company's sanitary
service was meant to clear the drops weekly; it now clears them "as scheduled", which is never. The **Hooks** clear them
instead. They're named for the long hooks they use to drag goods out of the drops, and they hold the drops as property.

Their law is **first hook**: whoever's hook touches an item first owns it, and the Hooks decide whose hook that is. Everything
else follows from this:

- Pickers must be Hooks or pay the Hooks a **hook fee** (a cut of what they pull) to work a drop.
- Anything pulled that is branded and VOID goes to the Hooks' sorting room on 6, where it's priced and sold to restorers up the
  tower, through the Voided (file 06b). The Hooks are the start of most restoration chains in the Stack.
- **Bodies** (and there are some, despite Bylaw 88) are not property. Any Hook who finds one stops work, and the drop is closed
  for the day while the Tide Folk are sent for. The Hooks are rough, and they are also, without exception, the people most
  careful with the dead in the whole tower.
- Hooks don't steal from leases. Their territory is the drops, and only the drops. A Hook caught robbing a lease is handed to
  the Pumpmen's bench, which the Hooks consider worse than anything they could do themselves.

**In play:** the Hooks are a **supplier**: the player can buy a **drop lot** (a sack of unsorted drop material, cheaper than
anything else, with worse odds than the chute) through a Hooks middleman, or contract them to look for specific items in the
drops ("any founding-era sync node, any condition"). The drop lot is the bottom of the material economy, below the player's
own chute. It's also where the player's own void items end up if they discard them, so a player who sells VOID goods back to the
Hooks for a little is completing a loop.

### The Ninefold

Floor 9 is the Sump's top dry floor and its door: the band gate to the Mills is on 10, the outer stair to the Flats starts on 9,
and the dock is on 9 (where the Bastion boat ties up, where Flats boats come in at high tide, where salvage is landed). Whoever
holds 9 holds the Sump's trade. The **Ninefold** are nine families who have held 9 since Y58, and their law is **the toll**.

- Every load up from the Flats, every load in from the water, and every load down from the Mills that isn't drone-borne pays the
  Ninefold toll at the 9 landing: one item in nine, the Ninefold's choice. (Which is why salvage arrives at the Mills in
  loads of eight.)
- Inside the toll, the Ninefold guarantee passage: a load that has paid can't be touched by anyone on 4 to 9.
- The Ninefold also keep the **Sump's register**: a book in which deaths, births, marriages and debts on 4 to 9 are written.
  It's the only register the Sump trusts, because the Company's is used against people and the Ninefold's isn't. The
  Ninefold book records **settled** debts too, which the Company's never does.
- The Ninefold deal with HPS. They're the only gang that does.

**The Quiet.** The arrangement between HPS and the Ninefold is called the Quiet. HPS does not enter the Sump except in a
**sweep** (a full raid in strength, scheduled, announced to the Ninefold the day before), and the Ninefold make sure that the
sweep finds what HPS needs it to find: a still or two, a bootleg broadcasting rig, someone already on the Varga Books who
would be better off in custody than in the water. In return the Ninefold hand over people HPS actually wants, and the
Ninefold's toll is never examined. Everyone on 4 to 9 knows about the Quiet. Nobody above 10 believes it is real.

**In play:** **salvage loads of eight.** Every salvage supplier who comes up through 9 has already paid the Ninefold. Loads that
come up by drone through the free shafts (avoiding 9) skip the toll and arrive with nine items, but the free shafts have their
own risks. A **Ninefold seal** (a wax stamp on a crate) on a salvage crate means "paid, untouchable": inspectors from the WWO and
the Bureau know what it means and leave the crate alone below floor 10, because the Quiet covers it. Above 10 the seal is
worthless. A player with a Ninefold contact can buy seals, which is a legal grey item that only works in one band.

### The Wet Widows

The drowned floors 0 to 3 hold the Works' lowest halls, and in them everything the plant left: founding-era machines, cells
by the thousand, cable, tooling, sealed stores. Divers go down. Many didn't come up in the early years. Their widows,
**the Wet Widows**, organised in Y61 to stop it, and now hold **dive rights** to 0–3: no one dives without a Widows' permit,
and no one gets a permit without a Widows-checked rebreather, a tether and a partner on the line.

- *No dive alone.* A lone diver's salvage is forfeit to the Widows.
- *The line is sacred.* Cutting another diver's line, or failing to haul a partner in trouble, is the one offence the Widows
  punish with banishment from all four Sump gangs at once (they have that agreement).
- *A share to the house.* One part in five of every dive's haul goes to the Widows' house on 8, which keeps the widows and
  children of every diver lost since Y57, and pays for rebreather cells.
- *What's below is not the Company's.* Bylaw 95 says salvage from outside the Structure owes the Salvage Levy. The Widows hold
  that floors 0 to 3 are outside the Structure, because the Company's own Lease Office declared them "uninhabitable and
  removed from the residential register" in Y58. So the drowned plant is, legally, the Company's when it wants the levy, and
  not the Company's when someone drowns in it.

**In play:** founding-era parts are among the game's most valuable items, and most come from below. A part with a **Widows'
mark** (a notch filed on the casing) has come through their permit system and is honest salvage. A part without one is
either from the Flats, from inside the walls (Company property under Bylaw 95: a crime to sell) or stolen from a diver. The
mark means the player is not buying from a line-cutter. It also costs more.

**Hook:** the Widows keep a list of dives that never came back. Some of the names on it are recent, and were good divers, and
went down in the same corner of floor 1, where the Works' **process water hall** was: under the Dark Floors, in the plant's
plan, almost directly. The hall is supposed to be empty and flooded. One of the last divers to come back from there said the
water was warm.

### Between the gangs

The four rarely fight. The Sump can't afford it: one bad week on the pumps and everyone drowns. They meet, by custom, on the
first of each month at the Old Post on 8, the old HPS post-turned-bar, and settle borders and disputes there, with the Vargas'
eldest (**Mother Varga**) keeping the record in chalk on the cage wall because she owes nothing to any of them.

**In play:** the Sump as an alternative legal order is a strong mid-to-late game texture. A player moving business down there
(or just taking more Sump contracts) swaps Company fines for gang tithes and tolls, swaps a credit rating for a **standing**
in the Sump that is earned in favours, and gains access to goods the Company would seize. Neither order is good. Both are
predictable once you know them.

## 12. The Mills Co-op's Round

The **Mills Growers' and Workers' Co-operative** (file 06a has the Co-op itself) began, as most of its members tell it, with
water theft. In the years after the Drowning, every bed in the Mills drew its water by the hour from a vessel, and vessels went
missing at night. HPS did nothing below 30. So growers started sitting up with their beds, then sitting up together, then
walking the floor in pairs. That became **the Round**.

The Round is a patrol of Co-op members, **roundsmen** (everyone, of any sex, is a roundsman), who walk their floor in pairs
on a rota: two hours a week each, more at harvest. Kit: a whistle, a lamp, a **round book** (who walked when, what they saw),
and a **green cord** tied round the upper arm, which looks enough like a warden's armband to give a stranger pause and is not
enough like it to be impersonation under Bylaw 8. (This was argued in a floor meeting for three evenings.)

### The Seven Rules of the Round

Painted on the wall of the Co-op hall on 21, and in pencil on the inside cover of every round book:

1. **Walk in twos.** Never alone; one to see, one to remember.
2. **Whistle, don't fight.** Three short blasts calls the floor. The floor does the rest.
3. **Water first.** A thief with water stops; a thief with anything else may run.
4. **No names to the Company.** What the Round sees goes to the floor meeting and nowhere else. A roundsman who files a warden
   report is out of the Round and out of the Co-op.
5. **The meeting decides.** No roundsman punishes anyone. The floor meeting does, by show of hands.
6. **Pay back, don't pay up.** Settlements are restitution in kind (water, produce, labour) and never fines. The Co-op does not
   take money for justice.
7. **Everyone walks.** If you eat from the Co-op beds, you walk the Round.

### The floor meeting

Held in the evening, in the Co-op hall on the floor or the widest corridor, open to every lease on the floor (Co-op or not).
A matter is stated by the roundsmen who saw it. The accused speaks. Anyone may speak. Then a show of hands on a settlement. The
usual settlements, from the Co-op's own unwritten tariff:

- **Water back**, double, over a week.
- **Bed days**: days of work in the Co-op grow beds, the bin rooms, or the tool library.
- **Suspension**: from the Co-op's tool library or water share, for weeks.
- **Shaming board**: the person's name, offence and settlement on a slate by the Co-op hall door until it's paid. (The Co-op
  argues this is not a breach of Bylaw 104 because Bylaw 104 is about the Company publishing names. It is a breach of Bylaw 84.)
- **Leaving the Co-op**: for repeat offences, or for informing. In the Mills, leaving the Co-op means losing the shared water,
  tools and seed, and is close to a death sentence for a small grower.

### Where the Round stands with the Company

Technically, the Round breaks several bylaws: Bylaw 17 (assembly without notice: every floor meeting), Bylaw 8 (it was ruled
not to, but the Company reserves its view), and an unnumbered Lease Office notice of Y69 prohibiting "unlicensed security
services". HPS tolerates it because it lowers call volume at Post Nineteen. When the Company wants something from the Co-op
(a rate agreement, quiet during a rent rise, the names of stinkers), the Round becomes an "unlicensed security service"
overnight, and roundsmen are taken into custody for a few days for walking their own floors.

This has happened three times, most recently during the **rent review of Y85**, when 20 and 21 were taken off the Round for a
month. Grow beds on both floors lost a quarter of their water in that month, and the Company's food-price index for the
Mills rose, and Orrin's Vitabrick sales on those floors rose with it.

**In play:** the Round is a **membership with a cost and a benefit**. Joining the Co-op (file 06a for the full picture) puts
the player on the rota: a few hours a week of the player's day are reserved (a scheduled interruption, or paid off by sending
a hireling, or by contributing goods). In return, thefts from the player's field drop sharply, and a theft that does happen
has a good chance of a **floor-meeting settlement**: the item, or its value in water or produce, comes back a day later.
Membership also forbids informing: if the player ever accepts a Company bounty (§13), they're out.

**In play:** a **floor meeting** is a small event the player may attend as a choice: someone is accused, the player has
seen something (the game shows it as a memory slip: "Saw a kid from 17-K by your beds at 06:00"). Raise a hand or don't.
Small stakes, real faces, and a later client remembering how you voted.

**Alternative:** the Round is not the Co-op's at all, but a separate older body, the **Lampmen**, from the Works' own night
security, inherited by their families: older, more secretive, with founding-era master keys to the plant halls, who walk
not for the growers but for the Old Hands (file 06a). The Co-op pays the Lampmen for cover. This version makes the Mills'
own law a bit less democratic and a lot more interesting.

## 13. Corruption, and what it costs

Bribery in the Stack is not hidden. It is merely never called bribery. Every officer in the tower is paid by commission, by
quota, by rebate, or so little that the job only pays with extras. So the extras are a price list everyone knows, quoted in
euphemisms everyone uses.

### The vocabulary

- **Stair money**: a small payment to make someone go away. ("Give him stair money and he'll take the stairs.")
- **A Foundation gift**: a payment disguised as a donation to the Foundation (file 06b), the Crown's charity, which issues a
  receipt. The officer has a Foundation collection tin. The donation is real; the Foundation receives a fixed share; the
  officer keeps the rest. Foundation gifts are the way bribes are paid above floor 30, because receipts make everything
  respectable. The Foundation's annual report thanks "the many Protective and licensing officers whose personal collections
  make our work possible".
- **Lamp oil**: a regular weekly payment, to keep the lights on in a relationship.
- **A courtesy**: a favour from an inspector (advance notice, a missed shelf). A courtesy is always "returned".
- **Feeding**: giving goods rather than money, as with Fair Ashe (§4).
- **Lost paper**: a document (a warden report, a recovery notice, a seizure tag) that never reaches its office.

### The going rates (Mills, Y86)

| What | Who | Rate | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Advance notice of a WWO inspection on your floor | a WWO clerk on 46, by drone note | 10 cr | the clerk sells it to the whole floor; the floor shares the cost |
| A taster who doesn't see the still | the taster | about a fifth of the standards charge (50 cr) | paid as a Foundation gift; tasters keep a tin |
| A yellowcoat grading pressings as Vitabrick | the grader | 2 cr per stack | lucrative for the grader; dangerous if Orrin marker-swabs later |
| A stamper leaves the shelf alone | the stamper | one item a week, stamper's choice | "feeding Ashe"; some stampers take cash at 10% of list value |
| A warden's report stays in the Book | the warden | 3 to 5 cr a week, or the warden's price in kind | Hester takes clocks |
| An HPS Attendant comes within the hour on Essential | the Attendant | 5 cr | "priority uplift"; officially sold by HPS for 12 cr, unofficially for 5 |
| A collector "can't find" a tagged item | the tagger of a Brisk & Lowe pair | 15% of the item's value | the talker never knows; or always knows |
| A yellow tag on someone else's goods | a collector | 20 cr | the dark one: steer a seizure to a rival's shop |
| A band pass after 20:00 | a gate Attendant | 8 cr | the gates on 30 and 60 are the most profitable posts in HPS |
| A lane scan that reads clean | a lane warden at a gate | 10 cr per drone, or a share of cargo | file 09 covers the scanners |
| A meter reading "corrected" | a Halden meter reader | 20% of the reduction | meter readers are Facilities, not HPS; the Old Hands say half of them can't read a founding-era meter anyway |
| A lost determination file | a clerk at 77 | 100 cr | the most expensive bribe in the tower that most people can afford; means a precedent cited against you can't be found |
| Expedited determination, unofficial | a clerk at 77 | 120 cr | cheaper than the official 300 |
| A registration that predates the inspection | a Lease Office clerk | 30 cr | turns a ghost into a Registered Occupant, as of last month |

**In play:** bribes are **a choice with a visible price** at the moment an enforcement event happens. A taster arrives; the
player can accept the standards charge (credits plus a credit note), or offer a Foundation gift (fewer credits, no note).
Most officers accept most of the time. Each bribe paid adds to a **favour ledger** with that office, which raises the price
next time and, past a threshold, means the officer comes back looking for it. That's not a hidden meter; it's a slip the
player keeps, a receipt from the Foundation, which is also evidence (§15).

**In play:** Foundation receipts. Every bribe paid as a Foundation gift gives the player a receipt (1x1). Receipts can be
thrown away, or kept, and a stack of receipts naming the same officer is **leverage**: a petition to the Arbiter, a
contract from a rival inspector, or a sale to Static, the pirate radio (file 06b). Keeping receipts takes space. Burning them
removes the risk and the leverage together.

### Informing

The Company pays for information, at published rates, under the **Wellbeing Assistance Scheme**: a bounty for any report that
leads to a standards charge. Rates: 10% of the charge for a Class A matter, 5% for B, nothing for C and D, because those are
"civic duty". Reports are made by drone note to the Lease Office, by a slot in the wall at Post Nineteen (the **Wellbeing
box**), or through a floor warden (who takes a cut).

Informing is the most dangerous crime in the Mills that isn't a crime. A known informer is out of the Co-op, refused by the
Hooks, tolled double by the Ninefold, and has a hard time getting their chute cleared. The Mills word is **boxer**, from the
Wellbeing box.

**In play:** a contract slip with no origin stamp ("A report regarding lease 17-K. 40 cr on confirmation.") turns up on the
board now and then. Taking it means writing a report about a neighbour the player knows, who is often a client. It pays
well. It raises the player's standing with the Company (a small credit rating bonus). It's remembered.

## 14. Notable cases

These are the cases people in the tower know by name. Some are in the Register; some are older than it.

### Pell v. Halden Works (Y55–Y57)

The last case heard by a real court in Calder. **Tamsin Pell**, back in Calder, a hydrologist with the city, sued Halden Works for
damages to the city's drainage, arguing the plant's groundwater pumping had lowered the ground under the eastern districts
by over a metre and that the seawall maintenance contract (held by Halden) had been let fall below its own specification. The
case went to trial in the spring of Y57. Pell's evidence was a set of subsidence surveys and a copy of the seawall inspection
schedule with half the inspections marked "deferred". The court adjourned for the winter recess. The storm came in the Third Month.
The courthouse was in old Calder. The court records, which included Pell's surveys, were in its basement.

Pell drowned in the Drowning, on the ground floor of the courthouse, by most accounts, where she had gone to try to save the
records. The Company's position since has been that the case was never decided and so there is nothing to say about it.

**Hook:** the Archivists say they have two of Pell's six survey volumes. The Wet Widows say there is a sealed **evidence safe**
in the courthouse basement, which is in old Calder, under water, reachable from the Flats at the lowest tides of the year.
People have tried. One came back with a door handle. It is in the Archivists' collection, labelled.

### The Wells Inquiry and the Wells Forty (Y58)

After the Drowning, the Company commissioned an inquiry into its causes. The **Wells Inquiry** found that the "primary
contributory factor" in the ground's subsidence was **illegal wells** dug by residents of the eastern districts in the years
of water shortage before the flood. Forty residents who had held well permits revoked in the Y40s were named. The **Wells
Forty**, those who had survived, were charged under the Calder Recovery Authority's emergency powers (still in force then) with
"contributing to a public catastrophe", and fined amounts that became debts. Their debts passed under Clause 77 to their
households, and many of them are still on the arrears register, owed by grandchildren who were born after the flood.

Everybody knows the wells were not the reason. Everybody also knows the Inquiry is the official account. The Wells Forty's
names are carved, illegally, on a beam in the Co-op hall on 21, and painted over by the Company once a year.

**In play:** a client named in the Wells Forty, a grandchild of one of them, comes with a contract that is not a contract: "My
grandfather's debt is 1,830 cr. If you see it on the Register for sale, buy it." Debts can be bought from collectors (if the
economy file lets it). Buying a Wells debt and tearing it up is one of the small human things a player can do with money.

### The Meter Strike (Y71)

In Y71 the Company replaced the **founding-era water meters** on floors 10 to 29 (which had worked for seventy years) with
new ones, citing the old ones' "age". The new meters read, on average, 18% higher. The Mills knew within a week, because the Old
Hands still had founding-era test rigs. For forty-one days, every lease on 14 to 23 paid its rent and refused to pay its water.
The Company moderated service. The Mills shared water from the Co-op's stores and from stinkers. On day thirty-eight, the
Company sent HPS to remove the Co-op's water stores as "unlicensed storage" (Bylaw 39). On day forty-one, the strike ended in
an agreement: the new meters would stay, but read 10% lower "for a transitional period". The transitional period ended in Y73. So did the new meters: they failed in the damp within the year, and the Company quietly refitted the founding-era Sorrels it had kept in store.

What people remember: the Old Hands' test rigs. Three of them still exist. Each has been offered for sale to the Company,
and the Company has bought two and broken them.

**Hook:** the third test rig is owned by **Teodor Halas**, an Old Hand on 16, who rents it out for a single evening to any
tenant who wants to know what their meter really reads. He charges 15 cr. People pay it and then can't do anything with the
answer, except know. Halas thinks this is worth more than people realise.

**In play:** a meter is a machine on the player's field (canon). A **meter test** with Halas's rig, as a one-time paid
service, can reveal the meter's **true multiplier** (a hidden stat shown as "?" until analysed, in the spirit of the
analyser idea). What the player does with knowing it is up to them: a petition, a Co-op complaint, a quiet bypass (B33,
Class A).

### The Gutter Case (Y86)

The most recent famous case (A/86/0204, §7): Skywater and the Company against the Rain Church, over rain caught on the
outside of the building. The ruling's last line ("The Office notes it cannot restrain the weather") is the most-quoted sentence
in the tower this year. The Rain Church read it as the Arbiter's quiet joke on their side. HPS read it as permission to seize
every vessel on every ledge. Sixty ledge-buckets were taken in the first week. The Rain Church has since started collecting
from inside the Crown's own gutters, which is a different and much worse offence.

### In re Bin Four (Y81)

The insect case (A/81/0217). Orrin's stock escaped and interbred with the Co-op's, and the Arbiter held that every insect
carrying the marker was Orrin's, with retroactive fees. After it, every unregistered insect rancher in the Mills began
testing their stock with marker swabs bought on the grey market, and culling any line that tested positive. A strain that
tests clean is now worth more than one that breeds well. The best-known clean line is **Seventeen Black**, a beetle bred on
floor 17 by a rancher named **Iolo Brask**, whose grandmother carried the stock up from a Works storeroom on 3 the night the water came in, Y57.
Black larvae, slow, reliable, and **legally nobody's**.

**In play:** insect stock as an item with a legal property: **registered** (Orrin's, fees, Vitabrick), **clean** (unregistered
and marker-free: pressings, safe from Bin Four claims), or **marked** (unregistered but carrying the marker: a liability; a
grader's swab turns it into an Orrin bin and a backdated bill). A player buying cheap stock from an unknown seller may be
buying a marked line. A marker swab (1x1, consumable) is the analyser for this.

### The Eleventh Floor Adjustment (Y83)

In Y83 the Company decided that the residential half of floor 11 would be rebuilt as **storage leases** for the Middle (households above 30 renting
squares below to keep what they don't have room for). Every lease on 11 was given a **reassignment notice** in the same week.
Some went down to 6 to 9. Some went to hot-bunks on 12. A few refused, petitioned, and lost (A/83/0400 to A/83/0471, seventy-one
determinations, identical text). The storage leases on 11 are now full of Middle furniture, Middle winter clothing, and
Middle children's outgrown toys, behind roller doors, while the people who lived there sleep two floors down in hot-bunks.

**In play:** this is the canon "rent climbs as the upper floors expand downward" made into one remembered event, and it can
happen again. A **storage conversion** notice for a floor near the player's is a mid-game shock: the rent on adjacent floors
jumps, the chute table of the converted floor becomes "storage clear-outs" (better goods, irregularly), and a lot of new
contracts appear from Middle households wanting things moved into storage. The gentrification of the Stack happens one
floor at a time, and each one has a case number.

**Hook:** a storage lease on 11 has not been opened in two years. The renter's monthly fee is still paid, by a Crown family's
counsel. Children on 11 say there's a smell. The Lease Office says that storage leases are private.

## 15. The law in play

Everything above, gathered into the shapes it could take in the game. These are proposals, consistent with the rules in
`IDEAS.md`: no proximity effects, no hidden untrackable numbers, space as the constraint, machines and special containers as
the only places with rules.

### A note on what was dropped

`IDEAS.md` drops "a guard spotting stolen goods at the counter" as too close to Probably Stolen's heat-and-inspection system.
Everything here avoids that shape. There is no heat meter that fills as you sell, and no guard watching the counter. The law
in this world is **paperwork that arrives**: scheduled and unscheduled visits that look at **specific kinds of container**
(meters, water machines, insect bins, grow beds, shelves) for **specific item statuses**, and leave slips behind. Enforcement
is an event with a clear cause, a clear target and a clear bill, more like weather than a guard.

### Item status

As in §4: every item can have one legal status beside its grade: **licensed**, **grey**, **void**, **restored**,
**counterfeit**. Plus a few specific statuses for chains: **unlicensed water** (vs licensed water), **pressings** (vs
Vitabrick), **uncertified produce** (vs real-grade), **marked / clean / registered** insect stock, **salvage (Widows-marked)**
vs **salvage (unmarked)**. Each shows as a corner mark. Nothing is hidden: the player always knows what is legal.

### Enforcement events

| Event | Trigger | What it looks at | Typical outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Courtesy inspection** (WWO / Orrin) | scheduled; a notice slip arrives the morning before with an hour | the licensed machines: plates, seals, registration tags | a visit fee; a pass, or a charge for a lapsed licence |
| **Unannounced inspection** | random, weighted by band and the bylaws "under review" | one container type (water machines, or bins, or shelves) | grey or unlicensed items in that container type are charged and retired |
| **Baseline review** | the lease's metered water draw is far below its expected draw | all water-producing machines | unlicensed apparatus is tagged and removed; a big charge |
| **Stamper visit** | random, weighted by how many restored/void items have been sold from the shelves recently | shop shelves and the counter's display containers | restored and counterfeit items seized at list value; or feed the stamper |
| **Occupancy count** | warden report | registered heads vs people on the lease | per-head charge for ghosts |
| **Collector visit** | the arrears ladder, day 7 | the whole field, highest value per square first | yellow tags with a deadline |
| **Lease adjustment** | arrears, day 10 and 14 | the field's edge | a row removed; items on it go to the Recovery Store |
| **Lane scan** | a drone passes a band gate on a licensed lane | the drone's bay | grey/void/restored/counterfeit items above the band they're allowed in: seized with the drone; or bribed |
| **Sweep** | rare, Sump only, announced through the Ninefold | the Sump leases | stills and rigs taken; a known name or two taken into custody |

Every event is announced by a slip or a notice. The player is never blindsided without a rule to point at. "Unannounced"
inspections still come with a knock: a visible hour's warning in the time bar ("Tasters on 15, coming up."), which is an
hour in which the player can **do something**: move things into a closed container, pack them into a drone and launch it,
sell them at the counter, or bribe.

### Hiding, honestly

Hiding is packing. Inspectors look at specific container types, so:

- **Closed containers they don't open** without cause: a **false-bottom crate** (a crate whose inner space is smaller than its
  outside: a 3x3 crate with a 2x2 hidden compartment), a **sealed water drum** that looks like Halden Pure storage, a
  **Ninefold-sealed** salvage crate (in the Sump band only).
- **Things that aren't here**: a drone in flight is off the field. A player who launches a drone full of grey goods an hour
  before an inspection has hidden them in the sky, and the cost is a launch, a route and a reason (a contract, or a round
  trip to nowhere).
- **Things that are legal for now**: grey items are fine below 30. An inspection in the Mills only cares about grey items in
  the specific circumstances that make them crimes: unlicensed water **offered for sale** (on a shelf), pressings **offered for
  sale** (on a shelf), restored goods **with intent to sell** (on a shelf). The same items in a storage crate are possessions.
  So the shelf is where the law looks, and the back room is where life goes on. This is canon-friendly and needs no hidden
  numbers: the container type is the rule.

### Drone routes and the law

Licensed lanes are safe from looters and **scanned** at band gates (30, 60, 90). Free shafts are unscanned and **unmanaged**
(Bylaw 63: anything in them is abandoned, which is why looting there is not theft). So the route choice at launch is:

- **Licensed lane**: pay the fee or carry a permit; legal goods arrive safe; grey goods above their band are seized at the
  gate (drone and all) unless a bribe slip is packed in the bay, or the bay is sealed (sealed bays get a scan fee and a
  manual check, and a manual check is bribable).
- **Free shaft**: free; no scan; a roll on landing for tolls or looting (one item lost); and goods arriving at a band above
  that won't accept them on paper still have to get past the **client**, who may be careful (no papers, no pay) or not
  (pays less, asks nothing).

**In play:** the contract slip says what it accepts: "Licensed only. Provenance slip required." or "No questions." Upper
contracts mostly want papers; lower contracts mostly don't. The middle contracts are the interesting ones: they want papers
and pay for them, but some clients will take a counterfeit at a lower price, and some of those clients are stampers
running a sting.

### Paper as items

The law fills the inventory with paper. Paper is light and small, but it is not nothing, and some of it is required:

- **The Charter** (2x3; bound Charter 1x2): required on the lease (B6).
- **Licences**: blue plate (1x1, fitted to a water machine), Orrin registration tag (1x1, fitted to a bin), Trader's
  Licence (2x1 framed, on a wall container), Lane Permit (1x1, packed in a drone's bay), Reclamation Licence (2x1 framed).
- **Provenance slip** (1x1): issued with licensed goods; travels with them; required by upper contracts. A forged provenance
  slip is a counterfeit of a document, and a nice product for a late-game grey operation.
- **Storage Notice** (1x1): lets the lease hold more than 40 l of water (B39).
- **Assembly Notice**, **Band pass**, **Day pass** (1x1): situational.
- **Incident number** (1x1): the useless product of Essential cover; needed for any insurance claim.
- **Notices and cards**: pink card, red card, yellow tag, Recovery Notice, custody bill, standards charge form. Each is a
  bill that persists until paid; binning one doesn't cancel it (the card goes, the charge stays), which the tooltip says
  plainly.
- **Form A-1** (1x2): needed to petition the Arbiter.
- **Precedent copies** (1x1 each): copies of determinations, which improve odds for a petition on the same clause.
- **Foundation receipts** (1x1): proof of bribes; evidence and leverage.
- **Ninefold seal** (1x1 wax stamp): applied to a crate; paid-and-untouchable in the Sump.

The more legal you are, the more paper you carry, and paper takes squares. The Crown's advantage is literally that its paper
is kept by counsel, somewhere else, in somebody else's squares.

### A week of the law on floor 17 (sample)

How it could feel, as a run of slips across seven in-game days, for a player running a grey-ish shop with one unplated
still, an unregistered insect bin, and a shelf of restored coats.

- **Day 1, 08:00.** Lobby board: *Bylaw 39 under review this month.* The player moves two water drums into a drone and
  sends them to a Co-op client on 21 who pays in produce.
- **Day 2, 11:00.** A drone note from the WWO clerk ("courtesy, 10 cr"): tasters on 17 on Day 4. The player pays.
- **Day 3, 14:00.** A stamper buys a restored coat from the shelf, pays full price, and leaves. Nothing happens.
- **Day 4, 09:00.** Tasters arrive at 15:00 per the note. The still is packed into the false-bottom crate (it fits; it's a
  small still; that's why it was bought). The tap draw for the week is low; the taster frowns at the meter, says "Your
  household drinks very little," and writes a letter of concern. No charge.
- **Day 5, 10:00.** The stamper comes back with a recovery sack. The coat he bought is evidence. He finds two more on the
  shelf. Standards charge: 40 cr × 3, plus the brand's recovery at Crown list (300 cr), or feed him one coat a week. The
  player feeds him.
- **Day 6, 19:00.** Hester's contract on the board: "a balance wheel from a pocket watch, any condition." The chute had one
  yesterday. The player sends it down the corridor. The Warden's regard pip ticks up.
- **Day 7, 08:00.** Rent day. The player is 30 cr short. Pink card. They take a "No questions" contract to the Sump, 12
  pressings and 10 l of unlicensed water by free shaft, and a Ninefold seal comes back in the bay with the pay.

Nothing in that week is combat. Everything in it is a choice about space, time, paper and risk.

### Smallest version

If only a little of this goes in: **item status marks** (licensed / grey / void / restored), **contracts that name accepted
statuses**, **lane scans** on the licensed route, **the arrears ladder** with a row removed at the end, and **one inspection
type** that looks at shelves. That's enough for the law to be felt in every decision, with a lore reason under each rule.
