# 08. Daily life and language

Speculative. Plain text is the main version; **Alternative:** marks another version of the same thing; **Hook:** marks a
mystery or story seed; **In play:** marks where it surfaces in the game. Prices are in cr and agree with `04-economy.md`;
places agree with `02-the-stack.md`; water customs are in `03-water.md` and are only referred to here, not repeated.

This file is about how the people of the Stack live: what they eat, when they sleep, how they cough, how they bury each
other, what they bet on and what they call things. It starts with ten days in ten lives, one or more in every band, and
then works through the topics. The lives are written so their people can walk onto the contract board: every one of them
needs something, sells something, or knows something.

## Part one: ten days

### 1. Linnet Aubrey, seventeen, floor 95 (the Crown)

Linnet wakes at nine because the glass lets the light in at nine. The windows of the Aubrey apartment on 95 are founding-era
photovoltaic, the same skin that powers the Crown, and they are set to clear from smoke-grey to white over twenty minutes,
which the household's steward, **Mr. Hallam Prest**, calls "a dawn". There is a real dawn outside, over the bay, two hours
earlier. Nobody in the Aubrey household has seen it in years.

The Aubreys hold a third of Orrin Provisions. Linnet has never eaten a Vitabrick. She knows exactly what one looks like
because her grandmother's portrait in the dining room is painted holding one, the first brick off the Orrin press in Y59,
presented to the family by the factory manager "in gratitude". The portrait is called *Provision*. Linnet thinks it is the
funniest painting in the tower.

Breakfast is laid in the morning room by **Odran**, who is not a waiter but the household's **disposal valet**: his real job
is to make sure that nothing leaves the apartment whole. He lays the table because the family likes to see him do something
pleasant. Breakfast is a soft-boiled egg (one of the two hundred hens in the tower; this one from the Glasshouses' run on 86),
toast from bread baked downstairs in the Pantry an hour ago, three slices of Greenhold pear, and water from the roof. Linnet
eats the egg and half the toast. The rest goes back on the tray, and the tray goes to Odran, and Odran stands at the service
sink for four minutes with a cobalt spray bottle of **Orrin Blue**, misting the half-slice, the pear, the crusts, until they
are the colour of a warning, because the household does not send down food that someone could eat. He does this with care,
as with everything. Everyone has a job.

Lessons are at ten: **Dr. Imelda Fawcett**, tutor, who comes up from 81 every morning (the Locals down to 60, the Glass Bank up to 90, a day pass at the Ninety Gate), teaches her history from the
Company's own *A City Stood On End* and economics from the Orrin annual report. Today's question is the cost of a protein
brick at the press house door (0.41 cr) against its price at the Hatch on 18 (3 cr). Linnet works out the margin. She is good
at this. Dr. Fawcett gives her full marks and does not mention that she grew up on 52.

In the afternoon Linnet swims in the Long Bath on 99 for an hour, alone in thirty metres of warm water under glass, and then
lies on the deck reading a paper novel from the Assembly library and listening to the rain on the Founders' Gutter. At 16:00 the
**Marchetti girls** come over from 94, and the three of them talk about the thing everyone under twenty in the Crown is talking about
this season: **crossing**. Mills clothes. The X slash that disposal valets cut into voided shirts, patched over by Mills menders
in contrasting thread, has been seen on a singer at the Pleasure Pier. Now everyone wants a crossed shirt. A real one, not one
made new with a fake X sewn on. The real ones have to come up from the Mills, through a fixer, and the fixers charge 300 cr a
shirt and do not say where they got them.

Linnet has a better idea. She goes down to the kitchen and finds **Roz**, the Pantry cook (see 10), and asks her, sweetly, if
she knows anyone in the Mills.

Dinner is at eight with her father, who talks to her about the Orrin Real-Blend trial, and with her mother, who does not talk.
There are nine courses and the Aubreys eat about a third of each. At 22:00 the Crown's quiet rule begins and the drop doors on
the North Chute are locked until morning. Everything the family did not eat that day sits blued in sealed bags in the service
corridor, waiting for six o'clock, when it will fall a hundred floors and arrive in the Mills as the best food anyone there
will see that week, provided they are willing to wash off the blue.

Linnet goes to sleep thinking about the shirt. She is not unkind. She has never been anywhere where kindness was a cost.

**In play:**
- **Crossed goods go up.** A recurring Crown contract type in a "fashion season" event: *one crossed shirt, real Mills mend,
  any size, delivered to the Postern under staff consignment.* The player can mend a voided shirt from the North Chute (sewing
  kit, 6 cr) and send it back up to the floor it fell from, for fifty times what it is worth in the Mills. The Crown Assay
  does not reject it, because it is not food and not water. The joke is the loop: the shirt was slashed so it could never
  undercut new stock, and now it sells for more than new stock.
- **Linnet as client** is a small arc: her slips are written in violet ink on cream card, misspell the Mills' aisle names,
  and ask for things she has heard about (a crust jug, a cricket in a jar, a real Vitabrick, "a Mills song"). They pay well
  and come with a reward bay of Crown castoffs that were never voided because she took them before Odran could. Each slip
  delivered raises the chance she sends another; one too many and her father's steward closes the channel and a Brand
  Integrity agent visits the player about "unlicensed resale of Orrin product".
- **Hook:** Linnet's sums are right. One evening she asks her father why a brick costs 0.41 to make and 3 to buy, and he
  tells her it is because of "transport and handling". She looks up the Lift Guild freight rate. It does not add up. She
  starts writing her questions in a notebook, and the notebook will eventually want to go somewhere.

### 2. Yetunde Farr, thirty-four, nurse, Vey Sixty-Four (the Terraces)

Yetunde lives on 44 with her mother in the flat she grew up in, a four-room inner flat whose light pipe died when she was
twelve. She has a Locals pass (monthly, 90 cr, deducted at source) and a Sixty Gate staff card, and she leaves at 06:10 to be
on the ward by 07:00, because the Locals stop everywhere and at 06:30 every car is full of Middle office staff going up.

She is a clinic nurse at Vey Sixty-Four, which pays 140 cr a day. That is more than anyone in her family has ever earned. Her
mother tells everyone on the corridor. Her mother does not tell anyone that half of it goes on the flat, a quarter on Yetunde's
own training debt to Vey (eight years left), and that the flat's Standing has been one late rent away from **Adjusted** since
her father died.

The first two hours of her day are on the **plasma desk**, by the back lift on 64. This is not the job she trained for. It is
the job that new nurses on the clinic's books are given, and she has been "new" for six years. The donors come up the back lift
from the Mills, ticketed through the Turnstile by the plasma buyers, sixteen at a time. She knows half of them by their veins.
She checks their cards (two donations a month, punched), reads their pressure, looks at their gums (blue line: refuse, Vey
does not want the metals in its product; the donor goes down unpaid and furious), and puts a needle in. Forty minutes each.
They are paid 15 cr by the buyer at the bottom of the lift; the clinic pays the buyer 40; St. Ober's Spire pays the clinic
something she has never been told.

She has a sentence for every donor who is too pale: "Your veins are tired. Come back next month." It is the kindest thing she
is allowed to say. The donors hear it as "no money this week".

At 09:00 she goes up to the **lung lounge** on 65, where Terraces residents lie in reclining chairs and breathe bottled
"altitude air" from masks for their health at 60 cr an hour, and she adjusts flow rates and listens to them complain about the
Gills. One of them, a Bureau supervisor called **Mr. Kettering**, has the Mills cough, which he got as an inspector twenty years
ago, and pays to breathe air that is very slightly cleaner than the air of his own flat. He asks her where she is from. She
says the Middle. He says "Ah," the way Terraces people say it.

Lunch is a Real-Blend tin in the staff room. She eats it slowly because it has a real pea in it, sometimes.

The afternoon is day surgery: dressings, drips, a Terraces child's tonsils. At 16:00 she does the thing every nurse at Vey
Sixty-Four does and nobody discusses. In the stock room there is a drawer called **the short drawer**: stock that will expire
inside thirty days and cannot be sold to Terraces patients ("Vey guarantees freshness"). By policy it goes down the South Chute
in sealed tubes. By custom, each nurse takes something home first. Yetunde takes gut tabs and wound film, never antibiotics,
because the DoseLock dispensers log their own opening. In her bag, under her uniform, she carries 20 cr of medicine down the
Long Stair every evening. Her mother's corridor on 44 gets its gut tabs at a quarter of the Vey price, and the corridor knows
not to ask.

She is home at 19:40. Her mother has a pot of noodle brick with an onion in it, a real onion, because it is the seventh day
and on 44 the seventh day has an onion. They write the day's water in the **water book** together. They watch *Climb* on the
corridor screen. Yetunde falls asleep in her chair before the final question.

**In play:**
- **Yetunde as supplier:** short-drawer medicine, offered through the counter or a slip, at grey prices. Real Vey goods,
  short-dated, legal to own and illegal to sell. They expire in-game within days: a time-pressure stock that sells fast
  down the tower.
- **Yetunde as client:** she sends slips for things the Middle cannot buy openly: a **crust jug** for her mother's birthday, a
  **mended DoseLock dispenser** she can refill with short-drawer pills, a real herb for the seventh-day onion's pot.
- **The plasma desk** is where a Mills donor client's "too pale" becomes a contract: a donor who was refused comes to the
  player's counter wanting iron-rich food (real greens, mushrooms, cricket paste) to pass next month. Food as medicine, sold
  down.
- **Hook:** Yetunde has noticed that donors with blue gums are refused at the desk but their samples are still taken, labelled
  and sent to St. Ober's in a separate cold box marked **CKP** (the Calder Kidney Programme in 03). She has started keeping a
  list of the names. She does not know why.

### 3. Anselm Dray, fifty-two, licensed repairer, floor 46 (the Middle)

The sign on the door says **DRAY & DAUGHTER. LICENSED REPAIRS. Repair Licence RL-46-0071.** The daughter, **Cori**, is sixteen and
at the Halden School on 50, on a fee that costs the Drays a year's rent each year. The "& Daughter" went on the sign the week
she passed the exam. Anselm painted it himself at night, so nobody on the corridor would see him crying.

He opens at 08:00. His shop is twelve squares on a corridor front on 46, at 26 cr a square, plus a Repair Licence fee of 30 cr
a month and a register of every third-party item on his premises, which an inspector reads twice a year. He repairs small
appliances: lamps, hotplates, radios, kettles, the cheap screens Middle families buy to watch *Climb*. Halden goods only, and
only "within licence", which means he may replace a fuse and re-solder a joint but may not open a sealed unit, and almost
everything Halden makes is a sealed unit.

So Anselm has a second business, and it is the real one. Every Thursday a boy comes up the Long Stair from the Mills with a
delivery slip that lets him through the Turnstile, and a crate on his back. In the crate are sealed units that have been opened,
repaired and resealed in the Mills, by people Anselm has never met, with heat-guns and resin, so neatly that only a Spectro wand
can find the seam. Anselm pays 30% of the new price. He fits each one in front of the customer, on the bench, as if he had just
repaired it, and charges 70%. On the slip he writes "unit reconditioned under licence". His register says the same.

He knows exactly what he is doing. He does it because the licence pays for the shop and the second business pays for Cori.

At 11:00 a woman from 48 brings a hotplate. At 12:00 a Protective from 52, off duty, brings a radio and asks without looking
at him whether it can be made to pick up "the other station". Anselm says it is not something a licensed repairer does. The
Protective nods and leaves the radio. Anselm will send it down to the Mills on Thursday with a note that says *Static, quiet
dial*, and it will come back next week with a second tuning knob behind the back panel, and he will charge the Protective
45 cr, and the Protective will pay without a word.

Lunch is a Vitabrick "Savoury", which he cuts into eight fingers and eats two at a time between jobs, with Kof from the kiosk
on the corner. In the Middle you do not eat a whole brick in one sitting at work; it looks like the Mills.

At 15:00 the corridor lights blink twice: power shed rotation, his quarter, one hour. He has a cell rack for the bench and a
battery lamp, and he keeps working, because a shop that closes during the shed is a shop that cannot afford cells, and people
notice.

At 19:00 he closes and goes home to the flat behind the shop (illegal; his lease is Commercial; Hester Moyle's opposite number
on 46, a warden called **Mrs. Pruett**, has known for eleven years and takes a kettle repair a year for free). He and Cori eat
together. She tells him about the Placement lists. He tells her about the hotplate. He does not tell her about the Thursday
boy. She already knows.

At night he does the water book and the shop ledger, and then the third book, which he keeps under the floor tile: a list of
every sealed unit he has sold that came up from the Mills, with the customer's name. If a Bureau agent ever comes, the first
book saves the licence, the second saves the lease, and the third is the one he will burn.

**In play:**
- **Anselm is the player's first Middle client** for restored goods: a standing weekly contract, *Thursday crate, sealed
  units, resealed, no visible seam*. Pay is good and in credits. The risk is not on the player's side until a **Bureau
  sweep** event on 46 turns up a unit with the player's work in it; then Anselm's third book matters, and whether he burns it
  is a relationship stat.
- **The Static knob** is a small product: a receiver with a hidden second dial, for Middle households who must not be seen to
  listen. It sells up, quietly.
- **Hook:** Cori Dray will sit the Placement exam this year. Her best subject is "Reconditioning Practice", where she has
  learned to spot a resealed unit at a glance. The Company is very interested in children with that skill. Brand protection
  agents are recruited from the Halden School.

### 4. Dolores "Dee" Kasprzak, forty-seven, insect rancher, floor 12 (the Mills)

Dee's day begins at 04:30, before the Whistle, with her hand flat on the side of a bin.

She runs **Kasprzak's**, fourteen insect bins in a forty-square pen of Hall Twelve-East, on the old cell line between Coater Run
and the core, a pen that smells of warm nuts, ammonia and wet cardboard, and that you can find with your eyes closed from the
lift lobby. The bins are steel cabinets on legs, each a grid of trays, each tray a colony of crickets on egg-flats. The first
thing she does every morning is touch each bin and read the warmth with her palm. Thirty-one degrees is right. Twenty-eight
and the crickets stop eating. Thirty-five and they start eating each other.

At 06:00 the Whistle blows and the Morning Fall roars down the East Chute four metres from her pen. Dee holds a one-in-six catch
on the Kitchen, which is the most expensive thing she owns (15 cr a week, every week, for nine years), and it is why she is still
in business. The catch bin is full of Lantern Row's night: peel, rinds, stale bread, cold noodles, bones, half a cake, a
smashed tray of blued rolls from Orrin's test kitchen on 80. Her son **Piotr**, fifteen, sorts it on a mesh table while it is
still warm. Bread and sweet things go to their neighbour **Malka**, who brews crust, in exchange for frass collection. Bones
go to the Co-op's bone-meal grinder. Plastic, foil and anything metal goes in a sack for a picker. The rest, the soft food,
goes into the feed hopper. Blue does not matter to crickets.

Her daughter **Ania**, nine, counts. Every morning she takes one tray from one bin, at random, and counts the adults in a
marked square, and writes the number on the bin's slate. It is the only way to see a crash coming. An Orrin Protein Starter
colony crashes after six generations, which is how Orrin sells you another. Dee's colonies are **wild-line**, bred from
crickets her mother brought up from the Sump in a jar in Y58, and they have never crashed. People ask her for a jar of starter
every week. She sells them at 15 cr and knows the buyers will be back in a year, because they will let a bin get cold.

From 07:00 to 13:00 she works. Harvest: three trays a day are tipped into a sieve, the crickets go into a chill box to slow
them, then into the **scalder** (a founding-era tank that used to rinse cell plates), then onto drying racks. Bedding and frass
are shovelled into sacks: the frass goes to the Co-op beds on 21 as fertiliser and to the Tide Folk, who make poultices from it.
Spent egg-flats go to the mushroom grower in the next pen, who grows oyster mushrooms on them in the dark. Nothing in Hall
Twelve-East is thrown away; it is passed along the aisle.

At 13:00 the paste. The dried crickets go through a grinder that she rents by the hour from the Co-op Crib, because a grinder
costs 220 cr and she would rather have the money in the bins. Paste goes into five-kilo tubs. Twice a week the tubs go to the
**Halloway press house** on 11, an Orrin contract press with a licensed Vitabrick die, which pays her 3.20 cr a kilo. The rest
she presses herself, on a hand press, into **slab**: unbranded, any shape but a Vitabrick's. She sells slab on the Ledge and to
the hot-bunk houses. Her slab is shaped like a flattened egg, so that it cannot be mistaken for a brick by a Food Standards
inspector, and every Mills child on 12 knows a Kasprzak egg.

At 15:00 she sleeps. At 19:00 she is up again for the evening tap hours, filling the bins' water trays from the aisle tap post
with her key-meter clicking. She does not sleep at night. Nobody who keeps live stock in the Mills sleeps at night, because the
socket board is shed at slack hour and the bins' heaters stop, and someone has to be there to swap the cells in. Her joke is
that she has not slept in the dark since she was married. Her husband **Marek** died of the wet chest in Y80. She has not slept in
the dark since then either.

The **chirp** in Hall Twelve-East is a sound you stop hearing after a week. Dee hears it all the time. A bin that goes quiet is
a bin that is dying, and she will wake from a sound sleep at the silence.

**In play:**
- **Dee is a supplier and a teacher.** Her wild-line starter colony (15 cr) is the best first insect stock in the game; her
  slab is a cheap bulk good the player can resell or pack down the tower; she buys any **food waste** in sacks.
- **Her rhythms are the insect chain's rhythms:** warmth (a machine slot), feed from the chute (the East Chute is the right
  trunk), water drawn hourly, and slack hour as the danger. A **Slack at Night** event (slack hour falls in the night block)
  is where a player with a cell rack keeps their bins alive and a player without one loses a generation.
- **A crash contract:** a neighbour's Orrin colony crashed. *Wanted: one wild-line starter, delivered today, 25 cr and two kilos
  of paste back when it recovers.* Dee will sell the player a jar to pass on, and remember whether they paid her.
- **Hook:** Dee's mother's jar came from the Sump. The crickets that survived the Drowning in the drowned plant's canteen
  storerooms had lived for weeks on warm floodwater and spoiled food. They are the toughest line in the tower. Orrin's lab on
  80 has asked her, twice, to "lend" a tray for analysis. She has said no twice. The third request will come with a lawyer.

### 5. Mikkel Arno, twenty-six, night crew, sleeps on floor 17 (the Mills and the Terraces)

Mikkel sleeps First: 06:00 to 14:00, bunk nine, **Tanaka-Breen's**, the hot-bunk house at the far end of 17 that sleeps twenty in
eight squares, three shifts a day. He pays the aired-bunk rate, 3 cr, because he will not lie down in someone else's warmth. He
has never seen the face of the man who sleeps in bunk nine on Graveyard. He knows him only by his smell (Kof and solder) and by
the fact that he leaves the sheet turned down and the pillow flipped, which, in a hot-bunk house, is a kind of love.

The rules on the door are the ones on every hot-bunk door in the tower (04): no cooking, no water on the bunk, no visitors, no
refunds after the first hour, and anything not in a locker belongs to the next shift. Mikkel's locker is 1 cr a day and holds
everything he owns: two shirts, a pair of founding-era boots that will outlive him, his Glasshouses staff card, a tin of seeds
he should not have.

The Graveyard sleepers come out at 06:00 with the Whistle, and the bunks are turned by **Gus Tanaka-Breen**'s keeper, a woman
called **Sabine**, who flips mattresses, airs sheets on a line over the corridor for the hour between shifts, and in the same
hour empties the drip trays under the house's two air-wells. Thirty sleepers breathe a litre an hour into a closed room. Sabine
pours the night's breath into a cask, filters it, and sells it back at the hatch for 0.20 cr a cup. Mikkel buys a cup before he
lies down. He calls it, as everyone does, **buying his own breath**.

He wakes at 14:00 to the Whistle and the clatter of Second coming in. Washes at the Co-op drip room (0.50 cr, a bucket of warm
soft grey). Eats a Kasprzak egg and a cup of crust on the Corner Stair landing. Goes to the clinic on 19 every second week for
the wound on his hand. At 18:00 he queues for Old Two to 30; at 19:40 he queues for the Locals; at 21:30 he is on 84, in the
**Glasshouses**, in white overalls and a hairnet, with a staff card that lets him past the Sixty Gate one way at one time each
night and logs him back down in the morning.

His job is the **cull**. Everything in the Glasshouses is grown under licence from Greenhold, and Greenhold's licence says that no
plant leaves the Glasshouses alive and no seed leaves at all. After harvest, Mikkel pulls the spent plants from their trays
(tomatoes on strings, lettuces, herbs), feeds them into a macerator, and hoses the green pulp into the West Chute in sealed bags.
Eight hours of it. A tomato vine that is still flowering goes in with the rest. He has fed the macerator about a hundred thousand
plants.

At 05:30 a supervisor runs a wand over the night crew and searches their pockets, boots and hairnets at the door. Mikkel has
learned that the wand looks for metal and the hands look in pockets. Nobody looks inside a man's cheek. Once a month, never more,
he walks out of the Glasshouses with four seeds under his tongue and does not speak to anyone on the Locals all the way down.

He does not sell them. Not yet. He keeps them in the tin in his locker at Tanaka-Breen's, which is the stupidest place in the
tower to keep anything, because the next shift owns what is not locked, and the lock is Sabine's. He knows a seed saver on 21
would pay 40 cr for five saved tomato seeds and 400 for a named heirloom. He knows what Greenhold does to people. He has not
decided what he is for.

He gets back to 17 at 06:00, as the Morning Fall roars. Bunk nine is still warm. He waits on the corridor for the hour it takes
to air, because he will not lie in someone else's warmth, and he watches the West Chute's catches bang open on the floor's
far side and wonders whose bin his night's pulp will fall into.

**In play:**
- **The hot-bunk house next door** is a neighbour and a client: Tanaka-Breen's buys crust by the cask, sheets from the chute,
  air-well servicing, and drip trays; it is also the floor's biggest source of warden reports (Hester Moyle files against it
  four times a month), so a hot-bunk raid event on 17 is a regular disruption: HPS occupancy counts on the corridor, the
  Turnstile slowed, customers scarce for a morning.
- **Mikkel's tin** is a branching contract. He offers the player Glasshouses seed: unlicensed, fertile, patented. Buy it and
  the player can grow Greenhold varieties that sell up as certified (once, until Greenhold's agents notice), or sell them to the
  seed savers (06b) for standing. Refuse and he goes to someone else. Report him and the player gets a Company reward in scrip
  and a hot-bunk house full of people who know.
- **West Chute pulp** from the Glasshouses is an actual chute material: a green, wet, 2x2 sack of macerated plant that feeds an
  insect bin well and contains, very rarely, a live seed (the hook in 02). Mikkel's job is why that sack exists.

### 6. Nell Gauntlett, thirty-eight, fixer, Gutter Row, floor 7 (the Sump)

Nell's stall is a steel cage on Gutter Row, between a cell-charger who works off a cable nobody admits to and a man who sells
cracked DoseLock doses out of a biscuit tin. The founding-era drain gutter runs open along the wall behind her, ankle deep and
fast at ebb, slow and rising at flood, and she keeps everything she cares about on shelves above the height of the tidemark on
the next pillar, which is at her knee.

She wakes at 05:00 because the Sump wakes with the tide, not the Whistle. Today low water is at 05:52, and at low water the
Flats come up the outer stair to Sixes with salvage, and a fixer who wants first look at the cells and fittings has to be on
the 9 landing when the Ninefold open the toll. She is there at 05:40 with a tin of chits and a cell gauge. A Flats boy has a
sack of eight (always eight; the ninth went to the Ninefold): two dead H-cells, a coil of copper, three cracked filter
housings, a brass valve, and a thing she doesn't recognise, the size of a fist, heavy, with a founding-era Halden number cast
into it. She buys the lot for 14 chits and the boy is pleased, because the Lander would have paid 9. She does not tell him what
she paid for. She does not know herself yet.

Back at the cage by 07:00. Standpipe hour on 7 is 07:00 to 08:00, and Nell's son **Rafe**, twelve, is in the queue with two
jerrycans. Nell does not drink standpipe water and does not let Rafe drink it either; it goes through one of her own filters
first, the one on the shelf by the bunk that she will never sell.

Her trade is **grey filters**. A Halden filter housing only accepts a Halden cartridge with a live 30-day strip, and refuses to
open its valve when the strip runs red. Nell builds **grey housings**: the same body, the strip reader bypassed, the cartridge
seat bored out so it takes any cartridge, red-strip or not, and half the cartridges that come down the chute with red strips
are still good for a month. She sells a grey housing for 30 cr in the Sump and 40 to anyone from the Mills, which is most of her
customers, because a Mills household that can't afford a fresh cartridge every 30 days can afford one of hers once.

She also re-terminates cells (Halden clips the leads of cells it retires; a jig and a soldering iron put them back) and
she listens. A fixer on Gutter Row is the person people come to when something breaks and they cannot pay a licensed fitter,
and so she hears everything first: whose pump is failing, which still is about to be raided, who is going on the Bastion boat
on Thursday.

At 11:00 the **pump horn** goes on 5, three long blasts. A Pumpmen call. Nell locks the cage, wades down the 6 stair to the
bilge room with everyone else on Gutter Row who can stand, and hauls on a hand pump for forty minutes in black water to the
thigh while the Pumpmen swap a dead motor. Nobody pays her. Not coming would cost her the pump tithe rebate and, worse, the
look on Old Sefton Marr's face. When it is done she goes back to the cage, wet to the waist, and works wet for the rest of the
day, because changing clothes is for people with two pairs of trousers.

In the afternoon she opens the heavy thing from the Flats. Inside is a founding-era valve actuator, still sealed, still oiled,
the kind the Old Hands call **first-fit**. She has seen two in her life. She wraps it in a rag and puts it under her bunk with
the filter she will never sell, and she will sit on it for a week before she decides whether to take it to the Canteen on 14,
where the Old Hands will tell her its worth and take a turn for it, or to a penthouse fixer, who will pay her more than her
cage makes in a year and never tell her where it went.

Her hands have started to shake. Not much. In the mornings. A fixer with the shakes is a fixer with five years left, and Nell
has been teaching a vent kid called **Sprat** to solder, slowly, because Rafe does not want to and she does not want him to
either.

At 21:00 she eats at the **Old Post** on 8: a plate of slab fried in insect fat with Flats salt and a cup of gin, 4 cr. The
Old Post's cage front keeps the drunks away from the bottles. Nobody in the Sump drinks Halden gin. They drink **Ninefold
white**, which is distilled from Mills crust dregs and tastes of bread and petrol.

**In play:**
- **Nell is the player's grey-goods vendor** in the Sump: grey filter housings, re-terminated cells, fixer services (unlock a
  bricked machine for a fee, takes hours), payable in chits and cells. A drone with a shopping list in its bay can reach her.
- **The first-fit actuator** is a contract seed: Nell will sell it, if the player has the money and the standing, or ask the
  player to broker it up the tower for a cut. It fits a specific founding-era machine (the player's, if they have the right one).
- **The pump call** is a Sump-wide event: for an hour, every Sump supplier is closed and every Sump contract is delayed. A
  player with a Sump lease has an hour of their own day taken.
- **Hook:** the fist-sized actuator has a Halden number with an extra digit no Old Hand has seen: a 5, in a series that runs 0
  to 4 in every Works catalogue. The Archivists have a page that lists a fifth production series. The page is torn below the
  heading.

### 7. Caddy Rourke, nineteen, magnet fisher, the Flats

Caddy's day is set by the moon. Her berth, **Tollgate**, eighteen people on the flat roof and top floor of the old Calder
tram depot, keeps a tide slate on the wall of the stair hut, and the reckoner, her uncle **Fergal**, chalks the day's two lows
and two highs on it every night. Today: low 05:52, high 12:04, low 18:21, high 00:30. Neap tides this week, so the deep
streets stay covered and the magnet fishing is close in, in the canals of Mercer Lane and around the half-sunk shopfronts on the
Strand.

She is up at 04:30 in the dark. Breakfast is half a slab bought at Sixes last week, softened in water, and a mouthful of crust
from the jug. Nobody in Tollgate eats anything that came out of the bay. That is the first rule a Flats child learns, before
any rule about tides: **nothing from the water goes in the mouth**. The bay is poison; the mussels on the tram depot's pillars
are fat and black and beautiful and would kill you by spring. Children are slapped for touching them, and later they stop
looking.

At 05:00 the ebb is draining fast. Caddy and her cousin **Brig** walk out on the mud in **splatchers** (planks strapped to their
boots, wide enough to spread the weight) along a line of stakes that Tollgate set years ago, to the edge of Mercer Lane where
the water stays waist deep at low tide over the old pavement. Her magnet is a Works lifting magnet the size of a dinner plate,
on twenty metres of rope, fished out of the Polishing Hall by a Widows diver in Y72 and bought by her mother with a winter's
salvage. It is the most valuable thing Tollgate owns.

She casts, drags, hauls. Bolts. Wire. A bicycle wheel. A tin of something sealed. A pair of scissors. A hinge. The first find of
the ebb goes back in the water without looking at it twice: **the Bay's share**. After that it is sorted into her shoulder sack
by a rule she could not explain if asked: does the Lander buy it, does a fixer buy it, does nobody buy it. Three empty casts in
a row and the magnet is "tired" and goes up on the roof to rest in the sun.

At 07:30 the flood turns. At 08:00 they are back. At 08:30 Caddy and her mother take the sack and the berth's other finds in
the punt to the **Landing** on 4, where **Ruben Vass** buys everything the Flats bring up at the prices he sets. Today he pays
11 chits for the lot. Most of Tollgate's money is chits with lifted seals, which the Mills will take at 80 and the Flats take at
face, because there is nobody to cash them with. Then the 9 landing, where the Ninefold take one in nine of what they have
bought back, and Sixes, where they spend it: four slabs, a 20-litre barrel of water at triple the Mills price, a tallow candle
pack, thread, Vey gut tabs. Sixes knows the Flats by their splatchers and charges them a little more because it can.

Caddy does not like the tower. It smells of people. Inside, the walls hold the air still and she can't hear the water. At Sixes
a Mills hawker calls her **mudfoot** to her face, the way they all do, and she smiles, the way they all do, and remembers his
face for later.

The afternoon is mending: net, rope, splatchers. At 18:21 the second low, shorter, poorer. At dusk Tollgate eats together on the
roof: slab fried in insect fat, a bowl of greens from the berth's one grow tarp (fed on rain caught in a sheet and on the
berth's own urine pails), and whatever luck has bought. At night the tower stands over them, a hundred and four floors of lit
windows going dark from the bottom up as slack hour sheds the Mills, and the Crown never dark at all. Fergal calls it **the
Nail**. The children call it the Candle.

Caddy goes to sleep listening to the tide come in under the depot and thinks about the tin she didn't open. It is in her sack.
It is sealed, and heavy, and has a Vey mark on it. The Lander didn't see it.

**In play:**
- **Flats clients pay by the tide.** Their slips are dated by low water ("by second low, Thursday"), they pay in chits
  (lifted seals), salvage and sometimes favours (a Tide Folk berth will "keep an eye out" for a named part), and they want
  slab, barrel water, gut tabs, rope, tallow, thread, filters.
- **The magnet** is a fixed point in the economy: a Flats berth that loses its magnet loses its living. A **Lost Magnet**
  event becomes a Flats contract: *find us a lifting magnet, any condition, pay: a month of first look at our finds.*
- **Salvage loads of eight:** goods bought through the Landing arrive in eights; buying direct from a berth by drone, skipping
  the Ninefold toll, gets nine and a grudge.
- **Hook:** the sealed Vey tin is a pre-Drowning pharmacy stock tin, from the Vey dispensary that stood on Mercer Lane. Inside,
  dry, are eighty founding-era antibiotic ampoules with no DoseLock, no expiry, no counter. Caddy doesn't know what they are.
  The player might.

### 8. Tallow, ten, vent kid, floors 15 to 19 (the Mills)

Tallow has no tally. He is ten and the Ledger does not count anyone under fourteen, which means that, as far as the Company is
concerned, he is not a tenant, not an occupant, not a sleeper and not anything. He finds this useful. He sleeps in a crawlspace
above the back of the **Glove Wall** on 17, where the ceiling void is warm from a duct and nobody leases the squares in front of
the wall because people don't like working with all those grey gloves at their backs.

He has five jobs, and he does all of them before noon.

At 05:45 he is on the Corner Stair landing on 17 with the other kids for the **count**. When the Morning Fall comes down at
06:00, every catch on every floor clanks open and shut, and the children count the bangs for the North Chute. The **Fall book**
on 16 (a man called **Dusty Kerrigan**, who works out of a crust hall) takes bets on the count, and pays the counter who calls it
closest 1 cr. Kerrigan says the count tells you how much the Crown threw out. The kids say it tells you who got married up top.

At 06:30 he walks the socket boards on 15 to 19, except 17, which is Wren's, and chalks the day's **tide table** beside each one, for 2 cr a week
a board. He gets the times from **Static**'s dawn broadcast, *The Tide*, which reads the bay tides at 06:00 after *The Rate*.
He chalks slack hour in a box. People plan their day by his chalk. He has four boards. Wren does the player's floor for 1 cr.

At 08:00 he sorts. A picker's job, someone else's catch bin, 3 cr a morning or a cup of paste, sitting on a crate turning over
the Fall: food here, cloth there, metal in the sack, VOID-stamped things in a pile for the owner to decide. He's quick. He knows
what things are by their weight.

At 09:30 he carries **chute mail**. People in the Middle write on the inside of cartons, fold them into cups, chalk a floor and a
trunk on the outside, and drop them. Most land in a stranger's catch. Tallow collects the ones addressed to Mills floors and
delivers them for a tip. Most are debts and love letters. He reads them all. He can read because the **reading room** on 14
teaches kids their letters from the only books it has, which are founding-era maintenance manuals, so Tallow can read "ISOLATE
SUPPLY BEFORE REMOVING COVER" and "CAUTION: CELL MAY RETAIN CHARGE AFTER DISCONNECTION" faster than most adults, and has never
read a story in his life.

At 11:00 there is usually a **jam**. A chute that jams in the Mills stops every catch below it, and the people below pay to get
it cleared fast. The Company's chute clearers come when they come (a Halden contract, paid in scrip, nobody hurries). Vent kids
come in ten minutes. Tallow goes into the access hatch above the jam with a hooked pole and a rope round his chest tied to a
pillar, and pulls, and kicks, and comes out filthy with a 5 cr chit and sometimes a thing from the jam he keeps. He's done it
forty times. Two kids from 19 went in last winter and only one came out, and that is why there is a rope.

In the afternoon he is ten. He plays **Floors** on the chalk tower drawn on the 17 landing, and **Chute or Keep**, and he goes to
the Canteen on 14 because the Old Hands give vent kids the bottom of the stew pot if they sit still and listen. He listens. He
has learned the clock numbers. He can tell a 2000s from a 4000s by the way they talk about heat.

At night, in the crawlspace, he lies on his back and listens to the building. Through the duct he can hear the Lung breathing,
the tide in the Race far below, the Whistle at 22:00, and, if he puts his ear to the concrete, the Hum from the floors above
the Mills where nobody goes, steady, like someone breathing in the next room.

In four years he will be tallied. He will get a band, an account, a Standing of 250 (children born in the tower "inherit a risk profile"), a registration
fee, and a bill. Until then he is what the Charter calls a non-party and the Mills call a **nobody**, which is not an insult
but a status: a nobody cannot be wronged, and a nobody's fine lands on whatever lease he was found on.
He will become a sleeper, and someone will charge him for the crawlspace.

**In play:**
- **Vent kids are the player's cheapest labour and best information**: a kid can be hired for the hour to sort the chute, clear
  a jam, carry a slip to a neighbour, chalk the tide table, or tell you which neighbour has been talking to the warden. They pay
  in chits, food and stew.
- **The tide table on the board** is literally Tallow's chalk: if the player stops paying him 2 cr a week, the table on their
  socket board is not updated, and slack hour becomes a surprise. A tiny, concrete service that teaches the slack-hour mechanic.
- **The Fall count** is a small daily bet the player can place (1 cr, see Entertainment) and a flavour line on the morning's
  chute: "Kids called forty-one. Kerrigan paid out to the Brodsky girl."
- **Chute Jam** event resolution: pay a vent kid 5 cr, wait for the Company (hours, scrip), or let your bin sit empty.
- **Hook:** the kids say there is a crawlspace above 21 that runs along the welded bulkhead of the Dark Floors and that if you
  lie in it at 03:00 you can hear someone whistling a shift song on the other side. The song is *Line Four*. Nobody under
  sixty knows the words.

### 9. Oona Fairweather, seventy-four, Clock 2051, Old Hand, floor 14 (the Mills)

Oona introduces herself the way they all do: "Fairweather, twenty fifty-one." Building services. Air plant. She was hired at
nineteen into the 30 Lung's fan rooms, worked them for ten years until the Shift, and has spent the forty-five years
since then listening to them fail.

She wakes at 05:30 on 14 in a two-square sleeping cube above the Canteen, in a room of Old Hands' cubes that the Co-op lets them
have for nothing, and she coughs for ten minutes. She has the **dust**, the dry Mills cough from the Works' old line dust, which
is the joke of her life: forty years keeping the air of the Plinth clean, and the air of the Plinth has filled her lungs with
the plant she worked for. She takes her Vey inhaler (refill 22 cr, every month, forever: the **Continuity** price) and goes down
to the Canteen for tea from the urn that hasn't been off in twenty years.

The Canteen at 06:00 is ten Old Hands and a dozen people who need them. Anyone can bring a founding-era thing and set it on
the long table. That's **the Bench**. Today: a meter with a cracked glass eye (Hollis Brandt's range, 3000s, and he takes it);
a lift door solenoid (a 2000s thing, Pavel Lisko's, but he isn't in yet); and a fan bearing from a Middle air handler, which
someone has carried down from 52 in a sock. Oona picks up the bearing, turns it, holds it to her ear and spins it with her thumb,
and listens. "Cage is gone. Race is fine. Repack it and it'll run five years. Don't let them sell you a new one." She doesn't
charge. The man from 52 owes her a turn now, and he knows it.

At 09:00 she goes up to 30, to the Lung, because Halden pays her. It is a consultancy contract: 50 sc a visit, scrip only, a
Company pass that gets her through the Turnstile and into the 30 Lung's plant floor, where four Company technicians who have
never seen a fan room running at full load ask her what a noise means. She tells them. Fan Room Two has a bearing going
(she can hear it from the door); Fan Room Four's belt is glazed. Fan Room Three has been dead since Y79, and she has told them
eleven times what is wrong with it (a founding-era motor controller, burnt; a spare exists in the drowned floors' spares
stores, in a sealed crate on the Polishing Hall's racks) and eleven times they have written it down and done nothing, because
nobody in the Company will sign for a dive.

She has decided she will not tell them a twelfth time. She will tell someone else.

She takes the scrip because the Hatch sells her inhaler refills, and she spends it the same day, because scrip expires and
she has seen too many Old Hands die with ninety days of pension in their tins.

In the afternoon she sleeps. In the evening she is back at the Canteen for the stew (protein block and Co-op greens, the same
every night since Y62) and the talk. Tonight it is about the Pension Book, and the Arbiter's yearly adjournment, and about whose
grandchild is going on the Bastion boat. Then **Dez Achterberg** starts telling the story about his boots, the ones still in his
locker on 23 behind the welds, and everyone groans, and everyone listens.

She keeps one thing from the Works on her: not her badge (that's on its cord at her throat, polished; everyone has that) but
her left glove, Line Four white gone grey, which she took off the Glove Wall in Y58 when the hall became leases and the new
tenant was about to scrape the wall clean, and which she wears every year on the 30th of the Ninth Month, on **Last Shift
Night**, and at no other time.

**In play:**
- **The Bench as a service:** take a founding-era item to the Canteen (a contract with the Old Hands as client, or a
  physical trip): an Old Hand **identifies** it (reveals hidden features over hours, the IDEAS analyser as people) for a turn.
- **Oona's Fan Room Three** is the late-game contract chain the 02 file names, told from the other side: she will give the
  player the location of the spare, and the clock-signed instructions, if the player can get it out of the Polishing Hall
  (through the Wet Widows' permit system) and up to 30. The reward is not money: a working Fan Room Three ends the Mills' **Air
  Days** for good, and the whole Plinth knows who did it.
- **Consultancy scrip** is how Old Hands touch the store; an Old Hand client who pays in scrip is common and honest.

### 10. Roz Achebe, forty-one, cook, the Pantry, floor 90 (the Crown's back door)

Roz has cooked for the Aubrey household for nine years and slept in the Pantry for all of them: a two-square bunk on a steel
mezzanine wedged between the roof cisterns and the Lung ducts, ten bunks to a bay, three hundred staff in all, paying rent to

She is up at 05:00 to bake. The Aubreys eat bread made that morning or not at all. She bakes four loaves, eats nothing from

Odran.

Roz grew up on 13. Her mother brewed crust. Her brother still sorts on the Ledge. She got out because she could cook and because
a Terraces restaurant on Lantern Row took her as a scullion at fifteen on a staff pass, and a Crown steward ate there one night.
She sends 60 cr a week down to 13 through the Ledger, and once a month a bag of real things through a channel the Pantry cooks
call **the dumbwaiter**: not the founding-era service lift, but a Mills shaft rat called **Wick** who will pick up a sealed parcel
at the Postern under "staff consignment" for 4 cr and drop it on 13.

The morning is breakfast, then lunch. Between them she takes the household's order book to Mr. Prest, who takes it to the

swore to Mr. Prest was "Greenhold sea salt, the old harvest". Mr. Prest believes her because the Crown Assay passed it, and the

At 14:00 Linnet comes down to the kitchen and asks Roz, sweetly, if she knows anyone in the Mills.

Roz does. Roz thinks for a long moment, wiping a bowl, about what the girl is asking for and what it will cost if Mr. Prest finds

**In play:**
- **Roz is the Pantry back door** from the 02 file made into a person: small Crown contracts that need no Crown clearance,

- **Linnet's order** is the intersection of two vignettes and a demonstration of the whole loop: the Crown's daughter wants the
  Mills' cheapest food and its ruined clothing, as fashion; a Mills cook in the Crown routes it; a Mills workshop fills it with a
  brick from the Hatch (3 cr) and a shirt from the North Chute (mended); the drone comes back with 120 cr and a pear.

## Part two: the shape of a day

Every band keeps time differently. The Crown keeps the clock the glass keeps. The Terraces keep office hours and clinic
shifts. The Middle keeps the Locals' chime and the school bell. The Mills keep the **Whistle**, the **Fall**, the **tap hours**
and the **tide**. The Sump keeps the tide alone. The Flats keep the moon.

A Mills day on floor 17, as the game's hour steps would see it:

| Hour | What happens on 17 | What it means for a workshop |
|---|---|---|
| 04:30 | Insect ranchers and bakers up. Hot-bunk Graveyard restless. | Night block ends; overnight machines finish. |
| 06:00 | Static's *The Tide*: tides, slack hour, news. Kids chalk the tide tables. | The day's slack hour is known. |
| 06:00 | **First whistle.** The **Morning Fall** roars down all four trunks. Catches bang. Tap hours open. Hot-bunk shift change. | Your catch bin is full. Tap is live. |
| 06:00–07:00 | Day labourers queue for Old Two to 30 (the **Pick** at the Turnstile; see Work). Sorting everywhere. | Sorting the chute is the first packing job. |
| 08:00 | Ledge market on 13 opens (to 10:00). Shops open. | Vendors open; counter customers start. |
| 09:00 | **Tap hours close.** Last fill. | Fill every vessel now or buy water later. |
| 10:00–13:00 | Working hours. Old Two every two hours. Hawkers. Chute trickles. | Machines run, drones launch. |
| 12:00 | **Noon tin**: lunch on the landings. | A lull at the counter. |
| 14:00 | **Second whistle.** Hot-bunk change. Second-shift workers out. | Afternoon customers; Second sleepers leave the corridor. |
| (shifting) | **Slack hour.** Socket boards dead for one or two hours; the time drifts ~50 minutes a day. | Machines on sockets stop; cells carry on. |
| 17:00–20:00 | Peak Window on power. | Power dear; run heavy machines earlier. |
| 18:00 | **Tap hours open again** (to 21:00). Cooking starts. **Tap supper.** | Second fill; evening counter rush. |
| 20:00 | Daily Service Statement on the pay posts. | The ledger slip prints. |
| 21:00 | Tap hours close. Crust halls fill. | Evening contracts land. |
| 22:00 | **Last whistle.** The Crown's quiet rule; North Chute drop doors locked above. Hot-bunk Graveyard in. | Night block begins. |

**In play:** the day already has a spine of public events that the player did not invent and cannot move: two whistles that
bracket the working day, two windows of water, one Fall, one drifting slack hour. These are the "something worth doing each
hour" that IDEAS worries about. A simple **day strip** at the top of the screen showing Whistle, Fall, tap hours and slack
would teach most of the world's rhythm with no text at all.

## Part three: food

### What each band eats

Food in the Stack is the divide on a plate. Every band eats down the same chain, from the opposite end.

**The Crown** eats real food, fresh, and wastes most of it, and the waste is part of the meal. A Crown dinner is nine small
courses, each eaten a third of; a "full plate" is a Mills idea. Produce comes from the Glasshouses on 84–87 (lettuce, tomatoes,
herbs, Greenhold-licensed), from Orchard House on 96–97 (lemons, figs, the quince), from Greenhold by courier (grain, fruit,
coffee, olive oil, farmed fish), and from the ten or so Mills and Flats suppliers on stewards' lists who send real goods through
the Postern with lead tags. Hens' eggs (about two hundred hens in the tower; Crown price 25 cr an egg). Real bread baked in the
Pantry every morning. Cheese, rarely, from Greenhold's dairy floors, the most expensive thing in any Crown kitchen. The Crown
does not eat insects in any form, and does not eat anything preserved: tins, jars and dried goods are "staff food". Water at
table is rain, from the roof, poured from glass. The Crown's food talk is about provenance: *which* Glasshouse tray, *which*
tree, which year's rain.

**The Terraces** eat certified. Lunch is bought (Lantern Row on 75 for those who can, clinic and office canteens for the rest,
where the dish of the day is a Real-Blend stew with a certified herb on top, the herb being the point). Dinner at home is
Orrin's premium range (**Orrin Table**: Real-Blend meals in black trays, 30% real, 14 cr a tray), a fresh vegetable from Halden
Select, Kof in the morning and real coffee on Sundays. The Terraces eat Vitabrick only in private and never call it that: they
call it **"a bar"**, buy the **Orrin Lean** variant in silver foil, and hide the foil. Terraces children are given a real egg on
their birthday. The Terraces' food talk is about certification: "is it plated?", "is it from the Glasshouses or from below?"

**The Middle** eats Orrin dressed up. The base is the noodle brick and the Vitabrick "Savoury" or "Sweet Ration"; the art is
what you do to them. Middle cooking is a cuisine of making the brick look like something else: grated brick fried into
"crumbs", brick soaked in broth and pressed into "cutlets", brick sliced thin and fried in insect-fat oil until it curls into
**shavings**. On the seventh day there is the **seventh onion**: a real onion bought from the Shelf Market, fried slowly so that
the whole corridor smells it and knows the household is still doing all right. A Middle hostess serves Kof in real-looking cups
and apologises for it. Middle people buy uncertified real tomatoes from Mills growers quietly and serve them as "from a friend".
Middle food talk is about cost and keeping up: "we had an onion", "they're on brick twice a day now, you know".

**The Mills** eat brick, slab, paste and whatever the Fall brings, with a great deal of chilli. A Mills meal is fuel first, and
the Mills are proud of it the way a hard trade is proud of its hands. The staples:
- **Slab and brick.** The Mills eat more unbranded **slab** than Vitabrick, because it is cheaper (2 cr against 3) and every
  press house makes its own shape (Kasprzak eggs, the Halloway diamond, the Co-op's plain square stamped with a leaf).
- **Fall food.** Bluewashed bread (Crown bread scrubbed of Orrin Blue, 2 cr a loaf, mildly toxic, very popular), peel, ends,
  broken cakes, Lantern Row's leftover noodles. Scrubbing blue is a household skill: vinegar, a brush, salt water, then a rinse
  in grey. A good scrubber gets nine-tenths of it off. "A bit of blue never hurt" is a lie the Mills tell daily.
- **Chillies.** They grow in anything, in a cup on a window ledge, under any lamp, and they make slab edible. The Mills eat more
  chilli per head than any people in Calder Bay ever did. Chillies are small change (0.15 cr each).
- **Mushrooms**, grown in the dark on spent insect bedding (oyster mushrooms on egg-flats), the Mills' one cheap real food.
- **Co-op greens.** Members get a share of the Co-op beds; it is paid partly in produce. A Co-op family eats something green
  most days. A non-Co-op family eats something green on holidays.
- **Crust** for breakfast (the bread kvass, 2.1%, in 03).
- **Roast crickets**, salted, in a paper twist: the Mills' snack, sold hot on every landing.

**The Sump** eats slab, crickets and gin, and fries everything in insect fat because frying kills what is on the food and fat is
cheap. Sump food is salt-heavy, because Flats salt is cheap (1 cr a kilo, tastes of metal). Hot food is a luxury because the
Sump's power is stolen and shed first; much Sump cooking happens on **tallow stoves** (a tin of insect fat with a wick under a
grill). The Old Post's fried slab is the Sump's restaurant food.

**The Flats** eat what they buy with salvage: slab from Sixes, barrel water, crust from the Sump, and greens from berth grow
tarps. Never anything from the bay (see Caddy, above). The Flats are the only people in Calder who see the open sky every day and
the only ones who never eat anything fresh that they did not grow themselves on a tarp.

### Meals

Mills meals are named for the clock that governs them:

- **First.** At first whistle, 06:00, before the Fall is sorted: a cup of crust and a **finger** (an eighth of a brick) or a
  heel of bluewashed bread. Eaten standing.
- **Noon tin.** At 12:00, on the landings and in the aisles: whatever is in your tin. From the Works days, when every worker
  carried a lunch tin; the **Lunch Tin Months** of the Archivists' origin (06a) are named for the same tins. A Mills child's
  first tin is a gift.
- **Tap supper.** The main meal of a Mills day, cooked during the evening tap hours (18:00 to 21:00), because cooking takes
  water and that is when the water is on. Families eat between 19:00 and 20:00. A household that eats outside tap hours is
  either rich in stored water or eating cold.
- **Late.** A fried thing after the last whistle, at a crust hall, for those who have the money.

Above 30, people say breakfast, lunch and dinner. A Mills person who has gone up in the world gives it away by saying "tap
supper" without thinking.

### The protein block in culture

The **Vitabrick** is the tower's bread. It is 250 g of pressed insect paste and food waste under an Orrin wheat-sheaf deboss
(a wheat sheaf, on a product made of crickets, is a joke nobody at Orrin has ever acknowledged), wrapped in green foil, and it is
a day's protein. It is the yardstick of the Mills economy after the cell: a cell is a brick, a hot-bunk shift is a brick, an hour
of a picker's work is half a brick.

What people do with it, say about it and build around it:

- **Fingers.** A brick is scored into eight. Children are fed in fingers; workers carry two fingers in the tin; a beggar asks
  for "a finger". "Not a finger" means nothing at all.
- **A two-brick day** is a good day (a working adult eating properly). A **one-brick day** is poor. A **finger day** is
  hunger. Mills people describe hard years by brick count: "Y79 was a finger winter."
- **The wedding brick.** At a Mills wedding the couple break one brick between them and feed each other a finger in front of the
  guests: "May you never be short." The rest is shared out among the guests a crumb each. The wedding brick is always a real
  Vitabrick, not slab, bought new from the Hatch, wrapper intact. It is the one time a Mills family insists on the brand.
- **Brick carving.** Dried bricks go hard as wood. Children carve them with nails into animals they have never seen (a horse, a
  dog, a cow from a founding-era Orrin poster), and there is a Mills craft in carved brick figures for the Middle's market
  stalls, sold as "folk art". The best carver on 16, **Absalom Toye**, sells to a Terraces gallery that varnishes them.
- **Sachets.** The flavour sachets of the "Savoury" and "Sweet Ration" bricks are sold loose by children for 0.10 cr, and
  traded among them like marbles. A full set of the eight Orrin flavours (Savoury, Sweet Ration, Smoky, Herb Garden, Tomato
  Sun, Curry Gold, Honey Morning, and the discontinued **Orchard** that tasted of pears) is a playground fortune.
- **Slab vs brick.** Slab is cheaper and often better (Dee Kasprzak's wild-line slab has more protein than a Vitabrick and no
  filler), but brick is *trusted*. Mills mothers buy brick for babies, slab for themselves. Slab has a reputation, unfair and
  durable, for being made from "Sump crickets" or "the Jaws". Press houses that want to sell to the Middle stamp their slab
  with founding-era-looking numbers to look official.
- **"We eat at the Crown's table, just later."** The East Chute's food waste is a large share of what goes into the insect bins
  that make the paste that makes the bricks. Everyone knows it. It is said at tap supper, sometimes as grace.
- **The Real-Blend joke.** Orrin's Real-Blend tin is 8% real. The Mills call it "the tin with a pea in it", and children fish for
  the pea.

**In play:**
- **Brick as a unit of value** on Mills and Sump slips: "pays 4 bricks" means 12 cr in credits, or four actual Vitabricks in the
  reward bay, depending on the client. A brick is a 1x1 item.
- **Slab shapes** as a maker's mark: the player's press can have a **die** (a machine setting): the licensed Vitabrick die
  (only if registered with Orrin; Orrin buys at a fixed price) or an unbranded die in a shape of the player's choosing, which
  marks the player's slab on every shelf. Clients learn to ask for it by shape.
- **The wedding brick** is a cheap, high-feeling contract: *one Vitabrick, plain, new, wrapper intact, for a wedding on 15
  tonight*. Easy to fill. Failing it is remembered.
- **Sachet sets** are a vent-kid trade good and a small collectible (the Orchard sachet is the rare one, worth 5 cr to a child
  and 40 to a Middle collector).

### Food etiquette

- **Never blue a gift.** Food given is never blued, even by the Crown's own staff to their own families. A gift with a trace
  of blue on it is an insult and a threat.
- **Scrub before you share.** Bluewashed food is scrubbed by the household that eats it, not by the one that gives it. To hand
  over unscrubbed Fall food is to say "you're a picker".
- **The heel goes to the eldest.** The first slice of a real loaf is for the oldest at the table. Old Hands are given the heel
  in the Canteen even when the loaf is bluewashed.
- **Chilli in the cup.** A Mills host who is short of water offers a chilli instead of a wet: "I'm dry, have a chilli." It
  means: I can't give you a drink, but you're welcome.
- **Smell is announcement.** Frying something real in a corridor is showing off; Mills and Middle families fry real food with
  the door shut and the vent open, so that the smell goes up the duct to the floor above, which is considered a fair revenge.

**In play:** the **demand for food by band** is the core of the "same order, two worlds" idea: upward slips name certified
real produce in small, urgent, high-paying lots; Middle slips want dressed-up brick ingredients (insect-fat oil, chillies, a
seventh-day onion, uncertified real tomatoes "from a friend"); Mills and Sump slips want slab, paste, crickets and chillies in
bulk. A **Seventh Onion** micro-contract on the Middle's rest day is a reliable small earner for a grow bed.

## Part four: sleep and shifts

### The three shifts that outlived their factory

Halden Works ran three shifts round the clock, and the Works Whistle still blows them: **06:00, 14:00, 22:00**. Nobody has
found its controller; the Crown had it muffled above 60 in Y58; below 60, eighty-six years after it first blew, it still divides
the day. The Mills use the Works' names: **First** (06–14), **Second** (14–22), **Graveyard** (22–06). Hot-bunk houses rent beds
by them. Day labour is hired by them. Old Hands still wake at whichever whistle was theirs.

### Where people sleep, by band

- **Crown:** bedrooms with windows, dark-glass at night, a full-spectrum lamp to wake to. Silence. Nobody shares a bed they did
  not choose.
- **Terraces:** private bedrooms, sealed windows, vent registers on **Fresh Plus**; white-noise machines sold by Vey to cover the
  Lung's hum ("Vey Lull Tone", 90 cr).
- **Middle:** family flats, often with the children in one room and the parents on a fold-down in the living room. Inner flats
  are dark all day. The Middle sleeps 23:00 to 06:00, in step with the Locals' first car.
- **Mills:** mezzanines above workshops, reached by ladders; **sleeping cubes** (two squares, curtained) stacked three high in
  dormitory halls; hot-bunks; the back of a lease behind a curtain (illegal on a Commercial lease, which most workshops are, and
  done by almost everyone). Mills families commonly **shift-share** a bed: a parent who works nights sleeps in the day in the
  bed the children sleep in at night. "We share a bed but we've never been in it at the same time" is a Mills joke about
  marriage.
- **Sump:** bunks on pallets above the tidemark, in rows, in the old chemical stores; hammocks slung between pillars, because
  water does not reach a hammock.
- **Flats:** roofs, stilt-houses, rafts. The Flats sleep by the tide: a berth is awake at every low water, whenever it falls.

### Sleep as money

Every square a bed takes is a square paying rent and not producing. That one fact explains most Mills sleeping arrangements:
the hot-bunk (a bed that earns three times a day), the mezzanine (a bed that takes no floor squares, because the Lease Office
measures squares on the floor), the shift-shared family bed, and the cube stacks (six beds on two squares). A Mills person asked
where they live will often answer with the number of squares, not the floor: "I'm on two and a half."

**Mezzanines and the rule of the floor.** The Lease Office leases floor squares. Anything built above them (a mezzanine, a
shelf bunk, a hammock) is, legally, an **alteration** (a reportable irregularity), but it does not add to the rent. Every Mills
hall is therefore two storeys inside one, and a warden who wanted to could report every one of them. None do, because they
sleep on one too.

### Sleep trouble

- **Air Days**, when a 30 Lung fan room trips: hot, stale, nobody sleeps; the next day is slow and quarrelsome. Counter
  customers are fewer and touchier.
- **The Fall** at 06:00 wakes everyone below 59 who isn't up.
- **Slack at night**: when slack hour falls in the night, anyone with live stock or a grow bed on the socket is up swapping cells.
- **Vey Lull**: Vey's sleep tablets, 8 cr for ten. Hot-bunk sleepers on Second, who have to sleep through the afternoon noise,
  take them every day. A **cracked Lull** (pills chiselled out of chute dispensers) is a steady grey good.
- **The Hum**: on floors 20, 21, 27 and 28, next to the Dark Floors, people say they sleep badly on nights when the Hum is
  loudest. The Co-op delegate for 21 logs them (06a).

**In play:**
- **Sleeping on the lease** is the player's own question. A workshop lease is Commercial; sleeping on it is an occupancy
  irregularity. The player can rent a cube elsewhere (a weekly cost, and travel hours), sleep on the lease and accept the
  warden's attention, or buy a Domestic add-on (more rent). This can be invisible flavour or a real choice.
- **Bed contracts:** sheets (from the South Chute, washed), cube curtains, hammocks, earplugs (wax from the honey woman on 28),
  Lull, crust by the cask. Hot-bunk houses are bulk clients with weekly orders.
- **Air Day** affects customers and machines (02) and, here, mood: an Air Day doubles the chance of a quarrel event on the
  floor (a neighbour complains to the warden; a client cancels).

## Part five: work

### How people get work

**The Pick.** At 05:30 on the Turnstile landing on 30, day labourers from the Mills queue for the **Pick**: Middle employers'
foremen and Company contractors walk the line and choose who they want for the day. Those chosen get a day ticket through the
Turnstile and a chit at the end of the shift (30 cr, the yardstick). Those not chosen go back down by 07:00. Picks are made by
looking: hands (no shakes), teeth (etch-teeth suggest bad water), cough (none), clothes (clean, mended neatly), shoes (dry).
Everyone in the Mills dresses for the Pick even if they don't intend to go: it is how you show you could.

**The board.** Contract boards on every lift lobby: Company jobs (chute clearing, lift greasing, meter rounds), mostly in scrip.

**Through someone.** Most real work above 30 comes through a person: an aunt in the Glasshouses' night crew, a cousin in the
Lift Guild (Guild seats are inherited), a Pantry cook who puts you on a steward's list. "Who's your stair?" means who do you know
above you.

**Your own pen.** The Mills dream: a workshop of your own. Most Mills workshops are one person and a few squares, with family
working for food and a kid on the chute.

### Kinds of work in the Mills

Insect ranching and the trades that hang off it (bin hands, press operators, frass carriers, mushroom growers); Co-op growing;
chute sorting and picking; refurbishing and restoring (menders, resealers, re-terminators: the work of undoing the VOID, 06b);
day labour above 30; hot-bunk keeping; stinking (water, 03); shaft-ratting (drones, 06a); hawking; cooking for crust halls;
children's work (counting, chalking, sorting, jam-clearing, bed-watching).

### Work customs

- **Clocking.** Mills workshops of more than three people keep a **clock board**: a board of nails, one per worker, and a brass
  tag that moves from "out" to "in". It means nothing legally. It is how the Works did it.
- **The Whistle break.** At second whistle, 14:00, everyone in the Mills stops for a minute and drinks something. It used to be
  the shift change. It is now just a minute, and taking it is a matter of pride.
- **Hands are the CV.** Calluses where a tool goes, burns where a soldering iron slipped, the green stain of frass under the
  nails, the clean hands of a resealer who wears gloves: Mills people read each other's trades from their hands.
- **Wet sweeping.** Mills halls are swept wet (a bucket of grey, a stiff broom) because dry sweeping lifts the old line dust
  into the air. Sweeping dry near someone's pen is rude; sweeping dry near a child is a fight.
- **Injury.** No Mills workshop has insurance (the Mutual excludes "occupational events below floor 30"). An injured worker is
  carried to a **mender** (see Health) and the workshop pays, or does not. A workshop that does not is known.

**In play:**
- **The Pick** explains why many Mills clients are gone during the day (fewer counter customers 06:00–19:00 on weekdays) and why
  the counter is busiest at tap supper.
- **Labour as payment:** a client who cannot pay can offer a day of a family member's work (a sorter for a morning, a
  bin-watcher overnight). That is the hireling idea without new systems: one day of a helper who sorts your catch or watches a
  machine's slot.
- **"Who's your stair?"** maps onto contract access: knowing someone (a Pantry cook, a Glasshouses cleaner, a Lift Guild cousin)
  unlocks a contract source above your Standing. Relationships are keys.

## Part six: health, the cough and medicine

### The cough

Everyone in the Stack below the Terraces coughs, and the cough tells you where they live.

- **The dust** (Mills): a dry, tight, hacking cough from the Works' **line dust**: fine grit from the chip and cell lines that
  settled into every seam of the Plinth's halls and lifts every time a hall is swept dry, a pallet dragged, a partition torn out.
  It builds over years. Old Hands have it worst. It is why Mills halls are swept wet.
- **The wet chest** (Sump, lower Mills): a loose, rattling cough from mould, damp and the sweet chemical breath that comes up
  from the drowned floors at low tide. Worse in winter. It kills: Marek Kasprzak (above) died of it at forty-nine.
- **Glass cough** (Terraces): a dry little throat-clear, from the Gills' air and the Fresh Plus scent cartridges. Terraces people
  call it an allergy. Mills people call it "coughing for show".
- **The Crown** does not cough. A Crown resident who coughs at dinner is asked if they have been "downstairs".

The cough has a whole etiquette. **Coughing into your sleeve** is polite. Coughing into the room is "giving it to the floor". A
**cough-cloth** (a square of cloth tied over nose and mouth, often from a Crown dress lining, fine weave) is worn when sweeping,
sorting the chute, or visiting someone who is sick. In the Middle, coughing on the Locals is a social crime; Middle people with
the dust hold it in until their eyes water, and are known by that.

### Medicine, by rung

Health care is a ladder, and every rung up costs ten times the one below.

1. **Home and the Fall.** Gut tabs (Vey, 6 cr for ten, the most-bought medicine in the Mills, because of the water), cracked
   doses from the chute, short-drawer medicine from nurses like Yetunde, chilli-and-insect-sugar **cough candy**, steam tents
   (a towel over a pot of boiling water with a crushed herb in it; a Co-op mint plant is a household treasure), **frass
   poultice** (Tide Folk; 1 cr; does something), mushroom broth.
2. **Menders.** Unlicensed healers in the Mills and the Sump: former nurses who fell from the Middle, Tide Folk midwives like
   Ma Senna Duku, a bone-setter on 15 called **Haskell** who learned on Works injuries, a **tooth-puller** on 7 (10 cr a tooth,
   and he buys old teeth with founding-era fillings). They are paid in credits, chits, cells, food and turns. Menders are
   illegal under the Vey licence ("unlicensed provision of care"); HPS never enforces it below 30, because nobody would replace
   them. The best Mills mender is **Sister Odette Abara** (no religious order; the title came from her Works first-aid badge, which
   said SISTER on it), who runs a curtained clinic of four cots on 19 and charges by what you have.
3. **The Vey pharmacy counter** in the Middle (fever tabs, inhalers, DoseLock courses at 45 cr). The pharmacist can sell; she
   cannot diagnose. Diagnosis is a clinic visit.
4. **Vey Sixty-Four** (Terraces): 180 cr before treatment, which is six days of a Mills labourer's wage to be looked at.
5. **St. Ober's Spire** (the hospital tower, 07). A transfer to St. Ober's costs the boat, the clinic's referral fee, and a
   **Care Agreement**: a debt to the charity that runs it, at a rate the Mills call "the Saint's interest". People come back
   cured and owing for a decade. "Gone to the Saint" means gone for treatment, and also means gone into debt for good.

### What people get

The water diseases (the shakes, blue gums, stone-belly, etch-teeth, the slows, sweet-sick) are in 03. On top of those:
- **Fall fever**: a gut infection from badly scrubbed Fall food. Common in summer. Children get it first.
- **Bin hands**: a rash on the hands and forearms of insect ranchers and bin hands, from ammonia and frass. Treated with grease.
- **Lamp eyes**: Mills workers who spend years under purple grow lights or amber work lamps lose colour vision first, then
  night sight. Growers are known by a squint.
- **Hot-bunk itch**: lice and mites passed bed to bed. Hot-bunk houses that boil their sheets advertise it.
- **Stair knees**: Mills people walk everywhere, up and down, because the lifts cost; knees go by forty.
- **Pale**: not a disease, a look. People who never see daylight have a grey-white skin with dark under-eyes. The Mills call
  Terraces people **sunned**, and Crown people **gold**.

### Medicine as a product

Vey's pricing rule, **Continuity**, means the first inhaler is cheap and every refill is dear (8 cr, then 22 every month, in the
Mills). Every Vey product with a dispenser (DoseLock antibiotics, inhalers, Lull) has a counter or a lock, and dispensers go down
the chute when they lock, and the Mills chisel them open. Cracked medicine is the largest single grey good in the Mills after
water.

**In play:**
- **Medicine demand is permanent and seasonal.** Gut tabs and cough candy all year; inhaler refills monthly; Fall fever in summer
  (gut tabs double in price for a week, and **clean water** contracts rise); the wet chest in winter (steam herbs, cough-cloths,
  mould-lung inhalers).
- **Menders are clients**: Sister Odette wants clean cloth (boiled), real herbs (mint, thyme), gut tabs in bulk, cracked DoseLock
  (with the risk of crushed pills), cell power for a sterilising lamp, and clean water by the cask. She pays partly in turns: a
  player who supplies her gets treated free if a **worker injury** event ever hits their lease.
- **Cough candy** is a small chain from things the player already has: insect sugar (from the bins), chillies, a pot on a heat
  source. A cheap good that sells to every band below 30.
- **Lamp eyes** gives a reason for a **tinted visor** item (a Crown sunglass lens, from the North Chute, re-framed): sells to
  growers.

## Part seven: children and schooling

### Being born

Babies in the Mills are born at home, on the lease, with a mender or a midwife (Ma Senna Duku comes up from the Flats for
families who can pay her in salt money; Sister Odette on 19 delivers most of Hall Nineteen). Terraces babies are born at Vey
Sixty-Four; Crown babies at the Crown physicians' rooms on 91, or abroad.

Customs at a Mills birth:
- **First water** (03): a baby's first drink of plain water is the best the family can find, saved for months.
- **The floor number.** A newborn is told its floor, out loud, by the oldest person present: "You're seventeen." Mills people
  answer the question "where are you from?" with a number all their lives. Sump people tattoo theirs (a small numeral on the
  inside of the wrist, where a tally will go).
- **The registration question.** A child can be put on the Charter as a Registered Occupant (a registration fee, a higher water
  baseline, and a line in the Ledger: Standing will start at 250 at fourteen because tenants' children "inherit a risk profile")
  or left off it, a **nobody**, free, invisible and unprotected. Most Mills families register the first child and not the rest.
  An unregistered child has no right to a Co-op water cup under Company rules, and gets one anyway under the Co-op's.
- **Gallery children.** Babies left on the pickers' gallery of the Bale Hall on 8, where the bale pickers will see them before
  the Jaws close, are called gallery children. The Ninefold write them in the Sump register with the floor "8" and the surname
  **Gallery**. There are a few hundred Gallerys in the tower. Some of them did well.

### Schools

- **The Halden School** (floors 50–52, with the Protectives' quarters above it). Middle children, trained for Company posts:
  bylaws, bookkeeping, Reconditioning Practice (spotting resealed goods), Standing management, Company history (*A City Stood On
  End*), deportment. Mills children may sit the **Sixty exam** at eleven; the fee for a place is the size of a year's rent. One
  Mills child a year gets the **Ascension Bursary** (Foundation-funded; the family's account is flagged Assisted for the
  duration, which blocks its Proprietor Pathway). The exam questions are published every year after the fact and sold in the
  Mills as practice sheets.
- **Terraces tutors.** Terraces children are tutored at home and sent to schools in other towers (Vantage has the best) at
  twelve.
- **Reading rooms** (Mills). Run by the Archivists (06a) on 14 and 21, in exchange for nothing but attention. They teach from
  what they have, which is founding-era documents: maintenance manuals, safety notices, parts catalogues, shift rotas. **Mills
  literacy is technical.** A Mills child can read a wiring diagram before a story, knows "CAUTION" and "ISOLATE" and "RATED
  LOAD" by six, and learns arithmetic from torque tables. The first sentence many Mills children ever read is the one stencilled
  on every Works door: **THIS DOOR MUST REMAIN CLOSED.**
- **Hall schools** (Mills Co-op). A rota of Co-op members teaches children in the Co-op hall in the hour after noon tin:
  growing, insect keeping, water testing, sums, the Seven Rules of the Round. Taught as a job, not a lesson. Children earn share
  hours.
- **The Canteen.** Old Hands teach vent kids for the bottom of the stew pot (Tallow, above). No writing, ever; they teach by
  hand and ear. A kid who has sat at the Canteen for a year can listen to a bearing.
- **Sump and Flats.** Reckoners teach the tide tables; Pumpmen teach the pumps; the Widows teach the line. Nobody teaches reading.

**Manual-speak.** Because Mills children learn to read from manuals, Mills speech is full of founding-era safety phrases used as
everyday idiom. "Isolate before servicing" (think before you speak; calm down first). "Rated load" (someone's limit: "she's at
rated load this week"). "May retain charge" (still angry; still dangerous). "Do not operate without guard" (don't go there
alone). "Return to stores" (go home). "Out of tolerance" (wrong, crooked, a lie). A Mills grandmother saying "that boy's out of
tolerance" is quoting a calliper manual from Y12.

### Children's work

Mills children work from about seven: counting crickets, sorting the chute, chalking tide tables, carrying chute mail,
watching a neighbour's grow bed overnight ("bed-watching", 1 cr a night), selling roast crickets on the landings, clearing jams
(vent kids, older). It is not called work. It is called "helping", and a child who helps is paid in chits, food and pride.

### Games and rhymes

- **Floors.** Hopscotch on a chalk tower drawn up a landing wall and onto the floor: squares numbered 1 to 104, banded
  (the Sump squares chalked blue, the Crown in white). You throw a bottle cap up the tower and hop to it, and if it lands in the
  Crown, it is gone: by the rules, nobody may step in the Crown squares, so the cap stays there. Every landing's chalk Crown has a
  little drift of lost bottle caps in it. Children who lose their best cap to the Crown learn something early.
- **Chute or Keep.** One child names a thing; the others shout "chute!" or "keep!" and whoever is wrong (by the namer's ruling)
  is out. Good players know that in the Crown everything is chute and in the Mills everything is keep, and they name Crown
  things to trap the little ones.
- **Inspector.** Tag: one child is the inspector and must "seal" others by touching them and saying "VOID". Sealed children
  freeze until another child "mends" them by drawing an X on their back.
- **Old Two.** A chain of children hold hands and walk the aisle; at each "floor" (a pillar) one child may get on or off, and the
  chain stops for exactly eight seconds at a pillar nobody called. Whoever is caught moving in those eight seconds is out.
- **The count**: counting the bangs of the Fall, for Kerrigan's book.

A counting rhyme for choosing who is "it", chanted floor by floor:

> *One for the Lantern, two for the Glass,*
> *three for the Middle who won't let you pass,*
> *four for the Mills where the crickets sing,*
> *five for the Sump and the pumps and the ring,*
> *six for the Flats where the mud won't hold,*
> *seven: they knew, and they never told.*
> *Out goes you.*

("Seven: they knew" is the list in 01. Children chant it without knowing it is history.)

### Tally Day

At fourteen, every child the Ledger knows about (and every nobody who wants to be somebody) goes to a pay post with a parent and
is **tallied**: given a band, an account, a Standing, and a first bill. It is the Mills' coming of age. Customs:
- **The first rent.** The family pays the child's first registration from savings, as a gift, so that the child starts with no
  debt. "Happy Tally, you owe" is the greeting, and the family says "not yet".
- **The sleeve.** Friends give a **tally sleeve** (a cloth or leather cover for the band; Mills sleeves are embroidered with the
  floor number). Middle children get silver-coloured sleeves to suggest a higher Standing colour.
- **Tally supper** is the one meal of a Mills childhood with real food in it on purpose: a tomato, a real egg if the family can
  get one.
- Some Mills families **delay** a child's tally as long as they can, because a tallied child is a sleeper, a Registered
  Occupant or a leaseholder, and costs. Some vent kids never get tallied at all.

**In play:**
- **Tally Day contracts**: *one real egg, for a Tally supper on 16, tonight*; *a tally sleeve, embroidered "17"*. Small, frequent,
  well paid for the Mills, and they make the board feel lived in.
- **Sixty exam season**: Middle and Mills families buy practice sheets, lamps (study light), Kof, Lull (nerves). A **Placement
  Day** event in the Middle changes demand for a week.
- **Children as nobodies** gives vent kids their status in the rules: they can be hired with no registration fee and no water
  baseline, and a warden cannot report them, only the lease they are found on.

## Part eight: death and what happens to bodies

### The law of the dead

Under **Bylaw 88**, human remains on a lease are **waste** and must be discharged by the Company's sanitary service at the
scheduled rate (45 cr in the Mills). Removing them any other way is "improper discharge of waste outside the Structure" (a 30 cr
standards charge), and the Arbiter has upheld it (determination A/83/0350, in 05). Under **Clause 77**, the dead leaseholder's
debts pass to the Registered Occupants. The tally is not cancelled automatically: someone has to report the death at a pay post,
which costs a **closure fee** of 12 cr, and, if nobody does, the account goes on billing.

The Company offers three things, at three prices:
1. **Remains Recovery**: free. The sanitary service collects the body in a sealed bag on Old Two's **three o'clock** (a
   sanitary run the Lift Guild makes at 03:00, the one car nobody rides) and takes it into the Waterhouse perimeter. Nothing
   comes back. The service's contract lists among its outputs "recovered process water, returned to the tower system" (the hook
   in 03). Nobody says this at a funeral. Everybody knows it.
2. **Halden Repose**: a funeral plan, 4 cr a week for life, sold door to door in the Middle by agents in grey suits. Cremation in
   the Repose rooms on 8 (an old Works kiln, inside the Waterhouse perimeter), and the ashes returned in a **Repose box**: a
   sealed steel tin exactly the size of a Vitabrick. Nobody at Halden seems to have noticed. Everybody else has.
3. **Niches.** The Middle keeps its Repose boxes in **the Niche Wall** on 30, a corridor of small steel doors near the Turnstile,
   rented by the year (20 cr). A lapsed niche is cleared after ninety days' notice. Cleared boxes go to the Reclaim on 59 with
   the metal.

### The Carry

The Mills do not, if they can help it, let the Company take their dead. The Mills way is **the Carry**.

The body is washed (in rain if the Rain Church can bring any, in the best water the family has otherwise) and wrapped in a
sheet. Friends and neighbours, six or eight, carry it down the Corner Stairs from the floor of its life to the Sump, by hand, on a
door taken off its hinges for the purpose (every Mills hall has a **carrying door**, kept by someone, lent for funerals). On every
landing on the way down, the people of that floor come out and each lays a hand on the sheet for a moment, which is called
**the touch**, and someone on each landing lights a candle on the stair. A Carry from 17 to 9 passes eight landings and is
touched by a hundred people. You can tell how loved someone was by how long their Carry took.

At the bottom, the body is handed to the **Tide Folk**, who take it out on the lowest ebb of the month for **ebb burial** (06a):
wrapped, weighted, walked out to a place only the reckoners know. The Tide Folk's fee is whatever is in the pockets and the sheet
itself. The **Ninefold** write the death in the Sump register, which is the only record of a Carry that exists. The Company,
some months later, bills the family 75 cr (sanitary charge and standards charge) for improper discharge, and the family pays it,
and considers it the price of a funeral.

The carriers are given the dead person's stored water (03), which is why a household that loved someone keeps their cask
full to the end.

### By band

- **Crown:** the dead are taken by the Crown Pad to **Greenhold**, where the agricultural tower keeps **Memorial Terraces**:
  burial in real soil, under a tree. The tree is patented. The family pays an annual **ground fee**; when the fee stops, the
  tree is "retired" and the plot "renewed". The ultimate Crown luxury is to take up soil after death.
- **Terraces:** Halden Repose, premium tier: the ashes are pressed into a small glass disc and set in a window. Terraces windows
  have a little row of them along the sill.
- **Middle:** Repose and the Niche Wall. A Middle funeral is a gathering in the flat with Kof and a plate of brick shavings, and a
  trip to 30 to put the box in its niche. The dead are spoken of by their niche number.
- **Mills:** the Carry and ebb burial, or Remains Recovery for those with nobody to carry them. "He got recovered" means he died
  alone.
- **Sump:** the Hooks stop work for a body found in the drops and send for the Tide Folk; Sump families do their own Carry, a
  short one, down to 4 and out of the Spill Door at low tide.
- **Flats:** ebb burial.
- **Old Hands** are carried with their clock badge on its cord around their neck. It goes into the bay with them. Old Hands do
  not let the Company have their bodies or their badges.

### After

- **Keeping the band.** Many families do not report a death, so as not to pay the closure fee and so as to keep the account (a
  good Standing is worth more than a life insurance the Mills cannot buy). "We're keeping Gran on the Ledger" is a common
  arrangement. Rented tallies of the dead are a known grey trade (04).
- **The grey thread.** Mourners wear a grey thread tied round the wrist above the tally until it rots off. People below 30 wear
  them for months; nobody above 60 does.
- **The name on the stair.** On Wall Night (see Holidays) the names of the year's dead are said at the South Door with the
  Drowned.
- **"Going down the stairs"** is to die, in the Mills. "He's gone down" means dead, not demoted.
- **"They'll drop you"** (be disposed of down the chute) is the worst threat in the tower. There are bodies in the drops,
  despite Bylaw 88; the Hooks find a few a year.

**In play:**
- **Funeral contracts:** candles for every landing of a Carry (*eight candles, landings 9 to 16, by 20:00*); a clean sheet; a
  **carrying door** (a big flat item; the player's shop may be asked to lend theirs, which takes it off the field for half a day);
  rain or clean water to wash the body; real flowers (the rarest).
- **Death in the neighbourhood** changes the floor: a neighbour's lease is cleared (Clear-out on 17: their goods go to the Lease
  Office's **broom-clean** sale, or out with the Carry's water to the carriers), a client disappears from the board, a new
  tenant arrives.
- **Repose agents** are a recurring visitor in the Middle; in the Mills, a Repose agent at the player's counter is a small comic
  event (buy the plan: 4 cr a week forever; refuse: nothing happens, yet).
- **Hook:** the Repose box weighs about 250 g. An Archivist weighed a hundred of them from lapsed niches before the Reclaim
  took them. The weight varies by less than two grams. A human body's ashes vary by far more than that.

## Part nine: faith

The big faiths of the Stack have their own entries: the **Rain Church** (06a, and its water in 03) and the **Tide Folk** (06a).
This section is about the small faiths everyone keeps, whatever else they believe.

### Everyday observance below 30

- **Touching grey.** The founding concrete (the "grey") is the one thing in the tower everyone trusts to last. People touch a
  bare patch of grey for luck before a hard thing: an inspection, a Pick, a Carry, a first launch. Every Mills hall has a
  pillar worn smooth at hand height by eighty years of touching.
- **Saint Pump.** The dead founding-era pump on 5 with a drip-candle on it, where divers leave a bolt before going down (02).
  Mills people who have never dived leave bolts there too, for a sick child, a drone that hasn't come back.
- **The Whistle.** The Works Whistle is half a clock and half an oracle. If it is late (it is sometimes, by seconds), someone on
  the floor will die before the next. If it blows when it should not (rare: once in Y71, once in Y79), the floor holds its
  breath for a week. Mills people stop for a breath at each whistle as a habit, the way others cross themselves.
- **The Night Shift.** Some Mills people, mostly the old, believe the lights that came on in the Clean Core the night after the
  Last Shift (01) were turned on by the dead of the Works: that the Dark Floors are staffed by everyone who ever clocked in, and
  that the Hum is them working. They leave **tin offerings** at the welded doors on 22 and 26: a lunch tin with a finger of brick
  and a cup of tea in it, left at noon. The tins are gone by morning. (Vent kids. Probably vent kids.)
- **First cup for the bay** (03): the first of anything goes back.
- **Chalked numerals.** "5" on a stair door, "7" on a tap (01): half curse, half prayer.

### The Middle's faith: Standing

The Middle's religion is self-improvement, and its scripture is the Company's. **Rise meetings** are Company-sponsored evening
circles in the Middle's community rooms, where neighbours read aloud from Ansel Halden's Founding Day speeches and from a little
blue booklet called ***Halden Hours: Thoughts for the Upward Tenant*** ("A square kept tidy is a square kept." "The Ledger
remembers kindness." "Every floor was once a ceiling.") and share their Standing gains. Standing is checked at the start and end
like a pulse. People pray, privately, in the form "please let the Rate Review be flat this month."

### The Crown's faith: stillness

The Crown does wellness. **Stillness** retreats at the Long Bath on 99; a Pleasure Pier spiritual teacher who charges by the
breath; fasting (the Crown fasts for health; the Mills fast for no reason they would call religious). Crown households keep a
**rain bowl** on a table by the door, filled from the roof on the first rain of autumn: a decorative custom from the founding
families, older than the Rain Church, which the Rain Church considers blasphemy.

### Holy objects that sell

Founding-era bolts (for Saint Pump), drip-candles, Works lunch tins, grey thread, Rainmarks (06a), clock badges (never sold by
an Old Hand; sold by their heirs, which is a scandal every time), chalk.

**In play:**
- **Tin offerings** are a quiet daily flavour on floors next to the Dark Floors and a source of a hook: a player with a lease on
  21 could find a tin on their own doorstep one morning, returned, washed, with something in it that came from the other side.
- **Rise meetings** and *Halden Hours* booklets are Middle clutter in the West Chute; a booklet is a 1x1 item worth nothing,
  unless a Middle client asks for one to show they attend.
- **Bolts for Saint Pump:** any founding-era bolt sells for 0.50 cr on 5 to people who want to leave one. A player with a bin of
  founding-era fasteners from the Bench has a tiny pious income.

## Part ten: entertainment, gambling and music

### Static

**Static** (the pirate radio, 06b has the station) is the Mills' company in the dark. A receiver costs 12 cr in the Mills; most
households have one, hidden or not. The day's programmes, as the Mills know them:
- ***The Rate*** (05:30): the street rate for scrip, chits and Hope Vouchers, the price of a litre and of a Vitabrick.
- ***The Tide*** (06:00): the bay tides, slack hour for the day, weather (the Sounding's forecast, read out a day late because
  Static can't afford it fresh), and the news the Company channel doesn't carry.
- ***The Names*** (20:00): requests for the dead. People send in names and a song; the host reads the
  name and the floor and plays the song. Every night has its list.
- ***The Floor*** (22:00, after last whistle): Co-op Floor meetings live when there is one, Arbiter determinations read out
  with the sums, and which floors the inspectors walked today.
- The sign-off, every night: "*That's the seven. Keep your head above it.*"

### The Company channel

**Halden Daily** plays on the lobby screens and on cheap household screens: the price board, notices, Bylaw amendments, Ledger
reminders, and the evening shows:
- ***Climb***: the most-watched programme in the tower. Middle contestants answer questions about bylaws, Company history and
  household budgeting on a set built like a staircase; each right answer is a step; the winner of the week gets a **year's
  lease on a floor of their choice up to 59** and is photographed in the Glass Bank. Losers go home with a bound Charter. In the
  Mills it is watched in crust halls, ironically and very closely, and audiences cheer the losers. Every Mills person can name
  the one Mills contestant ever to reach the top step (**Delphine Okoro**, Y77, floor 13), and that she lost her lease on 56
  within two years.
- ***The Halden Hour***: news. The Lifeline barges, Rate Review results, interviews with satisfied tenants.
- ***Tower Kitchen***: an Orrin cookery show in which a Terraces chef cooks Vitabrick nine ways. Middle families take notes.

### Gambling

- **The Fall book.** Bets on the count of bangs in the Morning Fall, on a chosen trunk, at 1 cr a call; or on "the first big
  thing" (the first item out of a chosen catch: furniture, a screen, a cake). Run by crust-hall bookies like Dusty Kerrigan on 16.
  Pays fast; argued over forever.
- **The Two Clock.** Bets on when Old Two will reach a floor, to the minute. Its timetable is wrong on every landing, so the odds
  are real. Lift Guild operators are forbidden to bet, and are the best-informed gamblers in the tower.
- **Chirp matches.** Not fights: singing. Ranchers keep a singing cricket in a small cage (a **chirp box**, carved brick or wire)
  and match them in crust halls; a judge with a stopwatch (by tradition blindfolded) times the longest unbroken song. A champion
  singer sells for 20 cr; the best breed lines are secrets. Dee Kasprzak does not compete; she judges.
- **The Ascension Draw.** The Company lottery, sold at every Hatch: 1 sc a ticket, scrip only. Drawn on *Halden Daily* on the
  first of each month; the prize is a Middle lease for a year. "Nobody you know has ever won" is true, and is also the slogan
  of a Static parody.
- **Rent** (the card game). A pack of 52 floor cards in four bands (Sump, Mills, Middle, Terraces: no Crown cards, the Crown is
  the pot). Players climb by playing higher floors and pay the player above them in chips each round. It is a game about losing
  slowly. Crust halls play it all night.
- **Tide dice.** Flats and Sump: two dice marked with tide states; a throw "ebbs" or "floods" your stake.
- **Empty runs.** Shaft rats race drones down **the Empty** (Freight One's dead well, 02) from 60 to 10, at night, for stakes
  and reputation. Illegal, loud, watched from every hatch.

### Drink and going out

- **Crust halls** (Mills): long rooms with benches, crust and Mill ale on tap, a screen for *Climb*, a slate, a card table. The
  Mills' living room. Most have a name from the Works: **the Coater**, **Bake Road Rooms**, **Last Line**.
- **The Old Post** on 8 (Sump): the cage-front bar; Ninefold white (gin from crust dregs).
- **Middle cafés**: Kof and a pastry made of brick and syrup, in places with real-looking tablecloths; you sit for an hour on one
  cup.
- **Lantern Row** (75): the Terraces' restaurants.
- **The Pleasure Pier** (07): the Crown's and Terraces' night out, by boat. Mills people know it from Static and from the
  wrappers that come down the chute after a Pier night.

### Music

Mills music is work music that outlived the work.
- **Shift songs** from the Works days, sung in the halls, in the crust halls, at Carries. Each line had its own: ***Line Four***
  (the clean-room women's song, slow, about gloves), ***Coater Run*** (fast, for hauling), ***Bake Road*** (a call-and-answer for
  the furnace crews), ***Ninety-Nine Steps*** (about the Long Stair). Old Hands know all the words. Young people know the tunes.
- **Instruments** from the tower's scrap: the **pipe-horn** (a length of founding-era conduit with a mouthpiece, one deep
  note); **tin drums** from cell cans; the **comb** (a founding-era heat sink, all fins, played with a rod, sounds like rain on a
  roof); fiddles made of real-wood scraps from the North Chute, strung with Crown wire. A **real** instrument (a guitar, a violin)
  comes down the North Chute about once a year, voided (neck snapped), and is mended by somebody, and becomes the most
  famous object on its floor.
- **The Hum note.** Mills musicians tune to the Hum. The Dark Floors' hum is a steady low note (the Old Hands put it a little
  under a B-flat), audible at night on the floors around 22–26, and Mills songs are pitched to it, so that singing at night on
  21 sounds like singing with the building.
- **Crown music**: a string quartet hired from the Pleasure Pier for dinners; a real piano in the Assembly on 100; recordings.
- **Middle music**: whatever *Halden Daily* plays between shows, cheerful company jingles (the Supply jingle, "*Halden Pure, for
  you and yours*"), and choir in the Halden School.
- **Static** plays everything, especially songs the Company would rather not hear: ***The Wall Book***, ***Sluice Four***,
  ***Who Shut the Door***.

### Sport and dares

- **The Stair Run** on Founding Day: a race up the Long Stair from 4 to 30, sponsored by the Company; the prize is a year's
  Locals pass. Mills runners train on the Corner Stairs all year.
- **Ledge-walking**: a dare among Mills teenagers, walking the forty-centimetre drip ledge outside a cut window from one window
  to the next. Fatal several times a year. Banned by the Company, the Co-op and every parent; done anyway.
- **Pick-up drone racing** with toy-sized frames down the Corner Stairwells.

**In play:**
- **A Fall bet** each morning (1 cr, a single number) is a micro-gamble the player can ignore; it gives the Morning Fall a
  number to care about.
- **Crust halls are clients**: crust and Mill ale by the cask, roast crickets in bulk, cards, chalk, a mended screen for
  *Climb* night.
- **Chirp boxes and singing crickets** are a small luxury good from the insect chain: one cricket in a carved box, sold to a
  hall or to a Middle collector who thinks it's folk art.
- **A voided instrument** in the North Chute is a rare repair project with a single, enormous buyer (a crust hall, a Crown
  heir who wants "a Mills guitar"), and a choice.
- **Static's *Lost Shift*** could carry the player's own requests: send in a name and a song (costs nothing) and floor
  goodwill rises slightly. Static's sign-off can close every in-game day.

## Part eleven: fashion and status

### Clothes, by band

- **Crown:** natural fibres, undyed or dyed with real dyes, cut for people who do not carry things. No pockets on Crown
  clothing, because nobody in the Crown carries anything. Shoes that have never been wet.
- **Terraces:** office wear in good synthetics, certified-real accents (a silk scarf, a wool collar, a leather tally sleeve),
  lift passes worn outside the jacket on a lanyard so everyone can see the colour. Terraces women wear **Gill coats** (light,
  long, pale) because the Gills make the corridors draughty.
- **Middle:** pressed. The Middle's whole style is **pressed**: collars stiff with starch made from insect chitin, creases sharp,
  shoes polished, everything clean and **mended invisibly**. A Middle man owns one mended Crown silk shirt (45 cr, X patched so
  well nobody can see it) for interviews and *Climb* auditions. The colour of the Middle is brown (brownglass light makes
  everything brown anyway, so Middle people buy brown to look intentional).
- **Mills:** Halden workwear grey (seam glue, eighty washes), and everything the South Chute drops, **crossed**. Aprons with
  many pockets. Knee pads. Headlamps on elastic. Cough-cloths. Rubber clogs for wet floors. Mills fashion is pockets and
  mending.
- **Sump:** **wetskins** (oilcloth jackets and trousers made from Crown tablecloths, waxed with insect fat), rubber boots,
  everything above the knee dry and everything below it stained.
- **Flats:** rope belts, splatchers, faces wrapped against sun and wind, clothes faded white by daylight. Flats people are the only
  people in Calder who are sunburnt.

### Crossing: the X as a style

When a disposal valet voids a garment, he slashes it with an **X** across the chest or back (04, 06b). A mender in the Mills
patches the X. How you patch it says where you are:
- **The Middle** patches **invisibly**: matched thread, darned so the X disappears. Visible mending is shame.
- **The Mills** patch **in contrast**: red thread on grey, a patch cut from another garment, the X turned into a star or a
  window. A **crossed** shirt is a Mills shirt and Mills people wear it with pride. Some cross their own clothes on purpose.
- **The Crown** this season wants crossed shirts as street fashion (Linnet, above). A real crossed shirt, mended in the Mills,
  sells to a Crown fixer for 300 cr. A fake (a new shirt with an X sewn on) is detected by the **wear**: a real voided shirt has
  been worn by its first owner, and the Crown, it turns out, can tell.

### Status markers that are not clothes

- **The tally.** Its colour is your Standing (red, amber, white, blue, silver; 04). People hide red tallies under sleeves, and
  show silver ones. Middle people buy **tally sleeves** in silver-coloured cloth. Crown residents do not wear tallies: staff
  touch theirs for them.
- **Skin.** Daylight is the deepest status marker: Crown residents are **gold**, Terraces people **sunned**, everyone below 30
  pale. Middle people buy a **sunlamp hour** at Halden Select (8 cr) before a wedding.
- **Teeth.** Etch-teeth (03) mark a Mills childhood for life. Terraces dentistry can whiten them for 400 cr. The surest sign of a
  Mills person who has made it is a white smile in a lined face.
- **Hands.** Shakes, frass stain, solder burns, bin rash: the Pick reads them, and so does everyone.
- **Hair.** Long hair below 30 means you have never had to sell it (hair is 20 cr a head to the Crown's wigmakers, 04). Cropped
  hair on a woman means she sold it. Mills women wear their hair long and pinned up as a statement; Middle women wear wigs
  made from Mills hair.
- **Smell.** The richest smell is **nothing**. Crown residents smell of nothing; Varne & Daughters sell a perfume called
  ***Nothing*** (a neutraliser in a green Skywater-style bottle, 140 cr) to Terraces people who want to. Middle people cover the
  Mills smell (insects, solder, frying) with **Orrin Clean Linen** scent sticks. Mills people smell of their trade and say so.
- **Dry shoes.** "She's got dry shoes" means she's comfortable. Wet feet mean the Sump or a Mills hall with a weep.
- **A window.** Owning a lease with a cut window is the Mills' great status; owning a **working light pipe** is the Middle's.
- **Badges and cords.** Old Hands' clock badges on cords; the Co-op's green cord on the arm; the Widows' notch (a notched bolt
  worn on a string by divers' families); a Rain Church Rainmark on the hand; Lift Guild members' brass car-key pins.
- **Something real.** A real-wood thing, a real plant, a real egg on a birthday: the Mills keep one real object somewhere
  visible, the way the Middle keeps its onion.

**In play:**
- **Mending as grades:** a mended voided garment can carry its **mend style** as a property: invisible (sells to the Middle,
  needs a fine thread and more hours) or crossed (sells to the Mills and, in season, to the Crown at absurd prices). Same item,
  two finishes, two markets.
- **Fashion seasons** as events: "crossing is in" (the Crown buys crossed shirts); "tally sleeves for Tally Day"; "Gill coats in
  the Gill season" (the Terraces want light pale coats; the South Chute drops last year's).
- **Hair** as a client story: the woman who sells her hair at the counter for a jar of real soil (04).

## Part twelve: holidays and the calendar

The year has twelve numbered months of thirty days (dated "14.IV.86"). The Company year begins on Founding Day; below 30 nobody
counts from it. The week is seven days; the seventh is **rent day** (the Ledger runs rent at midnight).

### The weekly holiday: Seventh

Rent day is the tower's sabbath in reverse. The evening before it, **Thumb Night** (after the Ledger's thumb), is when the Mills
settle up: slates are wiped, markers are paid, jugs are cashed, turns are called in, and the plasma cart comes up to the Mills
(04). Crust halls are quiet on Thumb Night. On **Seventh** itself, after midnight's rent has gone through, there is relief or
there is not. The Middle has its onion. Mills families who made rent eat a **seventh supper** with something extra in it. Mills
families who did not go to Granny Oyelaran's hock on 11 in the morning.

### The year

**In play:**
- **The calendar is a demand curve.** Each holiday has a shopping list:
  - *Wall Night*: candles (tallow or wax), rain or any clean water, flowers (real, rare), toll-free deliveries down.

  supper (small food contracts), the hock (buy forfeits cheap the week after).

## Part thirteen: how people talk

### Accents and registers

- **Crown** speech is quiet, slow and impersonal: things "are seen to", "go down", "are dealt with". The Crown never names a floor
  number below 60; everything below is "downstairs". Crown children call staff by their function ("Cook", "Valet").
- **Terraces** speech is Company speech: "at this time", "going forward", "in line with". Terraces people say "the Middle" with
  a slight drop in pitch, and "the Mills" not at all.
- **Middle** speech is careful, polite, full of numbers (Standing, niche numbers, the water book) and of brand names said in full
  ("a Vitabrick Savoury", never "a brick"). A Middle person who says "brick" is slipping.
- **Mills** speech is fast, loud (halls are noisy), full of manual-speak, Works words and numbers used as names. Floors, clocks,
  pillars and squares: "I'm seventeen, four pillars in, on six squares." Terraces people call it **etch-tooth**.
- **Sump** speech is Mills speech wet: slower, with tide words for time ("by flood", "at slack") and Ninefold terms for money
  ("a ninth", "a load").
- **Flats** speech counts in tides and calls the tower **the Nail**, its people **drys**. Flats-born names are often truncated by
  the Ledger's 24-character limit, and Flats people sometimes keep the truncation as a second name, proudly.

### Proverbs and sayings

Water sayings are in 03. These are the rest.

**From the Mills**
- **"Everything falls but rent."** Prices, people, luck, rubbish: all of it comes down. Rent only goes up.
- **"A full catch catches nothing."** Clear your bin or lose your share; use what you have before you want more. (It is also how
  the game's chute works.)
- **"What goes down the chute comes up the stairs at three times the price."** Said of anything restored, resold or relabelled
  (Anselm Dray's whole business).
- **"Don't count the bangs before the Fall."** Don't count your chickens.
- **"The Whistle doesn't know it's dead."** Habits, rules and institutions outlive their reasons. Said of the Company, the Lift
  Guild, marriages.
- **"Brick today, brick tomorrow, brick at your wake."** Life is the same, so enjoy the onion when it comes.
- **"Every floor is somebody's ceiling."** However low you are, someone is beneath you; however high, someone is above.
- **"Climb by the stair, fall by the chute."** Rising is slow and costs; falling is quick and free.
- **"Never sell your last cell."** Keep a reserve.
- **"First-fit outlives the fitter."** Good work lasts longer than the person who did it; also, the Works will outlast us all.
- **"Good neighbours share a weep."** Neighbourliness is splitting a free drip, a hose, a cell.
- **"Don't build on a weep."** Don't depend on a free thing; it stops.
- **"Isolate before servicing."** Calm down before you act.
- **"Keep one glove on."** Stay ready; don't let them see you finished. (From the Line Four woman who wore her glove to dinner,
  01.)
- **"The Hum never takes a holiday."** Machines don't rest, so neither can we; also, whatever is in there is always working.
- **"A brick's a brick, whoever pressed it."** Don't be a snob about slab. (Said by people who buy brick for their babies.)
- **"Scrub your own blue."** Deal with your own problems; don't hand them over unscrubbed.

**From the Middle**
- **"Two missed rents and a cough."** The distance between the Middle and the Mills.
- **"Iron your shirt, they'll think you're on fifty."** Appearances are rising.
- **"The Ledger remembers kindness."** (From *Halden Hours*.) Said sincerely in the Middle and as a joke everywhere else, where the
  answer is "and forgives nothing".
- **"Mind your niche."** Mind your own business; pay your dues.

**From the Sump and the Flats**
- **"Wet feet, dry purse."** If you're standing in the water, you're working for someone else.
- **"The bay keeps better books than Halden."** The tide is never late; the sea collects what it's owed.
- **"Nothing from the water goes in the mouth."** The first rule; also, don't take what's offered by a poisoned hand.
- **"Eight for the Mills, one for the Nine."** Every road has a toll.
- **"The pump comes first."** The common good before your own business.

**From the Crown** (as overheard by staff)
- **"One should never know the floor of one's food."** Good taste is not asking where things come from.
- **"Waste is a kind of confidence."**
- **"Downstairs will manage."**

**In play:** proverbs are free flavour that teaches rules. A rule tooltip can carry one: the chute container's ("A full catch
catches nothing"), the rent screen's ("Everything falls but rent"), the cell rack's ("Never sell your last cell"), a grey
item's ("What goes down the chute comes up the stairs at three times the price"). Clients can speak them on slips.

## Part fourteen: sounds and smells

The Stack is a building you could find your way around blind. Mills children play a game, **Where Am I**, where one child is
led blindfolded up or down the Corner Stairs and has to name the floor from the sound and the smell. Good players get it to
within two floors.

### The Crown (90–104)

**Sound:** almost nothing, on purpose. Rain on the Founders' Gutter. A fountain that runs because it can. Carpet. The soft
click of the Cradle's doors. A string quartet on a dinner night. Under it all, if you are staff in the Pantry, the Lung roaring
and the cisterns ticking.
**Smell:** nothing, which is the most expensive smell there is; then, close up: real soil in the orchard troughs, lemon leaves,
bread from the Pantry at six, chlorine from the Long Bath, the floral decontamination mist at the Ninety Gate. On still nights
in the Lantern, faintly, solvent from the riser caps.

### The Terraces (60–89)

**Sound:** air handling, lift chimes, clinic pager tones, quiet voices in corridors "as if somebody important might be
asleep" (02), the Gills whistling on windy days, cutlery on Lantern Row. White-noise machines in bedrooms.
**Smell:** coffee (real on 75, Kof elsewhere), disinfectant, Fresh Plus scent cartridges, rented pot plants, the faint sugary
smell of Orrin's test kitchen on 80 that makes everyone on 79 and 81 hungry at 15:00.

### The Middle (30–59)

**Sound:** radios, the Locals' chime, school bells on 50, the 30 Lung's up-feed breathing like a tide, neighbours through thin
walls, the corridor lights' **blink** before a power shed, *Climb*'s theme tune at 19:00 from every door at once.
**Smell:** cooking brick (toast, slightly sweet), detergent, the seventh-day onion, damp in the inner corridors where the light
pipes have failed, Clean Linen scent sticks, the Shelf Market's dye stall and fish-paste kitchen coming up the 30 Lung's louvres
at noon.

### The Mills (10–29)

**Sound:** a hall full of small machines (fans, pumps, grinders, presses thumping), three radio stations at once, the **chirp**
from the insect halls on 11–13 coming up through the floor, the dead conveyors creaking as the building moves, kids, hawkers.
The **Works Whistle** at 06:00, 14:00 and 22:00. The **Morning Fall**: ten minutes of roar in the trunks and every catch
clanking open and shut. Old Two's bell. Socket boards dying all at once at slack hour, and the sudden quiet. At night, on the
floors around 22–26, the **Hum**.
**Smell:** warm insects (nutty, with ammonia under it), wet soil, solder, hot cells, frying in insect fat, chilli, crust brewing
(sour bread), a still somewhere, wet sweeping (grey water on concrete), people.

### The Sump (4–9)

**Sound:** the tide booming in the drowned floors below, twice a day, a slow wet thud felt in the feet; pumps; dripping in a
hundred places; the **Jaws** on 8 when the Fall lands; the Pumpmen's **horn** (three long blasts: everyone to the pumps); gulls at
the Landing; the Bastion boat's engine on Thursdays.
**Smell:** salt, rot, methane from the drains, gin, tallow smoke, frying, and at low tide the sweet chemical breath from the
drowned floors that old people say smells exactly like the forming rooms used to.

### The Flats

**Sound:** wind, water, gulls, a rope creaking on a stake, children calling the tide, the tower's Whistle faint across the mud
at shift change, the Spill Door's ramp booming when the bales go out.
**Smell:** mud (sulphur and salt), weed, the bay's chemical edge, tar, tallow, and, when the wind is from the tower, the Mills'
insect halls breathing out of the Plinth's louvres.

**In play:**
- **Audio as information.** The Whistle (shift change, customers move), the Fall (the catch is full), the blink (slack hour
  coming), the pump horn (Sump suppliers closed), Old Two's bell (a freight delivery has arrived), a silent bin (a colony is
  dying), the Hum (night on the floors near the Dark Floors). Each is a sound the player learns to act on, which is the cheapest
  possible way to make the world feel present.
- **Backdrop by floor:** the field's backdrop (IDEAS: "looks follow the data") can carry the band's sound as well as its light.
  A player who moves floors hears the move.

### Flavour for slips

Slips should sound like the people who send them. Some lines, by band, for the generator:

- **Crown** (cream card, embossed, violet or black ink): *"To be delivered to the Postern, cradle 31. The household requires
  that the item be authentic."* / *"Staff consignment. Do not send anything blue."*

  next load."*
- **Flats** (dated by tide, scratched on a tin lid): *"Tollgate berth. By second low Thursday. Slab 10, gut tabs, thread.
  Pays first look."*
