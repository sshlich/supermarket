# 01. History of Calder and the Stack

Speculative. Plain text is the main version; **Alternative:** marks another version of the same thing; **Hook:** marks a
mystery or story seed; **In play:** marks where it surfaces in the game. Dates are in years since the Stack opened (Y0).
Negative years (Y−12) are before it.

---

## How people count the years

The Company counts from the opening of Halden Works: Y0. Every lease, meter reading and Arbiter ruling is stamped with a
Halden Year, and the Company calendar starts each year on **Founding Day** (the 1st of the Fourth Month, the day the first
cell came off Line One). Nobody below the Terraces dates anything from Founding Day except in paperwork.

Below floor 60, people count from the water. A thing happened "before the water" or "after the water", and a person is
"a dry-born" (born before Y57) or "a wet-born" (after). Older Mills people still say "in the Works days" for anything before
Y41, and "after the Shift" for the sixteen years between the layoffs and the flood. Flats people count tides, not years: a
Flats elder will tell you she has seen "eleven thousand tides since the Wall", and she has done the arithmetic.

The present is Y86. A dry-born who remembers the flood as an adult is at least in her late forties. A Works hand who was on
the lines before the Shift is past sixty-five. The people who built the tower are dead.

**In play:** contract slips from above are dated in Halden Years ("Due 14.IV.86, 16:00"). Slips from the Mills and the Sump
are dated by day of the week or not at all. A slip from a Flats client is dated by the tide ("by second low water").
Readable at a glance: the date format tells you who wrote it.

## Before Y0: Calder Bay

### The town that was there

Calder was a delta town. The Calder river came down to the bay through reed flats and split into a dozen channels before it
met the sea, and the town grew on the silt between them: a salt-pan and fishing town of maybe thirty thousand people, a
cannery, two boatyards, a ferry to the south shore, a market called **Saltmarket** that sold eels, samphire and salt by the
sack. The good streets stood on the one bit of high ground, a low gravel ridge the locals called **the Knuckle**; the rest
of the town was a few metres above the sea and drained by ditches and tide gates.

The town had been sinking slowly for a century, the way delta towns do, and had built a seawall to cope with it: the
**Calder Wall**, a stone-faced earth bank along the bay front, raised twice by the town council, the second time in Y−40.
People were proud of it. There were postcards.

Under the delta, under thirty metres of silt and clay, lay the reason Halden came: the **Sweetwater Beds**, a sand and gravel
aquifer fed from the hills inland, holding water so low in dissolved minerals that the old salt-workers used to rinse their
pans with it. Well-diggers in Calder had always known the deep water was strange: "soft as rain, and it tastes of nothing".

### Why Halden came

**Ansel Halden** (Y−58 to Y31) made his first money in battery chemistry and his second in buying the companies that did it
better. By Y−15 his firm made power cells and the control chips that managed them, in rented plants scattered across three
provinces, and he wanted one plant, one site, one workforce, under one roof he owned.

A cell-and-chip works needs four things in quantity: ultrapure water, power, a port, and people who will stay. Calder Bay had
the first in a form nobody else had (the Sweetwater Beds needed less treatment than any river water on the coast), a
deep-water channel for the third, and a dying fishing economy that would supply the fourth. Power, he would build himself.

The province wanted him badly. The **Calder Development Accord** (Y−11) gave Halden a forty-year exemption from land tax,
"abstraction rights to subsurface waters sufficient for the operation of the Works, without limit of volume", and a promise
that the province would never charge him for water drawn from beneath his own land. In return, Calder's council, which could
barely afford its own seawall, got the clause it asked for: **Clause 14**, under which the Company "shall assume the
maintenance of the Calder Wall and its tide gates in good and sufficient repair for so long as the Works shall draw water
under this Accord". Free water for a wall. Everybody thought it was a good deal for the town.

The Accord is still the oldest document the Company quotes in disputes. The two clauses the Archivists keep copies of on
every floor they can reach are the water clause and Clause 14, printed back to back on one sheet, because they are one
bargain, and only one half of it was kept.

The Company formed the **Halden Seawall Section** in Y0 to do the work: about three hundred masons, pump men, sluice keepers
and tide watchers, the **wall gangs**, housed not in the Stack (outdoor workers did not get tower flats) but in **the Strand**,
the low harbour district under the Wall. They kept a log of every tide, surge, crack and repair, the **Wall Book**. (Their
descendants are the core of the Tide Folk: file 06a.)

Halden bought the salt-pans and the marsh north of Saltmarket through four different land companies over two years (the
**Saltmarsh purchases**), so the price did not move until it was too late. The cannery sold last. Its owner held out for a
seat on the Works board and got it, which is why one Crown family today still carries a cannery's name.

> "I am not building a factory. A factory is a place men go to and leave. I am building the place they live, and the place
> they work, and the place their children will go to school, and I am putting them on top of one another so that nobody
> ever has to leave."
> Ansel Halden, address to the Provincial Assembly, Y−11

**Alternative:** Halden did not choose Calder for the water. He chose it because the province offered the Accord and nobody
else would, and the aquifer was luck, discovered by the first test bores. This version is popular with the Old Hands, who
like the idea that the whole thing was an accident of paperwork.

**Hook:** the original Accord has a schedule of land parcels attached. One parcel number (Schedule B, lot 41) appears in the
Accord but on no Company land register since. Archivists argue whether it was the seawall strip, the lagoon site, or a piece
of the Knuckle that still stands above water.

**In play:** a framed reproduction of the **Accord water clause** is a decorative item Crown clients buy ("Heritage print,
Halden Archive edition"). A torn original page of the Accord, if anyone ever finds one, is an Archivist contract worth more
than a month's rent, and a liability to be caught carrying.

## The promise

### A city stood on end

The prospectus the Company printed for recruits in Y−3 was called **A City Stood On End**. Twenty pages, heavy cream paper,
a cutaway drawing of the tower on the cover with tiny figures in every floor: a woman hanging washing on a sunlit balcony,
children in a classroom, men in white suits behind glass, a bus waiting at the base. Recruitment offices in four provinces
gave it away. Thousands still existed after the flood. A few hundred survive, mostly as wall decoration in the Middle.

What it promised, in the order it promised it:

1. **A job for life** on the lines of Halden Works, with training paid.
2. **A home in the Residences**, tied to the job: "your tenancy follows your employment, and your employment is for life".
3. **Rain for everyone**: the roof would catch it and the tower would share it, floor by floor, by gravity.
4. **Clean air** "manufactured to clean-room standard", piped to every flat.
5. **The Commissary**: food, clothes and goods at cost, on every tenth floor.
6. **Schools, a clinic and a chapel** inside the tower; the **Common** on the podium roof, a park in the sky.
7. **A pension** "drawn from the Works' own reserves, as long as the Works stands".

The small print, which almost nobody read, said the tenancy was a **tied tenancy** (it ended when employment ended, for any
reason), that rent and utilities were deducted from wages at source, and that the Commissary could, "at the Company's
discretion, accept Works tokens in lieu of currency". Every item in the small print was later used.

> "Ask your father where he lived. Ask him how far he walked to work. Ask him what his water tasted of. Then come to Calder,
> where the walk is a lift ride, and the water tastes of nothing at all."
> *A City Stood On End*, page 4

**In play:** the prospectus is a collectible paper item (1x2). Middle clients pay for clean copies, Archivists for annotated
ones (a previous owner's margin notes are sometimes the only record of what a floor looked like). A water-stained copy is
worth a fraction. It is also a good gift item for an Old Hand client: improves standing with the faction more than its cr value.

### Who came

The first intake, Y−1 to Y2, was eleven thousand workers and their families: fishing families from Calder itself (hired for
the jobs nobody wanted: wellheads, lagoon, refuse), skilled hands poached from Halden's older plants, and young people from
the inland farm provinces, who arrived by bus with the prospectus in their pockets. By Y10 the Works employed twenty-two
thousand. About half of them lived in the Stack's six thousand flats, three to a room in the early years; the rest lived in
the town, which tripled. Peak employment, in Y35, was a little over thirty-one thousand, and the Stack housed about forty
thousand people.

The Calder fishing families never quite stopped being "townies" in the tower. The inland recruits were "buses". Their
grandchildren, in the Mills, still use both words as insults without knowing why.

## Building the Stack (Y−7 to Y0)

### The architect

The Stack was designed by **Ilse Marrow** of the firm **Marrow Teague**, who had never built anything taller than eleven
floors before. Halden hired her because she was the only architect who answered his brief with a single building instead of
a campus. Her drawing set, called **the Marrow Set**, runs to four thousand sheets. The Archivists hold roughly a third of it,
scattered, water-damaged, often out of sequence. Nobody holds all of it. The Company claims to and has never shown it.

Marrow's ideas, in her own words from her notes:

- "Industry at the root, life in the branches. The heavy plant goes where the ground can take it, and the people go where
  the light is."
- "No pipe that cannot be reached. No cable that cannot be pulled. The floor is a lid on the services, and every lid opens."
- "Water falls. Let it. Everything we pump uphill, we pay for forever."
- "Build it so that nobody needs to understand it to live in it, and so that somebody always can."

The last line is carved over the door of the Old Hands' hall on floor 19. The second half of it stopped being true around Y80.

### The shape

The Stack is one building in two parts:

- **The Plinth** (floors 0 to 29): the Works. A broad, deep base of tall industrial floors, six metres floor to floor,
  wider than the tower above it on every side. Floors 0 to 2 were dug below grade into the delta clay (the **Well Hall** with
  its wellheads, the pump galleries that kept the dug-in floors dry, the chemical vaults); floor 3 was the street floor, the
  **Gate Floor**, where the public doors and the loading quays opened onto Calder; floors 5 to 8 held the **Waterhouse**,
  the plant that turned groundwater into ultrapure process water; floors 9 to 29 were the fab halls, cell lines, chemical
  stores, power hall and offices.
- **The tower** (floors 30 to 104), **the Shaft**: the Residences. A narrower shaft of normal floors, three and a half metres floor to
  floor, rising from the Plinth's roof around a single concrete **core**. The Plinth's roof, at floor 30, was **the Common**:
  trees in planters, a running track, a bandstand, open to the sky on three sides.

Three floors were built differently from the rest, and they are why the floor bands break where they do. Floor **30** was
the Plinth's roof, where the Works ended and the homes began: a structural transfer deck, thicker than any other slab, with
the header tanks for the Works below it. Floors **60 to 62** carried **the Gills**, the band of louvres where the tower
breathes in, and the header tanks for the middle storeys. Floor **90** was the transfer to the Crown's lighter frame and the
roof cisterns. Each was a natural wall between one part of the tower and the next. When the Company later wanted to sell the
top of the tower to one kind of person and the middle to another, it found the walls already there, and put a turnstile in
each.

The top four floors above 100 were not in Marrow's first set. Halden added them in Y−4: the **Glasshouse**, a glass crown for
the Company's own offices and his family's apartments (the Company renamed it **the Lantern** after Y57; the Mills still use both names), which is why the tower has 104 floors and not 100. Marrow objected in
writing: the Glasshouse sat directly over the cap of the Works' exhaust risers. Halden overruled her. The Glasshouse got its
own roof air, filtered separately, and its windows were never opened.

**Alternative:** the Glasshouse was always in the plan, and the 104 is symbolic: the Accord was signed on the 104th day of
the year Y−11. The Company tells this version on Founding Day.

### The founding-era kit

Marrow Teague and Halden's own engineers built the tower with the best of what existed then, and with several things that
were made specially and never made again because the factories that made them were Halden's own lines, retooled after Y0 for
cells and chips. These are the things that are failing now. People call original parts **first-fit** ("is that first-fit or
a copy?"); the Company calls them "heritage infrastructure".

- **The lattice.** The tower's frame is a cast composite lattice around the core, poured on site in a process Marrow's notes
  call "slow casting". It flexes in wind and is meant to; old residents can feel the tower lean on storm nights. Nobody has
  poured lattice since Y0. Cracked lattice members are bracketed with steel, which the lattice does not like.
- **Lease tiles.** Every floor in the Stack is laid in lift-out tiles, grey composite, each a lid on the services underneath
  (water, power, data, air, drain) and each with a brass stud and a coin-sized sensor that reported load and use to the
  engineers. Marrow's rule that "every lid opens" made the building maintainable. It also made it countable. The engineers
  called them service tiles. After the Drowning the Company started calling them lease tiles, and what it bills for under
  each one is a **square** of rent: the cell of your board, with no size the Company will put in writing. Your shop's grid is
  the floor of an old fab hall. (See The Partition, below.)
- **The Lung.** The air plants on the three mechanical floors (30, 60 and 90), each with four fan rooms around the core, founding-era electrostatic **plates**, supply and
  return trunks to every floor, fresh air drawn through the Gills on 60 to 62. Built to clean-room standard for the Works
  and generously for the Residences. Fan Room Three of the 30 Lung died in Y79. The air you breathe in the Mills has been breathed by the
  Middle first.
- **The rain taps.** The roof's catchment ring, the **Founders' Gutter**, fed settling tanks and downpipes that ran the whole
  height of the tower. On every tenth landing from 90 down to 10 there was a brass **rain tap** with RAIN cast in its cap:
  free, unmetered washing water, because rain was cheaper than pumping and clean workers got sick less. Marrow's "water
  falls, let it". This is the system that was capped in Y61. (See the Capping, below; the Rain Church's side of it is in 06a.)
- **The lifts.** Ropeless linear-motor cars built by **Kessin Vertical** to a Halden specification, several cars to a
  shaft, each driven independently by magnetic motors set into the shaft walls, in **six cores** lettered A to F: the
  Glasshouse and directors' express, passenger cores, and the two great freight cores that carried plant materials from
  the Gate Floor quays. Fast, quiet, and serviceable only by people trained on them. Nobody alive can make their drive chips.
  The Lift Guild holds the training now. (Which cars still run: files 02 and 06a.)
- **The Tube.** A pneumatic post and parcel system: sealed capsules sent through dedicated tubes from the Works offices to
  every tenth floor. Its tube shafts are the **licensed lanes** that drones fly today, with the Company's transponder gates
  bolted where the capsule stations used to be.
- **The risers.** The Works' exhaust and process-gas risers, eight of them, running from the fab halls up through the core to
  vent at the roof under the Glasshouse. Workers called the whole building after them: the Company's name for it was **Halden
  Works & Residences**, and nobody ever called it that. It was the Stack, because it smoked. Most risers went cold after
  Y41. The cold ones are the **free shafts**. One is not cold. (See the Dark Floors.)
- **The meters.** Every flat, every workshop, every tap and socket in the building had a **Sorrel meter** from day one: small
  brass-faced units with a glass eye, recording water, power and air drawn. They were not for billing. In the Works days,
  rent and utilities were a flat deduction from wages; the meters fed the engineers' allocation models so the Lung and the
  header tanks could be balanced. In Y43 someone in the Company asked why they were not being billed from. (See the
  Landlord Years.)
- **The Ledger.** The account system that paid the Works payroll and billed six thousand flats on the same day without a
  clerk. It still runs, in the **Count House** on 71, and it still rounds in the Company's favour. (Money: file 04.)
- **The refuse system.** Pneumatic refuse inlets on every landing fed sorting rooms on floor 8 (today's Bale Hall), which sent recyclables to the
  Works and the rest to barges. After the flood the sorting rooms were wrecked, the pneumatics failed, and the inlets became
  gravity chutes. What comes down them every morning is the **garbage chute** in your shop. (See Chute Day, below.)

**Hook:** Marrow's notes mention a **ninth riser**, "R9, reserve, capped", drawn in the first set and absent from every
later sheet anyone has seen. Shaft rats claim there is a sealed duct that runs from the Dark Floors to somewhere above 90.

**In play:** first-fit parts are the most valuable salvage in the game. A **Sorrel meter (first-fit)**, a **Kessin rail
segment**, a **Lung plate**, a **lease tile (original, with maker's stamp)**, a **Tube capsule**. Each is worth
more than any new equivalent, and each has an obvious buyer: the Company (who will pay in scrip and ask where you got it),
the Lift Guild, the Old Hands, a penthouse fixer. Who you sell a first-fit part to is a choice with consequences for the
tower, not just your margin.

### The Forty

Construction took seven years and killed forty-three workers, most in the slow-casting of the lattice above floor 60, where
the forms were poured in winter. The Company erected a plaque on the Common: "In memory of the Forty, who built higher than
any before them." The number on the plaque was wrong from the day it went up; the three missing names were casters hired by
a subcontractor and paid in cash. The plaque was moved indoors in Y63 when the Common was leased, and now hangs in a stairwell
landing on 31 behind a vending machine.

**In play:** "Find the Forty plaque" is a small Archivist or Old Hand errand early on: a reason to learn that floors can be
visited, and a lesson in what the Company does with memory.

### Opening day

Founding Day, Y0. The first cell came off Line One at 06:12. Ansel Halden carried it in his bare hand from the line on 12 up
to the Common on 30 in a Kessin car, and placed it in a glass case on a plinth by the bandstand. The case is still in the
Company's Glasshouse reception. The cell is still charged, according to the Company. Nobody has been allowed to test it.

> "Every one of you will be paid in the same currency as I am, will drink the same water, will breathe the same air. That
> is not charity. That is engineering."
> Ansel Halden, Founding Day speech, Y0

## The Boom (Y0 to Y40)

### Work

Halden Works ran three shifts, round the clock, every day but Founding Day. By Y20 it had nine production lines:

- **Lines One to Three**: power cells, in the **Cell Hall** (floors 11 to 16). Coating, winding, filling, forming. The forming rooms ran hot and the electrolyte
  smelled of sweet solvent; a cell-line worker could be picked out in Saltmarket by the smell of her hair.
- **Lines Four to Six**: control chips, the small dumb chips that sat on every cell and every machine and kept them honest.
  Wafer halls, lithography, etching, the clean rooms in white suits. "Bunnies", the town called them.
- **Lines Seven and Eight**: assembly. Cells and chips into packs, packs into the products Halden sold: the drone cells,
  vehicle packs, grid banks, the **Halden Cell No. 4** that everyone in the province had in something.
- **Line Nine**, opened Y26 on floors 22 to 26: the **Clean Core**, the newest and most advanced hall, which made the
  high-density cells and the chips for them. Only grade-three staff and above. This is the hall that would become the
  Dark Floors.

Under and between the lines, the Works' water ran without stopping: the **Well Hall** on floor 0 with its forty wellheads
drawing from the Sweetwater Beds; the pump galleries on 1 and 2 that kept the dug-in floors dry against the clay; and the
**Waterhouse** on 5 to 8, where groundwater was filtered, reverse-osmosed, deionised and polished into water purer than rain,
so pure it ate ordinary pipe. (The Waterhouse still runs. Since the flood it drinks the bay instead of the aquifer, which
is harder on it. File 03.)

### Pay and life

A line operator in Y20 earned around **1,900 cr** a month. A clean-room tech earned 2,600. An engineer, 5,000 and a flat on
the sixties. Rent and utilities for a forty-square-metre flat in the forties ran **210 cr**, deducted at source, everything
included. The Commissary sold a kilo of bread for 2 cr and rain-tap water was free. A family on two line wages could
save. (A credit then was worth a good deal more than a credit now: a Y20 line wage bought roughly what four Mills labourers'
wages buy in Y86, and that is the comparison old people make, constantly, unprompted.) Many did, in the **Halden Savings Fund**, which paid three per cent and was invested in the Works.

Housing followed grade, by floor. Grade one and two (line operators, wellhead crews, refuse) in the thirties and forties.
Grade three and four (techs, foremen) in the fifties. Engineers and managers in the sixties to the eighties. Directors and the
family above 90. The tower was stratified from the first day; it just paid everyone enough that the stratification felt like
a ladder rather than a wall. People moved up. A foreman's flat on 52 was a real thing a line operator's daughter could get.

Life in the Works days, as the dry-born remember it:

- The **six o'clock lifts**: the shift change, eight thousand people moving through the cores in twenty minutes, Kessin cars
  running full, the stairs packed with people who preferred to walk down.
- The **Common** on summer evenings: the bandstand, the Works choir, the track, children running between planters,
  everybody's washing on the balconies above.
- The **Commissary queue** on payday, and the Works tokens kids collected from the canteen and swapped like cards.
- **Rain-tap water** carried home in a bucket from the landing, warm in summer because the gutter tanks sat in the sun.
- The smell: solvent in the hair, ozone on the stairs near the forming rooms, salt and diesel at the quays.
- The **Bay Run**, an annual race from the Gate Floor to the end of the Calder Wall and back.

**In play:** Old Hand clients pay above market for "Works days" goods: a Commissary bread tin, a canteen token, a choir
programme, a bandstand chair leg. Nostalgia is a demand line, steady and small, and it pays in favours as well as cr.

### The Union

The **Calder Cellworkers' Union** (the **CCU**, "the Cell") organised in Y9, after a solvent fire on Line Two killed six. Its
hall was on the Gate Floor, by the quays. It was organised in **locals**, one per hall: Local 9 was the Cell Hall, Local 4
the wafer halls, Local 12 the Waterhouse and wellheads, and so on. At its height it held eighty per cent of the Works'
hourly staff. Every member had a **clock number**, the number on the brass badge they clocked in with, and Old Hands still
introduce themselves by it.

The Union won three things that people still remember by name:

- **The Cleanroom Strike** (Y14): eleven days, over suit hours. Won the twenty-minute air break every three hours.
- **The Mask Settlement** (Y19): after the first cases of "forming cough" (lung damage from the forming rooms), the Union got
  respirators issued free and a sick fund. The Company paid into the fund and admitted nothing.
- **The Tenancy Clause** (Y22): the Union's great defeat that looked like a victory. It won a clause that a worker's family
  could keep a tied tenancy for six months after the worker's death or retirement. It did not try to end tied tenancy itself.
  Its leader, **Dorrit Vass**, said later it was the worst deal she ever signed: "We negotiated the length of the rope."

The Union broke in Y41, when there were no more hourly staff to belong to it. It never formally dissolved. Its funds were held
in the Halden Savings Fund. They are still listed there as an inactive account.

**Hook:** the CCU's membership rolls (who worked which line, which floor, which wellhead) would prove who was exposed to what.
The Old Hands say Vass hid them before the Union hall was cleared in Y42. Static has twice claimed to have them.

**In play:** an **old Union card** (CCU, with a line number and a grade stamp) is an Old Hand identity token. Carrying one in
your shop's display gives a small standing bonus with the Old Hands and Mills Co-op, and a small risk with floor wardens, who
are paid to note who displays what.

### The water underneath

At its peak, around Y30, Halden Works drew **ninety thousand cubic metres** of water a day from the Sweetwater Beds through
the Well Hall and the outlying **North Well Field** in the old salt-pans. Ultrapure water is wasteful: for every litre that
reached a wafer, nearly two litres went down the reject line. The reject water (brine, solvents, metals) went to the
**lagoon**.

The aquifer was being drawn down faster than the hills could refill it. As the water came out, the clay above it compacted,
and the ground sank. This was known in the industry; it is called subsidence; it does not reverse.

The ground under Calder began to drop: two centimetres a year in Y12, six in Y20, eleven in Y28 at the worst point near the
Well Field. The Stack, on piles driven to the gravel, did not sink. The town around it did. By Y30 the Gate Floor's quays
stood a step higher than the street, and by Y40 that step was more than a metre. The Company built ramps.

### The lagoon

The Works' reject water, grinding slurry and spent electrolyte went to a settling lagoon on the reclaimed land north of the
Stack, against the bay. The first lagoon was a pond. In Y18 the Company built **Lagoon Six**, the one everyone means: a
forty-hectare basin of grey-green slurry behind an earth embankment, sharing its seaward bank with the **Calder Wall** for
eight hundred metres. The shared stretch was called **the Shared Bank**. It saved the Company a third of the embankment cost.

Children were told never to go near the lagoon. Children went near the lagoon. Its surface had a skin that took a footprint
and held it. Gulls would not land on it.

**Hook:** Lagoon Six was never drained. Whatever was in it is now spread over old Calder, but some Flats divers say the
lagoon floor is still there under the mud, intact in places, a layer of slurry metres deep that nobody has disturbed. What
was in a Works lagoon that collected the reject of a cell-and-chip plant for thirty-nine years? Metals, yes. What else?

### The first cracks

The town noticed before the Company admitted anything. The signs, in the order people remember them:

- **Y21**: the **Leaning Row** on Ferrybank, eleven terraced houses that began to tilt toward the river. Tourists from
  inland photographed them.
- **Y24**: the tide gates on the Tannery Reach channel stopped closing properly; the channel had sunk relative to the gates.
- **Y27**: cracked foundations across Low Calder. The Company paid for repairs on some houses as a "goodwill gesture" in
  exchange for a waiver of claims.
- **Y29**: the **Pell Report**.
- **Y33**: the Saltmarket **sinkhole**, nine metres across, which took a fish stall and a delivery van. No one died. The
  Company blamed an old cannery culvert.
- **Y36**: **sunny-day floods**: high spring tides now came up through the drains into Low Calder streets on calm days. People
  put sandbags across doorways and called them "Halden steps".
- **Y38**: the Calder Wall's crest, built at 6.2 metres above datum, now stood at 4.9 in the north stretch. The council
  measured it and did not publish.

### The Pell Report (Y29)

**Dr Tamsin Pell**, a Works hydrologist on the sixties, was asked in Y28 to explain why the Well Field's yields were falling.
She answered a different question. Her report, *Abstraction and Settlement in the Calder Basin*, ran forty pages. The Company
classified it as "internal commercial" within a week of receiving it, moved Pell to the battery chemistry group, and in Y31
released a two-page summary called *Ground Conditions in Calder: An Overview*, which said the town was settling "within the
range expected for deltaic sediments".

The full report leaked after the Drowning. Excerpts:

> "Settlement at benchmark CB-14 (Ferrybank) is 1.1 m since Y0. Settlement at the Works piles is nil. The difference is
> abstraction. There is no plausible natural mechanism for a rate of this magnitude."

> "At present rates, the effective crest of the Calder Wall in its northern section will fall below the 1-in-50-year storm
> water level by approximately Y45. At this point the town is protected by luck."

> "I would add, outside my brief, that the Shared Bank at Lagoon Six is founded on the same compressible clay as the town and
> is subsiding at a similar rate, while carrying the additional load of the lagoon. It is the weakest section of the wall. If
> it fails, it will not fail alone."

> "Recommendation: reduce abstraction by sixty per cent within five years. Purchase surface water. Inject treated reject water
> to recharge the aquifer. Raise the northern Wall by two metres. Estimated cost: [redacted in leaked copy]."

Pell left the Company in Y34. She died in Calder in Y57. Her flat was on the Esplanade, at the north end, near the Wall.

**Alternative:** Pell did not die in the flood. She left Calder in Y34 for the province and lived long enough to be called to
the Inquiry, which declined to hear her because she had "a commercial interest in the matter" (the Company was suing her for
breach of confidentiality). This version is in Static's broadcasts.

**In play:** a copy of the Pell Report is the single most charged document in the game. Archivists want it. The Company wants
it destroyed. Static wants to read it on air. A **Pell excerpt** (one page, water-stained) is a contract reward for the right
client; a **full copy** is a late-game object that makes the brand protection agents and HPS treat your shop differently. Who
you give it to could be an ending condition (see file 13).

### Subsidence denied

The Company's line, Y29 to Y57, never changed in substance, only in wording:

- Y31: "Settlement within the range expected for deltaic sediments."
- Y36: "Calder, like many coastal communities, faces challenges from a changing sea."
- Y40: "Halden is proud to support Calder's resilience with a 50,000 cr contribution to the Sandbag Fund."
- Y46: "Unlicensed private wells in Calder are a significant contributor to local ground movement."
- Y52: "Halden Civil maintains the Calder Wall to the highest standard consistent with available funding."

The fourth line, from Y46, is the one that mattered. By then Calder residents were digging their own wells, for reasons that
belong to the next part of this history.

## Lights-Out (Y31 to Y41)

### The son

Ansel Halden died in Y31, in the Glasshouse, of a lung condition his doctors did not name in the obituary. His son **Marcus
Halden** took the chair at forty-four. Marcus had grown up on 103 and gone to school in the province. He had never worked a
line. He was, everyone agrees, very good at numbers.

Marcus's first act as chair was to buy the Clean Core's equipment supplier. His second was to commission a study called
**Programme Daylight**, whose purpose, stated on its cover sheet, was "to determine the minimum human presence compatible
with continuous production". The Union got hold of the cover sheet in Y34 and printed it on a banner. Marcus had the banner
framed and hung in his office, which people took as a joke until Y41.

### The pilot

In Y38 Line Nine, the Clean Core on floors 22 to 26, was switched to **lights-out** running for a ninety-day trial: no
operators, no techs on the floor, machines tending machines, a handful of engineers watching from a control room on 27. The
lights in the hall were turned off because nobody needed them. Yield went up eleven per cent. Contamination fell to
almost nothing, because the largest source of contamination in a clean room is people.

The trial was never ended. The ninety days passed and the Clean Core simply stayed dark. The Line Nine staff were
"redeployed" to the other lines, which were already overstaffed. The welders came in Y40 and sealed the Clean Core's stair
doors from the outside "for contamination control". The control room on 27 was moved somewhere else. Nobody below the
directors' floors was told where.

### The announcement

The Company announced the end of manual production in the Fifth Month of Y40, with a year's notice. The text was pinned in
every lift car:

> **HALDEN WORKS ENTERS ITS NEXT CHAPTER**
>
> For forty years, the people of Halden Works have powered the province. Today we begin a new chapter in that proud story.
>
> Over the coming twelve months, Halden Works will complete its transition to advanced continuous production. Our most
> dedicated colleagues will move on to new opportunities, supported every step of the way by our **Transition Support
> Package**, the most generous in the industry.
>
> Halden Works is not closing. Halden Works will never close. We are simply learning to do more with less, so that Calder
> can look forward with confidence.
>
> Marcus Halden, Chair

The **Transition Support Package**: nine months' wages, paid in **Halden scrip**, redeemable at the Commissary, plus "priority
consideration" for the remaining roles. The scrip was printed for the occasion: green paper notes with the Glasshouse on the
front and the words NOT LEGAL TENDER on the back in letters too small to read without effort. The Commissary renamed itself
**the Company Store** the same month. Its prices went up eight per cent the week the first scrip was paid.

Scrip is still printed. The Glasshouse is still on the front.

### The Last Shift

The last manual shift on Halden Works clocked off at 22:00 on the 30th of the Ninth Month, Y41. Some thirty-one thousand
jobs had gone in the twelve months before. Around thirteen hundred stayed: maintenance, water plant, security, the lagoon
crew, the refuse sorting rooms, the lift engineers. Within six years it was four hundred.

On the last night, the operators on Line Four peeled off their white clean-room gloves and pinned them to the hall wall with
solder wire, hundreds of them, fingers up, rows and rows, before they walked out. The **Glove Wall** was never taken down. The
hall became workshop leases in Y58 and the wall became the back of someone's lease. The gloves are grey now.

> "My mother worked Four for twenty-two years. Her last day, she came home with her glove still on, the left one. She forgot
> it was on. She wore it to dinner. Nobody said anything."
> **Bryn Calloway**, Mills Co-op, recorded by the Archivists, Y79

**Hook:** the Old Hands say the night after the Last Shift, the lights came on in the Clean Core. Everyone on the Mills
stairs saw the glow under the welded doors. It has been on, at night, ever since. In the day it is dark. Nobody has a good
explanation for why a lights-out hall would turn on its lights only after its human neighbours stopped working.

**In play:** the Glove Wall is a landmark in the Mills (a lease that contains it is cheaper per square: people do not like
working with the wall at their backs). A single **Line Four glove** is a curio. A Works hand might ask you, as a contract,
to get "her mother's glove" back from the wall, where the leaseholder wants a fee for it.

### The pension

*A City Stood On End* promised a pension "drawn from the Works' own reserves, as long as the Works stands". In the Y41
restructuring the Company split itself: the profitable lines and the Clean Core went to **Halden Works Holdings**; the
pension liabilities, the Savings Fund and the old Union accounts stayed with **Halden Works (Calder) Ltd**, which owned
nothing but a lease on the Gate Floor. Calder Ltd paid pensions in scrip from the first month, at full face value until Y43 and at thirty per cent of it
thereafter, and in Y62, citing "the exceptional costs of recovery after the storm", **suspended them pending recovery**.
Recovery is still pending. The Old Hands formed that year, in the old Works canteen on 14, to complain about it, and never
left (file 06a).

The Old Hands' standing demand, repeated every Founding Day for twenty-four years, is three words: **"As it stands."** The
Works still stands. It still makes things. It just pays nobody.

## The Landlord Years (Y41 to Y57)

### The Conversion

With the jobs gone, the tied tenancies ended. Nearly all of the Stack's six thousand flats, and the forty thousand people
crowded into them, were now held on employment that no longer existed. The Company could have evicted them. Instead, in the
First Month of Y42, every household received the same letter, which the dry-born call **the Conversion Letter**:

> Dear Resident,
>
> We are delighted to offer you the opportunity to remain in your home under a new **Halden Residential Lease**.
>
> Your tied tenancy will end on the date shown below. To continue to enjoy the comfort and security of the Stack, simply sign
> and return the enclosed lease before that date. Your new rent, reflecting current market conditions, is shown overleaf.
> Utilities will be billed separately under our new **Metered Fairness** programme, so that you pay only for what you use.
>
> If you choose not to remain, we wish you every success in your new home, and remind you that Halden's Transition Support
> scrip remains valid at the Company Store.

The rent overleaf, for the same forty-square-metre flat on the forties that had cost 210 cr a month all-in, was 340 cr, plus
utilities. Most signed. There was nowhere else to go that had work, and there was no work.

The exception was the Cell Hall. Local 9 had bargained on its way out: under the **Settlement of Y41**, every laid-off Local 9
member kept their lease at frozen rent for fifteen years, and the Local's strike fund was paid out to members as a mutual
fund. Eleven hundred families. For the next fifteen years they were the luckiest people in the tower, and everybody knew to
the day when their luck would run out: Y56. It ran out one year before the water came. (File 06a: the Co-op grew from them.)

This was the moment Halden became a landlord. It did not feel like a moment. It felt like a letter.

### Metered Fairness

The Sorrel meters had been counting since Y0. In the Third Month of Y43 they started counting money. Every flat received a
statement showing what it had drawn in water, power and air over the previous month, priced per litre, per unit and (for
the first and only time) per cubic metre of air. The air charge was withdrawn within a year after the **Breath Riots** on
44, which were four days of tenants holding their breath on the stairs in front of the Company's floor office, a protest that
was funny until a seventy-year-old woman fainted down a flight of stairs and died. The air meters were never switched off.
They are still recording. The billing line for air is still on every statement, at zero.

**Hook:** why does the Company still read the air meters? A floor warden on 47 says the readings go up the Tube once a week
in a sealed capsule, same as in Y43. Someone, somewhere, is still keeping the books on how much each flat breathes.

**In play:** the **air line at zero** is a running joke on every statement and a looming threat. An event deck card late in
the game: "Metered Fairness Review: air charges reinstated on floors below 30 at 0.02 cr per cubic metre." Your Lung-fed
squares suddenly have a running cost, and a bought **air scrubber** that takes three squares and runs off your socket starts
paying for itself.

### The Mills are born, slowly

The Plinth's fab halls stood empty: twenty floors of high-ceilinged, clean-floored, well-wired space with a lease tile under
every footstep, stripped cell lines, clean rooms with their doors propped open, conveyor tracks running nowhere. In Y45 the
Company tried to lease them as whole halls to outside firms. None came; nobody was moving industry to a town that was
visibly sinking. From Y50 it let a few corners by the month to repair men, laid-off Works techs who knew how to fix anything
with a Halden cell in it, which was everything. Otherwise the halls stayed dark for sixteen years, and the Mills were a word
the town used, not a place anyone lived.

The name came from outside. Calder people had always called the Works "the Mill", because that was what it was to them: a
mill that ground people. When people did come to live in its halls, the name came with them, plural.

They came all at once, on the Lifeline barges in Y57, and were billeted in the empty halls by the thousand. The Partition
of Y58 turned the billets into leases by the square. Then:

- **Grow co-ops**, because the halls had power, drains, and water connections rated for industrial use, and the old clean
  rooms had sealed walls and their own air. The **Mills Co-op** registered in Y60, nine Local 9 families in half a clean room
  on 15 (file 06a).
- **Insect farms**, from Y58, because fresh meat had stopped coming below 60 and the chutes were full of food waste.
  **Orrin Provisions** licensed the first press line on 11 and began selling **Vitabrick** in Y59 ("Complete protein. Every
  brick, every day.").
- **Workshops** of every kind, in blocks of squares, run by anyone who could pay the first month.
- **Dormitories**: sublet barons leasing whole halls and partitioning them into bunks by the shift.

**In play:** this is the player's world. Your shop is a block of squares in an old Works hall on 17; each of your squares stands on a tile
with its own stud; your drains are industrial drains; the light is what the hall's founding-era fittings give, plus whatever lamps you
buy. The **lease history** of your own squares is something an Archivist can look up for you (a small paid contract): who
rented them before, what they made, why they left. Some of those answers are items in your chute.

### The town dies slowly

With the Works' payroll gone, Calder lost its customers. The cannery closed in Y43, Saltmarket shrank to a weekend, the ferry
stopped in Y47. The town's population fell from three hundred and ten thousand at its peak (Y35) to around a hundred and
seventy thousand by Y56. The people who stayed were the ones who could not leave: the old, the indebted, the fishing families
with nowhere inland to go, and the Esplanade rich, who had houses they could not sell on a sinking shore and lived on income
from somewhere else.

The council, short of money, sold what it had. Halden bought:

- **Y44, the Harbour Concession**: the harbour, the quays and the ferry pier, for a nominal sum. The Company moved its
  Seawall Section, and with it the Clause 14 obligation, into a new subsidiary called **Halden Civil**, which held the
  concession too. A subsidiary can be given a budget. A subsidiary can be given a smaller one.
- **Y45, Calder Water**: the town's water utility, pipes, pumps and all. Halden Civil raised tariffs four times in three years
  "to fund essential upgrades". The upgrades were metering.
- **Y48, the Calder bus company**, which it closed.
- **Y51, the Saltmarket freehold**, which it sat on.

### The wells

Two things happened at once. Calder Water's mains, which drew from shallow town wells, turned brackish as the ground sank
and the sea crept into the shallow sands; and Halden Civil, now owning those mains, put the price up for the brackish water.
From Y46, people in Low Calder dug their own wells: hand-dug and driven-point wells into the shallow aquifer, a few metres
down, in back yards and cellars. The water was no worse than the mains, smelled of the lagoon, and was free. Fine for washing
and toilets; boiled, for tea. An entire district on the north-east side, behind the lagoon, came to be called **Wellside**.

There were, at the peak, about four thousand of these wells. Together they drew perhaps **four hundred cubic metres** a day,
from the shallow layer, which does not compact the deep clay. The Works was still drawing **sixty thousand** a day from the
Sweetwater Beds, lights-out, for the Clean Core's ultrapure water.

In Y46 the Company's statement first blamed "unlicensed private wells" for "local ground movement". Halden Civil began
issuing **well notices**, fining households 50 cr for an unlicensed well, and offering a **well licence** at 12 cr a year.
About a third of Wellside licensed. The licensing records made it very easy, later, to count how many wells there had been.

> "We dug the well because the Company put the water price up. Then the Company said the well sank the town. Then the Company
> sold us a licence for the well. Then the town sank. I am not clever, but I can count."
> **Ada Merrow**, Wellside, later of the Flats, recorded Y71

**In play:** this is the origin of every licence in the game. The pattern (price something people need, punish the
work-around, then sell a licence for the work-around) runs through the Water Licence, the chute permits and the brand
protection rules. Anyone dry-born will tell you it began with the wells. A **Wellside well licence** (paper, Y48) is a
curio, and a cheap way to start a conversation with a Tide Folk client, whose grandparents had them.

### The Harrow Memo

In the Eighth Month of Y53, a Halden Civil field engineer named **Sefton Harrow** walked the Shared Bank at low tide and wrote
a two-page memo to his director. It is the second most charged document in the game after the Pell Report, and shorter.

> To: Director, Halden Civil
> Re: Lagoon Six Shared Bank, chainage 2+100 to 2+900
>
> Walked the seaward toe today at LW. Found: longitudinal cracking along the crest, 2+300 to 2+650, up to 40 mm wide; seepage
> at the toe at 2+480, discoloured (lagoon water, not sea); slumping of the facing at 2+510. Crest level surveyed at 4.31 m
> above datum. Design crest 6.20 m.
>
> The bank is now lower than a 1-in-20 surge on a spring tide. It is retaining forty hectares of lagoon on its landward side,
> which is heavier than seawater. If it overtops it will erode from the back. If it erodes it will release the lagoon into
> Wellside and Low Calder at the same time as the sea.
>
> I recommend: emergency raising of the crest with rock armour (est. 2.1M cr), drawdown of the lagoon, and a warning plan
> for Wellside. I recommend these urgently.
>
> S. Harrow

The reply, handwritten in the margin and initialled by a director whose initials the Inquiry was told could not be
identified:

> *Noted. No budget this cycle. Monitor.*

Harrow monitored. He walked the bank every month for four years and wrote one line in the Wall Book each time: "Condition as
previous. Worse at 2+510." He was on the Shared Bank on the night of the Drowning, because it was the night he had been
waiting for. His body was not found.

**Alternative:** Harrow survived. He was on the bank, saw it go, and ran. He testified to the Inquiry under a confidentiality
order and lived afterwards on a Company pension in the Terraces, on condition he never spoke of it. He is still alive, very
old, on 71. This version is a **Hook** in its own right: a Terraces client who sends you strange, specific contracts (rock
armour samples, a tide table from Y57, a pair of men's boots, size 44) may be him.

**In play:** the Harrow Memo is shorter than the Pell Report and therefore more dangerous: it fits on one square. A **Harrow
Memo (carbon copy)** found in a chute bag from the Terraces is an item that unlocks a small contract chain. Delivering it to
the Archivists, Static or the Rain Church each opens something different. Holding it on your shelves is a risk while brand
protection or HPS inspectors are about.

### The forecast industry

The **Calder Bay Meteorological Station**, on stilts in the outer bay, was a public service until Y50, when the province sold
it to a private operator who renamed it **the Sounding** and began selling forecasts by subscription. Calder council
subscribed until Y52, then cancelled to save money. Halden subscribed throughout, at the premium tier, which gave a
seventy-two-hour storm warning. The public got the free tier: twelve hours, delivered by radio.

This matters for what happened next.

## The Drowning (Y57)

The Company's name for it is **the Y57 Weather Event**, or on plaques, **the Calder Storm**. The Inquiry called it "the
events of the 19th and 20th of the Third Month". Everyone else calls it **the Drowning**, and Tide Folk call it **Wall
Night**. It happened on the equinox spring tide, the highest tide of the year, in a storm the Sounding had named **Marla**.

What follows is pieced together from the Inquiry record, the Wall Book (whose last pages survived in a sealed tin), the
Gate Floor duty log, the Sounding's bulletins, Static's interviews, and the Archivists' memoir collection. Where the sources
disagree, the disagreement is given.

### Three days out

**17th, 06:00.** The Sounding's premium bulletin reaches the Glasshouse by Tube:

> SOUNDING PREMIUM / STORM ADVISORY / SYSTEM MARLA
> Deep depression tracking NE, deepening. Peak winds 110–130 km/h on the 19th. Surge 1.4–1.9 m at Calder Bay, coinciding
> with equinox HW 21:40 on the 19th. Combined water level 5.1–5.6 m above datum. Overtopping of defences with crest below
> 5.5 m: HIGHLY LIKELY. Subscribers with coastal assets are advised to act on this guidance at once.

The Calder Wall's northern crest, by Harrow's last survey, stood at 4.31 metres.

**17th, 14:00.** A meeting in the Glasshouse boardroom. Present: the chair, **Imogen Halden-Ward** (Ansel's granddaughter,
chair since Marcus's stroke in Y52), four directors, the head of Halden Civil, the head of Works security. No minutes were
kept, according to the Company. The Inquiry later received a two-page "summary of actions", headed **Operation Keel**:

1. Move critical inventory and records from the Gate Floor, floors 4 to 9 and the Well Hall to floors 30 and above.
2. Seal the Waterhouse bulkheads; double the Waterhouse crew; supply them for seventy-two hours.
3. Confirm the Clean Core's independent power and its "protection status".
4. Lagoon Six: "no drawdown at this time (permitting, timing, public reaction)".
5. Public communication: "defer to the Sounding's public service bulletin".
6. Stakeholder engagement: "host key Calder stakeholders at the Founding Reception (brought forward)".

Item 6 is the one people remember. The Company's annual Founding Reception was normally held on Founding Day, two weeks
later. It was brought forward to the evening of the 19th, in the Glasshouse, and invitations went out by courier that
afternoon to **four hundred and twelve** people: the Esplanade families, the council's senior members, the cannery family,
the clinic's consultants, the Halden Civil directors and their spouses. Transport by Company launch from the Esplanade
steps "in view of the forecast weather". Black tie.

> "Mama said, it's very sudden. Papa said, it's Halden, darling, it's always sudden. We had our coats on in an hour. I was
> nine. I wore the green dress. I remember the launch going out past the Wall and the men on the Wall looking at us."
> **Celestine Aubrac-Mott**, floor 96, interview with a Terraces magazine, Y80 (the interview was about her gardens)

**Hook:** who decided the guest list? Four hundred and twelve invitations means four hundred and twelve decisions. The
Archivists have eleven of the original cards. The Rain Church has a rumour that a second, shorter list existed: people
who were invited and then uninvited on the afternoon of the 18th, when someone realised the Glasshouse would hold only so
many. Nobody has seen it.

### The day before

**18th.** Company trucks run all day from the Gate Floor quays to the Freights. Works security moves the Union's old
archive boxes from the Gate Floor hall to "secure storage" (they are never seen again). The Waterhouse crew is doubled to
sixty and sealed in. The Well Hall's pumps are left running.

Halden Civil sends its last eleven wall gangers to close the tide gates. The Tannery Reach gates, which have not closed
properly since Y24, are chained half-shut. Sergeant Oyelaran writes in the Wall Book: *"Gates TR3 and TR4 will not seat.
Requested sandbags x 4,000. Told none available."*

No warning goes to the town. The council's subscription to the Sounding lapsed in Y52. The town's sirens were sold for
scrap in Y54.

### The 19th, hour by hour

**09:40.** The Sounding's free public bulletin goes out on the provincial radio band: *"Strong winds and higher than usual
tides are expected this evening. Residents of low-lying areas should take sensible precautions."* Twelve hours' notice, as
the subscription tiers promise. The words "surge" and "overtopping" are not in the public bulletin. The Sounding's later
position: the public bulletin is "an indicative product".

**11:00.** A council van with a loudspeaker drives Low Calder repeating the bulletin. In Wellside, people move furniture
upstairs. That is the evacuation.

**15:00.** Wind at ninety. The ferry pier, closed since Y47, loses its end section. Rain sheeting. The Common on 30 is
closed; the bandstand roof goes over the edge at 15:40.

**17:30.** The first launches arrive at the Gate Floor's east quay. Guests are taken straight to the Express cars on the
east face and up to the Glasshouse. A string quartet from the province, booked by telegram the day before, plays in the
reception hall on 103. From the Glasshouse windows, guests can see the bay. Several later said it was "magnificent".

**18:50.** Lights flicker across Low Calder as the town's substation takes spray. The Tannery Reach channel is running
backwards, uphill, into the town.

**19:30.** Water comes up through the drains in Low Calder, as it has on spring tides for twenty years. The "Halden steps"
(sandbag thresholds) hold it at the doors for a while.

**20:15.** Sefton Harrow, on the Shared Bank with a radio, calls Halden Civil's control room. The Inquiry heard the tape:

> HARROW: Control, Harrow, Shared Bank, two-plus-five-hundred. I'm overtopping here. Green water over the crest, every
> wave. The back face is scouring. I can see the lagoon face moving. I need the lagoon outfall opened to sea, now, to bring
> the level down behind the bank, and I need Wellside out. Over.
> CONTROL: Harrow, Control. Outfall needs a director's sign-off. Stand by.
> HARROW: There is no stand by. Over.
> CONTROL: Stand by, Harrow.

Control tried the Glasshouse. The Glasshouse line was answered by a reception steward who took a message.

**20:50.** Overtopping along the whole northern two kilometres of the Wall. Water in the Strand to the first floor windows.
The wall gangs' houses are the first to flood. Wellside streets knee-deep and rising.

**21:12.** The Shared Bank breaches at chainage 2+510, the exact point in Harrow's Y53 memo. It does not burst; it slumps,
the back face giving way under the lagoon's weight as the sea scours it, and the lagoon goes out through the gap into the
town, and the sea comes in after it.

This is the **grey wave**. Survivors are consistent about it. It was not fast. It was thick: forty hectares of thirty-nine
years' slurry, grey-green, the consistency of wet cement at its front and thinning behind. It came down Wellside's streets
at walking pace, a metre and a half high, pushing cars, and it did not behave like water. You could not swim in it. It took
hold of legs. It filled ground floors and did not drain. Behind it the sea came in on the surge and stirred it into
everything.

> "My brother tried to swim to the wall of the chapel and he got halfway and stopped. Not sank. Stopped. Like he was stood
> in it. Then the sea came behind and lifted it all and lifted him with it and I saw him go past the chapel window, and he
> was looking at me, and he was standing up."
> **Tobiah Fenwright**, Wellside, later a diver on the Flats, recorded Y68

**21:40.** High water. The surge peaks a little after. The Wall is breached in three more places, at the Strand and at
the Tannery Reach gates. Low Calder is under two to three metres of water and slurry. The Saltmarket roofs are islands.
The town's power goes out entirely at 21:44. The Stack, on its own power, is lit from top to bottom: the only lit thing for
kilometres.

**21:40 to 22:30.** People run for the Stack. It is tall, it is lit, it stands on piles, and it has always been the place
where Calder went to be safe. Thousands wade, swim, row and are carried toward it, along the old Works roads, which are
higher than the streets because the Company raised them for its trucks. They come in through the Gate Floor's public doors
and up the quay ramps.

The Gate Floor duty officer is **Brede Lomax**, Works security, eleven years' service. His log:

> 21:52 Public doors open. Admitting.
> 22:04 Est. 2,000 on Gate Floor. Directing to N and S stairs and Old Two [Freight Two]. Freight One held at 30 by order.
> 22:11 Water at the quay sills. Est. 4,000.
> 22:18 Grey water on the ramps. Admitting.
> 22:24 Phone, Glasshouse. Instructed to secure the stair doors "to protect the structure and the Waterhouse".
> 22:26 Queried. Instructed again.
> 22:31 Stair doors N and S closed. Old Two recalled to 30 by Control.
> 22:31 Est. 2,500 on Gate Floor.
> 22:38 S door held open by hand, me and Ostrowski. Admitting up S stair.
> 22:45 S door closed, water.
> 22:46 —

The log has no entry after 22:46.

### The Stair Doors

The doors between the Gate Floor and the stairs are founding-era **flood doors**, Marrow's design: steel leaves on a
hydraulic closer, built to seal the stair cores from a flooded Gate Floor so that water could not come up into the
Plinth's electrical halls. They are the reason the Stack did not lose power below 30 entirely, and the reason everyone
above 4 survived the night dry. They are also the reason somewhere between six hundred and two thousand people died on the
Gate Floor, in the dark, in two to four metres of slurry, with the stairs on the other side of a steel door.

What happened at 22:31 is the most argued-over minute in the history of the Stack.

- **The Company's version, accepted by the Inquiry:** the flood doors closed automatically when their sensors detected
  water at the sills, as designed, "a founding-era safety system performing exactly as its engineers intended". The phone
  call at 22:24 was "a routine welfare check". Officer Lomax's log entry was written "under extreme stress".
- **Lomax's version**, from the log and from Ostrowski, the security guard who survived with him: the doors were ordered
  shut by phone from the Glasshouse, twice, and closed by hand from the stair side. The sensors had been disconnected in
  Y49 because they kept closing the doors on spring tides.
- **The Old Hands' version:** both are true. The sensors were disconnected, so somebody had to close them; and if nobody
  had, the water would have come up the stair cores into the Switch Hall on 4, the tower would have gone dark from top to
  bottom, the Kessin cars would have stopped between floors with people in them, the Lung would have stopped, and the
  Waterhouse would have flooded and never run again. Shutting the doors killed the Gate Floor and saved the tower. Nobody
  who knows the building thinks the order was wrong. They think it should not have been necessary, and they think the person
  who gave it should have had to give it standing on the Gate Floor.

The south door, held open by hand for seven minutes after the others closed, let somewhere around **eight hundred** people
up the South Stair before the water forced it shut. This is **the Seven Minutes**. Lomax and Ostrowski held it with a pry
bar and their backs. Lomax was on the Gate Floor side when it closed.

**Alternative:** Lomax was on the stair side when it closed, and walked up, and lived. He refused to testify, was dismissed
from Works security for "abandoning his post", and died in a Mills dormitory in Y70. His log was submitted to the Inquiry
by his daughter, against the Company's objection.

**Alternative:** there was no phone call. Lomax invented it afterwards, out of guilt, because he closed the doors himself
on his own judgement when he saw the slurry on the ramps. This is the version the Company's Y76 "Heritage Week" exhibition
very carefully did not quite say.

**Hook:** the Gate Floor is underwater now (floors 0 to 3). Divers who have been there say the north stair door is closed
and the south door is closed, and on the stair side of the south door, at shoulder height, there are scratches in the paint.
On the Gate Floor side, there is a pry bar still wedged in the hinge gap. Someone tried to open it again, from the stair
side, after it shut. Who?

**In play:** the **Seven Minutes** are a phrase, a toast and a unit of mercy in the Mills: "give me seven minutes" means
"bend a rule for me". A contract from an Old Hand or a Tide Folk elder might ask for exactly that: a deadline you could
meet by cutting a corner (a free shaft, an unlicensed water fill), framed as a favour owed since Y57.

### Midnight

**22:50.** The Well Hall and pump galleries (floors 0 to 2) are full. They will never be pumped dry again. The wellheads
that drew the Sweetwater Beds for fifty-seven years are under twenty metres of water and slurry.

**23:20.** Waves break through the loading doors of the raised quay deck on floor 4. Water runs into the Switch Hall.

**23:48.** The Switch Hall shorts. Power goes out on every floor from 4 to 29. The Works' empty halls, the repair men's
corners, the stairwells full of people from the Gate Floor, go black. The lights above 30 stay on, fed from the tower's own cell banks through a separate main.
The Glasshouse never flickers. The Clean Core, on its own supply, never flickers either. People in the dark stairwells of
the Plinth could see a line of light under the welded doors on 22 to 26, steady all night. This is the night most people
in the tower first noticed the Dark Floors.

The dry-born call it **Ten to Midnight**, and in the Mills "it's ten to midnight" means a power cut.

**00:30.** Water across floors 4 and 5 to waist height and rising with the waves; the Waterhouse's sealed bulkheads on 5 to
8 hold, with its crew inside, listening to the sea against the walls. The refuse sorting rooms on 8 (today's Bale Hall) take spray but stay
mostly dry.

**01:15.** Peak water. In old Calder, perhaps one house in five has its upper floor above water. Families on roofs. Fires
on the Knuckle, where the gas mains have burst.

**02:00 onward.** The tide turns. The sea does not leave. The breaches in the Wall let it in and out with every tide from
now on. The slurry it brought stays.

### The Glasshouse that night

The Founding Reception continued. The Inquiry was told that the quartet stopped at 22:00 "out of respect". The chair spoke
at 22:15, from notes the Archivists claim to have:

> "Friends. Outside, Calder is suffering. Inside, we are together, and we are safe, and that is not luck. That is planning.
> Halden has always planned. Tomorrow we will begin to help, and we will not stop helping until Calder is whole."

At 22:24, the Gate Floor log records the phone call from the Glasshouse.

### Dawn

**20th, 06:20.** Grey light on a grey sea. Where the town was, rooftops, chimneys, the cannery's water tower, the chapel
spire in Wellside, the Knuckle's houses on their ridge with smoke over them. The surface is skinned with slurry: it holds
the shape of things that float. Gulls circle and will not land.

Survivors on roofs across Low Calder can see the Stack, lit, at the edge of the flood.

> "I thought, they'll come. I thought it all night. The Stack had boats; we'd seen the launches. In the morning I could see
> the lights still on up the top and I thought, they're up, so they'll come. They did come. On the fourth day."
> **Ada Merrow**, Wellside, recorded Y71

### The Lifeline

Halden Civil's barges went out on the **22nd**. The Company called it **Operation Lifeline** and it is the act of the
Drowning the Company is proudest of. Over nine days the barges took around **thirty-one thousand** people off roofs and out
of upper windows.

Each barge carried a clerk with a folding table. Each person brought aboard was registered by name, given water (Halden
Pure, from the Waterhouse, the first time it was ever served to the public) and offered a choice:

1. Passage to the **provincial reception camp** at Ferrybridge, inland, a day's barge away, with "no guarantee of
   onward accommodation".
2. Immediate shelter in the Stack under a **Residential Lease (Emergency)**: a bed space, rent-free for ninety days,
   thereafter at "the prevailing rate", the lease signed on the barge.

About twenty-two thousand signed. The phrase for it, in the Mills, is **signed on the water**. Their leases were the first
in the Stack to be priced per square: the barge clerks had a form with a box for "squares allocated", usually a few (a bed
and a strip of floor beside it), because the halls they were going to were the empty Works halls on 10 to 29, already
tiled, already counted, tile by tile, by sensors nobody had read in sixteen years.

The ninety days ended in the Sixth Month of Y57. Rent fell due. Most of the signed-on-the-water had nothing; the Company
offered a **Lifeline Loan** to cover arrears, at interest. Some families are still paying it, three generations on, because
the Charter lets a lease's debt pass with the lease.

> "My grandmother signed with a pencil on a barge with her hands shaking. The clerk wrote her name for her because hers was
> too long for the box. That's the name I've got. It's not hers. It's the clerk's version."
> **Mira Tallow-Nkem**, floor 15, Y85 (the Ledger cannot hold names over 24 characters: file 04)

**Alternative:** the Lifeline barges were slow because they were busy. The four days before the barges went out were spent
taking the Company's own inventory, the Esplanade families' possessions, and the Works' salvageable plant off the Gate
Floor quays and the Knuckle warehouses. The barge logs for the 20th and 21st are missing from the Inquiry record. This
version is Static's, and the Rain Church's, and most of the Flats'.

**In play:** **signed-on-the-water** is a background a player character could have (file 13): you were born into a
Lifeline lease and inherited its debt. The **Lifeline Loan** can be a starting debt line in the ledger. A water-stained
**Lifeline lease (barge copy)** is a Flats curio, and a Company document an Archivist will pay for, because every one of them
shows the squares allocated and the rate typed in after the person had signed.

### Who lived, who died

| Group | Approximate number | What happened to them |
|-------|--------------------|-----------------------|
| Stack residents above floor 4 | about 33,000 | Dry. Lost power below 30 for eleven days. |
| Glasshouse reception guests | 412, plus staff | Dry, fed, and taken home by launch to the province on the 21st, or given flats on the Terraces. |
| Waterhouse crew | 60 | Sealed in for four days. All lived. Each received a Company medal and a bonus. |
| People who reached the Gate Floor | around 6,000 | About 3,200 up the stairs before 22:31; about 800 in the Seven Minutes; the rest drowned or survived on the quay gantries. |
| Taken off roofs by the Lifeline | around 31,000 | 22,000 signed on the water; 9,000 went to Ferrybridge. |
| Left Calder by their own boats | unknown, many thousands | Inland. Some came back. |
| Stayed in the ruins | a few thousand | The first people of the Flats (file 07). |
| The wall gangs | 11 on the Wall; ~400 in the Strand | Sergeant Oyelaran and four gangers lived. The Strand mostly did not. |

The dead:

- **Official figure**, Inquiry final report: **3,912**.
- **The Tally**, a count kept by the Archivists and the Tide Folk together since Y58, house by house, name by name, from
  the Lifeline registers, the well licences (which listed every adult in a Wellside household), the Union rolls and the
  memories of the living: **21,640** at the last revision (Y84), and still rising slowly as names are added.

The gap between the two numbers is mostly people the Inquiry classed as "unaccounted for, presumed relocated". The well
licence records are why the Tally can be so specific about Wellside, and why Wellside's dead are the best-named dead in
Calder. The Company sold them a licence for their well and kept a copy, and the copy is their headstone.

**In play:** the Tally is a physical thing: a set of ledgers in the Archivists' keeping, added to by hand. "A name for the
Tally" is a contract type with no cr reward: someone asks you to find proof that a person died in Y57 (a Lifeline register
page, a well licence, a Union card from the chute). Delivering it raises standing with the Archivists and the Tide Folk,
and may unlock a Flats supplier who will not otherwise sell to the tower.

## The story the Company told

### The press release

Issued from the Glasshouse on the morning of the **20th**, read on the provincial radio band at noon, and pinned in every
lift car in the Stack by evening:

> **HALDEN STANDS WITH CALDER**
>
> Last night an unprecedented weather event struck Calder Bay. Our thoughts are with every family affected.
>
> Thanks to the foresight of its founders, the Stack stood firm. Its founding-era flood protection performed exactly as
> designed, keeping thousands of residents safe and dry. Halden Works' water plant is operating normally and will begin
> supplying safe drinking water to the people of Calder today, free of charge.
>
> Halden is launching **Operation Lifeline**, the largest civilian rescue in the province's history. No one who needs
> shelter will be turned away.
>
> The causes of this tragedy will take time to understand. Calder, like many coastal communities, has faced a changing
> climate and a changing sea, and we must all learn from what happened. Halden will cooperate fully with any inquiry.
>
> Imogen Halden-Ward, Chair

The "free of charge" water was free until the end of the Fourth Month.

### The Inquiry (Y57 to Y59)

The province appointed a **Commission of Inquiry into the Events at Calder Bay** in the Fifth Month of Y57, chaired by
**Justice Corin Vance**, a retired provincial judge, with two commissioners: a hydraulic engineer from the provincial works
department, **Agathe Rusk**, and an accountant, **Leopold Sarne**. The Inquiry sat for fourteen months, in a hired hall on
floor 63 next door to the two rooms of the Calder Recovery Authority (file 05). Halden paid for the hall, the stenographers
and the Commission's accommodation in the Terraces, "as a gesture of cooperation".

Before it sat, the Company had already held its own. The **Wells Inquiry**, commissioned by Halden and reporting in the
Second Month of Y58, found that "the primary contributory factor" in the ground's subsidence was illegal wells, and named
forty residents whose well permits had been revoked in the Y40s. The **Wells Forty** who survived were fined under the
Recovery Authority's emergency powers, and their fines became debts their grandchildren still carry (file 05). The Wells
Inquiry's report was submitted to Vance as evidence. It was the first document in the Vance bundle.

The Vance Commission heard two hundred and six witnesses. It did not hear Tamsin Pell, Sefton Harrow, or anyone from the Glasshouse
reception below the level of director. It received the Pell Report's two-page Y31 summary, not the report. It received the
Harrow Memo, and found the margin note "not attributable".

**The Vance Report** (Y59), *Findings and Conclusions*, the paragraphs the Company quotes:

> **4.2** The Commission finds that the storm of the 19th and 20th of the Third Month, Y57, was an event of exceptional
> severity, coinciding with a tide of exceptional height, and that its consequences could not reasonably have been
> foreseen in their full extent by any party.

> **4.7** The Commission finds that the ground of Calder has settled over several decades. The causes of settlement in deltaic
> environments are multiple and complex. The Commission notes in particular the proliferation, from approximately Y46, of
> unlicensed private wells in the northern districts, and regards this as a significant and avoidable contributing factor.

> **4.11** The Commission finds that the Calder Wall was maintained by Halden Civil within the terms of Clause 14 of the
> Calder Development Accord, the standard of "good and sufficient repair" being a matter for the engineering judgement of
> the responsible party.

> **4.15** The Commission finds that the release of material from Lagoon Six was a consequence of the failure of the Wall
> and not a cause of it, and that the material released, having settled, poses no significant continuing hazard.

> **4.19** The Commission finds that the flood doors on the Gate Floor closed in accordance with their design. The
> Commission makes no finding regarding the telephone communication recorded at 22:24.

> **6.1** The Commission commends the Halden Company for Operation Lifeline, which saved many thousands of lives.

> **7.3** Recommendation: the province should consider whether the reoccupation of the low-lying districts of Calder is
> practicable. (The province considered it, and in Y59 declared Calder an **Unrecoverable Zone**.)

**The Rusk Dissent.** Commissioner Rusk refused to sign chapter 4 and attached a four-page **Note of Dissent**, which the
Company did not quote and which the provincial printer, for reasons of cost, did not bind into the public edition. The
Archivists have three typescript copies. The core of it:

> "I cannot join my colleagues in 4.7. The Commission has before it the Works' own abstraction figures and the well
> licence register. The first is sixty thousand cubic metres a day from the deep beds. The second, at its fullest, is four
> hundred from the shallow. To attribute the settlement of Calder to the second is not a finding. It is an arithmetic error
> that has been chosen."

> "I cannot join my colleagues in 4.11. 'Good and sufficient' is not a matter of judgement when the Wall Book records
> three hundred and seventy-one repairs requested in the final year and three carried out."

> "I cannot join my colleagues in 4.15. I have walked the Shared Bank. It failed from the back, under the lagoon. The
> lagoon did not follow the sea into Calder. It led."

> "I make no finding about the telephone. Nobody can, because the Glasshouse's telephone records were not produced. I note
> only that they were requested."

Rusk returned to the province in Y59. Her name is a word in the Mills: a **rusk** is a person who tells the truth to no
effect. ("Don't be a rusk about it.")

**Alternative:** Rusk did not write the dissent. The three typescripts in the Archivists' keeping were typed on the same
machine, which an Archivist identified in Y81 as a Works-issue typewriter from the Union hall. The Old Hands wrote it,
after the fact, in her voice. It is still all true; it is just not hers. (The Archivists do not like this version. It is
the sort of thing the Company would say, and it came from someone who works for the Company.)

### What everyone knows

Nobody in the tower below the Terraces believes the Vance Report, and most people in the Terraces do not either; they just
do not say so. What "everyone knows" is not a theory with sides. It is a list, recited, in roughly this order:

1. The Works pumped the ground out from under the town for fifty years, and the Company knew from Y29.
2. The Company owned the Wall and stopped mending it.
3. The Company's lagoon was in the Wall, and it went first, and that is why the water is poison.
4. The Company knew three days early, and moved its things and its friends, and told nobody.
5. The doors were shut from upstairs.
6. Then the Company blamed the wells, which it had made people dig, and the weather, which it had paid to know about.
7. Then it sold the survivors a bed, and then it sold them the water.

The Company does not dispute this list. It does not need to. Knowing it changes nothing about the rent.

**In play:** the list is folk knowledge and shows up everywhere: as graffiti numbers ("5" chalked on a stair door means "they
shut it from upstairs"; "7" on a tap), in Static's sign-off, in the way a Mills client says "the usual seven" when a
Company inspector leaves. A **chalked numeral** on your field's backdrop can mark the floor's mood: more numerals, more
sympathy for bootleg work and less for the Company, which shifts which contracts appear on the board.

### The memorials

The Company erected a memorial in Y58, a polished slab in the Middle's lift lobby on 30, beside the Turnstile:

> IN MEMORY OF THE PEOPLE OF CALDER
> WHO LOST THEIR LIVES IN THE STORM OF Y57
> AND IN GRATITUDE TO ALL WHO SERVED IN OPERATION LIFELINE
> *Halden Stands With Calder*

It names nobody. There are more words thanking the Lifeline than mourning the dead. People in the Mills call it **the
Receipt**.

The people's memorial is the **Tally**, and the **South Door** itself: on Wall Night, the 19th of the Third Month, people go
down the South Stair to the lowest dry landing, where the stair runs into black water above the Gate Floor, and leave
candles on the steps and a cup of rain if they have one. HPS do not stop it. The Lift Guild runs Old Two to the Sump for
free that night, the only free ride of the year, and nobody is quite sure whether it is a kindness or a tradition the Guild
is afraid to break.

The Company holds **Resilience Day** on the 22nd, the day the Lifeline barges went out: a Company Store sale, Lifeline
veterans' medals, a speech relayed to every lift lobby, and a free cup of Halden Pure for every tenant who presents a tally.

**In play:** two calendar events three days apart. **Wall Night** (19.III): demand spikes for candles, rain water (from any
source, no questions), flowers (real grade only: the rarest, highest-paying Mills contract of the year is a single real
flower for a Gate Floor family), and drone deliveries down to the Sump are toll-free. **Resilience Day** (22.III): the
Company Store discounts drones and machines for a day, scrip contracts flood the board, and anything Lifeline-branded sells
up to the Terraces as heritage. Which day you trade on, and what you sell, is a small statement your floor notices.

## Aftermath (Y57 to Y60)

### Eleven dark days

Power below 30 was out for **eleven days**. The Mills and the Plinth halls, where most of the signed-on-the-water had just
been put, were dark, cold, and wet at the bottom. The Lung kept running on the tower main, but the return trunks to the
Plinth were full of water vapour and the halls ran with condensation. The first cases of **Plinth lung** (the cough
everyone below 30 has now) date from those eleven days, according to the Old Hands; according to Vey, Plinth lung does not
exist as a distinct condition.

People cooked on Works solvent drums and Halden cells shorted into wire coils. The first improvised cell heaters date from
this time, and so does the first trade in **cells pulled from the chutes**, because everybody above 30 threw away their
spare cells to make room for the refugees the Company was billeting on them.

### The Emergency Stewardship Order

On the ninth day after the flood, the Calder Recovery Authority, which was what was left of the town council, signed the
Emergency Stewardship Order and delegated the government of the Stack to the Company "for the duration of the emergency".
The emergency has not ended. (File 05 has the Order and its consequences in full.)

### The Partition (Y58)

In the First Month of Y58 the Company published the **Occupancy Rationalisation**, which everyone calls **the Partition**.
The tower was re-surveyed, floor by floor, by reading the lease tiles' sensors, and every flat, hall and corridor was
re-let by the tile. Flats were split: a two-room flat on 44 became two one-room leases and a corridor sublet. Halls were cut
into blocks. Corridors became lease-able "circulation squares" with a right of way. A lease, which had been a home, became a
list of tile numbers.

The rate per square was set by floor: the higher the floor, the higher the rate, rising in steps at 30 and at the Terraces
line. The steps were designed by the same Company planners who had run the Works' clean-room allocation models; they used
the old Sorrel meter data to estimate what each floor could bear, and set the rate there.

**In play:** the Partition is why your shop is measured in squares, why your lease is a list of tile numbers, and why a
**dead tile** (one whose sensor stopped reporting) is a quiet fortune (file 02). The Partition's maps, printed on blue
survey paper (**Partition blues**), show every lease line in the tower as it was drawn in Y58. They are useful to sublet
barons and to anyone looking for a forgotten tile.

### The Ascension Sales and the Settling

Before the flood, the Crown was the Halden family and its directors, the sixties to the eighties were Works engineers and
managers, and the thirties to fifties were line families. After the Partition, the Company did two things at once.

The **Ascension Sales** (Y58 to Y60) offered long leases on the upper floors, at prices only capital could pay, to the
people who had lost houses on the Esplanade and the Knuckle and been paid out by their insurers, to provincial investors,
and to the Company's own senior staff. The Crown, which had been ten floors of directors' flats and the Glasshouse, was
extended down to 90. The Terraces were set at 70 to 89.

The **Settling** moved everyone else down. Engineers' families on the seventies and sixties who could not pay the new rates
were offered "suitable alternative accommodation" in the Middle; line families in the Middle who could not pay theirs were
offered the Mills. The signed-on-the-water were already in the Mills. The ones who could not pay the Mills went to the
Sump. Nobody was evicted, exactly. Everyone was offered something, one band lower.

> "We had a flat on 67 with a window facing the hills. Dad was a process engineer for twenty-six years. In Y58 we got a
> letter that said we were being 'resettled to a lease better suited to our circumstances'. Our circumstances were that he
> had been an engineer and now he wasn't anything. We went to 41. Then we went to 33. My children were born on 19. My
> grandchildren will be born where they can."
> **Ingrid Ostergaard**, floor 19, Y82

So the bands were not designed. They were the shape the water and the price left. The rich did not move up. They moved in,
at the top, and everyone underneath them moved down a band to make room.

### The Water Licence

Calder's water supply was gone: the town's mains salted and slurried, the Sweetwater wellheads drowned. The Waterhouse was
switched to the bay, through the old Intake on floor 3, and its output turned toward the tower's taps. It was the only safe
water for kilometres, and it was the Company's.

The **Public Wellbeing (Water) Ordinance**, Y58, made treating water without a Halden licence an offence "in response to the
public health emergency arising from the storm and from the contamination of Calder's groundwater by unregulated private
wells". The founding-era bypass valves that could have fed the lower header tanks first were welded shut the same year. The
first Company notice to use the word **stinkers** for unlicensed water sellers is dated the Ninth Month of Y58; the word was
already in use in the Mills for people who ran stills and ferments in the halls, because of the smell. (File 03 has the
Ordinance and the Waterhouse in full.)

### The title to the water

**Unrecoverable Zone** status, in Y59, did one more thing. Under the province's own land code, land that could not be
recovered from the sea vested in "the holder of the harbour concession, for the purpose of navigation and the removal of
hazards". The holder of the harbour concession was Halden Civil.

So the Company owns the seabed of old Calder: its drowned streets, its houses, its chapel, its dead, and the mud of the
Flats. Everything a Flats diver brings up was, in law, Halden's property the moment it went under the water. The Company has
never enforced this against the divers, because it would have to pay to do it. It enforces it against the buyers when it
needs to, which is why salvage passes up the tower through middlemen and arrives without a history.

**Alternative:** the vesting clause was challenged by Esplanade families who wanted their drowned houses back, and the
province settled with them privately. Their lots are the only privately owned land in old Calder, and several of those
families have since hired divers to recover specific things from specific houses: an **In play** source of oddly precise
contracts from the Crown ("the second drawer of the desk in the north room of 14 Esplanade").

**In play:** salvage carries a **provenance** problem. Any item tagged *drowned* is Company property in law; selling it
openly up the tower invites a brand protection or HPS visit; selling it through a fixer costs a cut. A **salvage receipt**
(a forged one, from a Sump fixer) makes a drowned item sellable to the Middle.

### The Flats

The people who stayed in the ruins (the wall gangs from the Strand, Wellside families who would not sign, fishing families
who still had boats) lived on roofs, then on rafts tied to roofs, then, when they learned that the falling tide uncovered
the old streets, on the mud itself. Within a year they were diving the houses. Within two they were selling what they found
to the Stack, through the quay at floor 4, where the Lift Guild's predecessors charged them to use the hoist. (File 07 has the
Flats; file 06a has the Tide Folk.)

### The other towers in Y57

Calder Bay had other tall buildings. What happened to them in the flood set what they are now (file 07 has them today):

- **Vantage**, on the south shoal, built Y49 to Y54 by the **Corvane Group**, a rival battery company, on piles driven
  twice as deep as the Stack's, with its own desalination plant and its ground floor raised four metres on a plinth.
  It took the storm with wet ankles. People in the Stack assume Corvane read the Pell Report; nobody can prove it.
- **St. Ober's**, the town's hospital, a thirty-floor block on the Knuckle's southern end, lost its lower wards and its
  generators; its charity trustees kept it open and began charging. It has been extended upward ever since.
- **The Calder Civic Tower**, the council's offices and flats, built in Y30 on cheaper piles over Low Calder, tilted
  six degrees in the storm and lost its northern third in Y58. It is **the Hulk** now.
- **The Grain Terminal** silos at the port, Y−20, survived as concrete cylinders in the bay and were later fitted out as
  **Greenhold** by a seed company.
- **Bastion**, the provincial prison on the bay island, was cut off for nineteen days. Nobody came to evacuate it.
- **The Sounding**, on its stilts, recorded the highest water level ever measured in the bay, and sold the figure.

## The chronicle, Y57 to Y86

What came after the water, as a chain of named events. Each one left something behind: a rule, a price, a word, a broken
thing, a grudge. Most of them are why some mechanic in the game works the way it does. Where another file holds the full
story, it is pointed to; this is the order things happened in and what each one did.

### Y57: the Drowning, the Order, the cancelled Founding Day

The 19th and 20th of the Third Month: the Drowning. The ninth day after: the **Emergency Stewardship Order**. The 1st of the
Fourth Month: **Founding Day is cancelled** for the only time in the tower's history, "out of respect". The Company Store
held its Founding Day sale anyway, on the 2nd, and called it a **Recovery Sale**. The Sixth Month: the Lifeline leases' ninety
rent-free days end and the **Lifeline Loans** begin.

### Y58: the Partition, the Wells Inquiry, the Water Ordinance

The year the tower became what it is.

- **The Partition** (First Month): every lease re-cut by the tile. The square is born as a unit of rent.
- **The Wells Inquiry** (Second Month): the wells are blamed. The Wells Forty are fined.
- **The Ascension Sales** begin: the Crown extended down to 90, the Terraces set at 70 to 89, long leases sold to
  capital. **The Settling** moves everyone else down a band.
- **The Public Wellbeing (Water) Ordinance**: the Water Licence. The bypass valves are welded. The word "stinkers" goes
  into a Company notice for the first time.
- Works security, now with nothing left to guard but the Waterhouse, the Glasshouse and the Clean Core, is renamed **Halden
  Protective Services** and offered to tenants "for those who wish additional peace of mind". The subscription tiers came
  later (file 05).

**In play:** a Y58 lease (the first Partition paper) is the oldest kind of lease document there is. A tenant who holds one
pays a **grandfathered rate** that the Company has spent twenty-eight years trying to end. Such a tenant, selling up or
dying, is an event: the lease comes onto the market and the Company re-rates it on the spot. A **grandfathered lease** is
also a thing a sublet baron will pay a fortune for, and a thing a player might inherit (file 13).

### Y58: the Foundation

The Crown's charity, **the Calder Restoration Foundation**, is launched at a reception in the Glasshouse on the first
anniversary of the Lifeline. Its founding chair is **Odile Aubrac-Mott**, whose daughter wore the green dress on the
launch in Y57. The Foundation gives **vouchers**, spendable only at the Company Store, to "families in need below floor 30".
Its first annual report records that every voucher it issued was redeemed at the Store, which it presents as evidence of
need, and which the Company presents as revenue.

### Y59: the Vance Report, the Unrecoverable Zone, the first Vitabrick

The Vance Report blames the storm, the wells and the climate. The Rusk Dissent is not bound in. The province declares Calder
an **Unrecoverable Zone**, the seabed vests in Halden Civil, and the reception camp at Ferrybridge is re-designated a provincial Resettlement Area (file 07); two thousand of its people are given a barge ticket back to the Stack, with a Lifeline lease, or a bus ticket inland. The **Calder
Recovery Authority** dissolves for lack of funds. Orrin Provisions sells the first **Vitabrick**, pressed on floor 11.

> "Vitabrick. Complete protein. Every brick, every day."
> First Orrin poster, Y59. Within a month someone on 11 had added, in chalk under every one: *"Every day."*

### Y60: the Co-op, and Chute Day

The **Mills Growers' and Workers' Co-operative** (the Mills Co-op) registers as a tenants' association (file 06a).

In the Ninth Month the Company issues the **Refuse Conveyance Bylaw**, later written into every Occupancy Charter as
**Clause 23**. Its operative words: the chute "conveys to the lessee such material as is discharged from above, without
warranty". Until Y60 the chutes had dropped straight to the Sump, where the gangs fought over what came down. The Bylaw
fitted **catch hatches** on the chute at every floor below 30, each hatch on a lease, each hatch opening once a day at a
time set by the Company: **the Morning Fall** (the Fall), at 06:00, when the night's refuse is released down the tower in one go and stops at
each catch for the leaseholder to take what they want before the rest falls on.

The people below 30 call the day the Bylaw took effect **Chute Day**. Its effect was to turn the tower's garbage into a
leased resource: what the floors above throw out is yours if you hold a catch, and nobody else's. Catch leases rent at a
premium. A catch on a floor directly below a rich floor rents at a large premium. The Sump gangs, who had lived off the
chute's end, lost the first pick of everything and got only what every floor above them had passed over. They have not
forgiven the Mills for taking the catches.

**In play:** this is the origin of the **garbage chute** container on your field: a catch hatch on your lease, which fills at
the Fall each morning from a table set by who lives above you, and which stops filling if you leave it full (the Bylaw's
"conveyance" stops at a blocked hatch, and the material goes on down to the next catch). What you do not take, someone below
you gets. A **catch-hatch lease upgrade** (a second hatch, on a different chute) is a real purchase. So is a **catch moved up
the queue**: a sublet baron on 18 will sell you the 05:50 pre-fall from a hatch he holds on a better chute, at a price.

**Hook:** the Bylaw sets the Fall at 06:00 for every chute but one. The chute serving floors 22 to 26 (the Dark Floors) has
no catch hatches and no Fall time. Something goes down it at night, in small amounts, and does not reach the Sump.

### Y61: the Capping, and the Food Delegation

Skywater receives the **Roof Catchment Concession**. The Founders' Gutter is re-plumbed into Skywater's risers and bottling
hall; the old downpipes are capped at 89; the brass rain taps on every tenth landing are welded shut. Skywater's first
bottles, "rain from the roof of Calder's tallest home", go on sale in the Terraces at a price per litre that the Mills
calculate, bitterly, in days of wages.

The same year, food inspection is delegated to Orrin Provisions under the **Food Standards Delegation** (file 05): the
company that makes the protein blocks inspects the people who make protein blocks.

In Y61 the **Office of the Arbiter** opens on 77 (file 05). Disputes between tenants and the Company, which since the Order had gone
to "the Company's discretion", now go to a counter, a fee and a sealed determination signed **A.**

### Y62: the pension, the Old Hands, the Enclosure

The Works pension is **suspended pending recovery**. The **Old Hands** form in the Works canteen on 14.

The Company announces **the Enclosure**: the Common on 30, the Plinth's roof garden, will be "brought into productive use".
Over eighteen months it is roofed, partitioned and leased. The bandstand is broken up; its cast-iron columns are sold to a
Terraces restaurant on Lantern Row, where they hold up the bar. The Forty plaque is moved indoors to a stair landing on 31.
Floor 30 becomes a lift lobby, the Turnstile, a block of Middle leases with no windows, and the Company's memorial slab (the
Receipt).

**In play:** a **bandstand column** fragment (cast iron, with the Works crest) is a heavy salvage item (2x4) that an Old Hand
will pay well over scrap for, and a Lantern Row restaurant will pay even more for, to complete its set. Selling it up or
giving it back is a small choice the Mills will hear about.

### Y63: VOID, the Dry Font, and the first drones

The **Ordinance on the Retirement of Branded Goods** (file 05): branded goods must be "retired" before disposal: slashed,
punctured, snapped or stamped **VOID**, so that nothing whole goes down the chute to undercut new stock. Brand protection
agents appear. The first VOID stamps are Company-issued, rubber, red. Within a year the Crown's households have their own,
and the stamp becomes a thing the rich do with a certain pleasure. Within two years the first people in the Mills are
cutting the VOID out of the leather and re-stitching.

The same year, on a rain night, **Dorota Kell** files through the weld on the dead rain tap at landing 30 and gives away
forty litres of old rain trapped in the pipe. The **Rain Church** dates itself from the **Dry Font** (file 06a).

And in the cold risers: the first **shaft couriers**. Lifts were tolled, slow, and in the Lift Guild's hands; the old Works
exhaust risers ran the whole height of the Plinth and much of the tower and nobody was using them. Kids from the Mills
began flying small Halden-cell quadcopters up them with parcels tied underneath: medicine, letters, a cell, a bottle of
something. They were called **shaft rats** within the month, not kindly. The first ones lost a third of their drones to the
risers' crosswinds and the rest to people with nets on the landings.

**In play:** the drone loop's prehistory. Your first drone, at game start, is a descendant of these: a cheap frame and a bay.
An **old shaft-rat frame** (Y60s pattern, open bay, no transponder) can be a starting machine or an early chute find: tiny bay,
no licence, flies free shafts only, and every Old Hand who sees it on your pad will tell you who flew one like it.

### Y64: the Gap

The South Stair's landing at 41 falls in, taking two people with it. The Company declines to repair it, as the North Stair
"provides adequate circulation". A family on 41 strings a plank-and-cable bridge across and charges for it: **the Gap** (file
02).

### Y65 to Y66: licensed lanes and the lane share

Drones have become how small goods move. The Company fits transponder gates in the open sections of the passenger cores
and in the old Tube shafts, and sells **Lane Permits**: the **licensed lanes**. In Y66 the Lift Guild, which has lost its
parcels and its produce runs to drones, makes its peace: a tenth of every lane fee goes to the Guild. From now on the Guild's
enemy is not the drone. It is the drone that does not pay, which is to say the shaft rats and the free shafts (file 06a).

**In play:** the route choice at launch (licensed lane: fee, safe; free shaft: free, rolls for trouble) dates from here. The
lane fee's tenth that goes to the Guild is why the Guild will **inspect** your drone's transponder at a lift lobby if you are
known to fly free, and why a Guild **freight chit** can be bought to send a whole drone up a lift car instead: slow, safe,
and visible.

### Y70: the Long Dark

In the Sixth Month of Y70 the Switch Hall on 4, rebuilt after Y57 on salvaged gear, fails again. Power below 60 is out for
**nine days**. Core D's cars stop at 44 and never move again; the shaft becomes the Stack's biggest free shaft. The Lung
runs on its emergency cells for three days and then slows. People on the Mills floors open the welded hall windows with
crowbars to breathe. Grow beds die in the dark across the Mills: the Co-op loses a season. Insect bins, which do not need
light, do not die; the insect ranchers come out of the Long Dark richer than they went in, and Vitabrick prices double.

All nine nights, the line of light under the doors on 22 to 26 stays on, and the hum does not change.

> "My father went and put his ear to the door on 24. He was a Line Nine man before the Shift. He came back and sat down and
> didn't say anything for an hour, and then he said, 'They're running the H-series.' And then he said, 'They can't be.
> That line was scrapped in Y39.' And then he went back and listened again."
> **Joss Arkady**, floor 21, recorded Y80

**In play:** the Long Dark is the template for a **blackout event**: power from the socket stops for some hours or days;
anything on your own charged cells keeps running; grow beds on lamps stop growing, insect bins keep going; prices for cells,
candles and protein jump. A player who keeps a charged cell bank (space, which costs rent) is the one who trades through it.

**Hook:** Joss Arkady's father heard the H-series. If the Dark Floors are making cells that were "scrapped" in Y39, where are
they going? Not to the Company Store, which has never sold one. (Files 02 and 14.)

### Y71: the Meter Strike

The Company replaces the founding-era water meters on 10 to 29 with new ones that read about eighteen per cent high. The
Old Hands prove it with their own test rigs. Forty-one days of the Mills paying rent and refusing water bills; HPS seizes the
Co-op's water stores as "unlicensed storage"; a settlement that lasts two years (file 05). In Y73 the new meters began failing in the damp and the Company quietly refitted the founding-era Sorrel meters it had kept in store; most Mills leases have Sorrels again. The Co-op votes its rule on
shared waste the same year (file 06a).

**In play:** the **first-fit Sorrel meter** is worth so much partly because of Y71: it is the one meter in the tower that is
believed to read true. A player who fits one on their own tap (illegal; the Company owns the meter) draws water at its real
volume. The difference shows on every bill.

### Y72: the Fractional Reduction

The per-square rate on one floor (Y72, floor 38, one quarter) goes **down**, by a fraction of a credit. The Company cites it
for eleven years as proof that rate review "operates in both directions" (file 05). In the Middle, "a thirty-eight" means a
favour too small to count.

### Y73: Static reads the Dissent

On a night in the Second Month Static (on air since Y57, file 06b) reads the Rusk
Dissent aloud, in full, with the stair-door log after it, and signs off with the number **seven**. That broadcast is the reason the Rusk Dissent is
known by heart by people who cannot read.

### Y74: the escaped stock

An Orrin insect bin on 21 is breached in a power cut; its stock escapes into the Mills and breeds with the Co-op's
unregistered lines. Nobody notices for seven years (Y81, below).

### Y76: Heritage Week

The Company marks the "Lifeline Year 19" (nineteen years since the Drowning, a number chosen because the Glasshouse
liked the sound of it) with **Heritage Week**: an exhibition on 30 of the founding of the Stack, Ansel Halden's first cell in
its glass case brought down from the Glasshouse for the occasion, the Marrow Set's cover sheet, and a panel on the Y57 storm
that describes the Stair Doors as "performing exactly as designed" in letters a little smaller than the rest. On the third
night someone chalks **5** on the glass case. On the fourth night the cell is moved back up to the Glasshouse.

### Y78: the Lift Strike and the second Lowering

The Lift Guild stops every car it runs for **twelve days** in the Fourth Month, after the Company raises the Lift Rent.
Machines cannot come up, the sick cannot go down to St. Ober's launches, and every small thing in the tower moves by drone.
Shaft rats have the best twelve days of their lives; lane fees triple; drone frames sell out at the Company Store. The
Company settles on the twelfth day by letting the Guild **double its freight rates**, which costs the Company nothing. The
Mills Co-op, paying double to lift its produce, switches to drones and shaft rats, and the Guild has never forgiven it (file
06a).

The same year, the **second Lowering**: the Terraces line, which had moved from 70 to 66 in Y69 (the first Lowering), moves to **63**. Floors 63
to 65 are re-rated as Terraces leases; their Middle tenants are offered "suitable alternative accommodation" in the forties.
A new gate is fitted at 63. The old gate frame at 66 is left standing, unhinged, and so is the one at 70.

**In play:** the **Lift Strike** is an event card (file 02 has the mechanics): the freight lift stops, deliveries of big
goods wait, drone contracts pay more. The **Lowering** is the long pressure: the canon "rent climbs as the upper floors expand
downward", with a history. Every Lowering pushes a band's worth of people down the tower, and each band's rent rises in the
year after, as the people pushed down bid for squares below. A player on 17 feels a Lowering at 63 two years later, as a rent
review and a crowd of new neighbours who used to be Middle and are not good at being Mills yet.

### Y79: Fan Room Three

The Lung's third fan room on 30 fails beyond repair. Old Hands strip it for parts to keep the other three running. When a
second fan room trips, the Mills get an **air day** (file 02). The first air days are in Y79. The Company begins selling
**room scrubbers** at the Store the same year.

### Y81: Standing, Bin Four, and Core F

The Ledger begins computing **Tenant Standing**, one number per tenant, from rent paid on time, contracts delivered, fines
and Arbiter determinations. Contracts above certain floors begin to require a minimum Standing (file 04). The Arbiter decides
**In re Bin Four**: every insect carrying Orrin's marker is Orrin's, with fees backdated to Y74 (file 05). Core F's track
above 60 fails, and Old Two has run only from 4 to 60 since.

**In play:** the credit rating in the game has a birthday. Old tenants remember life before Standing, when a contract was
offered to whoever was at the counter. A **pre-Standing reference** (a handwritten letter from a Middle client, Y70s) is a
quaint thing now, but a few old clients in the Middle still prefer it to the number, and will offer contracts to a player
who carries one regardless of Standing.

### Y83: the Eleventh Floor Adjustment

The residential half of floor 11 is converted to **storage leases** for Middle households; its tenants go to the Sump and to hot-bunks on 12. Seventy-
one identical determinations (file 05). It is the first time the upper floors expanded downward not by moving a gate but by
reaching past the Mills and taking a floor in the middle of it.

### Y84: the third Lowering, the Charter edition, the Tally revised

The Terraces line moves from 63 to **60**, to the Gills, where the Turnstile planners always said it belonged. The
**Occupancy Charter, Y84 edition** is issued (212 clauses, eleven schedules). The Tally's last revision reaches **21,640**.

### Y85: the cheap cells

A new line of power cells appears at the Company Store under the label **Halden Certified Renewed**: good cells, cheap,
with the Halden mark and no factory code. Old Hands open one and find a chip with a Bastion contractor's lot prefix. Others
open another and find a chip with no prefix at all, of a pattern last made on Line Nine. Both groups are right about their
own cell. (Files 07, 09 and 14.)

### Y86: now

The Gutter Case is decided (file 05): "The Office notes it cannot restrain the weather." Sixty ledge buckets seized in a week.
The Waterhouse's second membrane train is failing and the Mills have had more tap droughts this year than any year since
Y58 (file 03). There is a rumour of a fourth Lowering, to 58, and of a second floor in the Mills (17 is mentioned, because
17 is always mentioned) being looked at for storage conversion. The twenty-ninth Wall Night is in the Third Month.

## The Dark Floors, as history

The Dark Floors are the Clean Core: Line Nine, floors 22 to 26, opened in Y26 as the most advanced hall in the Works.

- **Y38**: switched to lights-out for a ninety-day trial that never ended.
- **Y40**: stair doors welded from the outside "for contamination control". Control room moved off 27, to nobody knows where.
- **Y41**: on the night after the Last Shift, its lights come on under the doors. They have been on at night ever since.
- **Y57**: on its own power through the Drowning; never flickers.
- **Y60**: its chute has no catch hatches and no Fall time.
- **Y70**: runs through the Long Dark unchanged; Joss Arkady's father hears the H-series.
- **Y85**: cells with Line Nine chips appear in the Company Store.

What the Company says, when asked: the Clean Core is "a secure Halden Works Holdings facility, operating in accordance with
all applicable standards". It is not on any lease map. It pays no rent, because the Company owns it. It draws power from the
tower's main at night, a lot of it (file 02). It is the only part of Halden Works that is still Halden Works.

> "Production never stopped. It just stopped employing anyone."
> Mills saying

**Alternative:** the Clean Core did stop, in Y57, when the water reached its supply lines, and what hums behind the doors
now is the founding-era cooling plant running on with no load, its lights on a timer nobody has reset since Y41. The cells
in the Store are Bastion's. This version is the Company's, unofficially, told to Middle tenants who ask; it is also the
version that would be easiest to believe if the Clean Core did not draw so much power.

**Alternative:** the Clean Core makes something other than cells: the drive chips for the Kessin lifts, the sensor chips
for the lease tiles, the chips in every tenant's tally. That is, it makes the tower. The Company does not know how either,
which is why it has never opened the doors.

**Hook:** every version has to explain one fact the Old Hands insist on: the light under the doors comes on at **22:00**,
the old Last Shift clock-off time, and goes off at **06:00**, the old first shift. A lights-out hall has no reason to keep
shift hours.
