# 04 — Economy and money

Speculative. Everything here is raw material for panning. Plain text is the main version, **Alternative:** is another version
of the same thing, **Hook:** is a story seed, **In play:** is how it surfaces in the game. Names, floor bands and the timeline
follow `00-canon-brief.md`. Prices are in credits (cr) unless marked as scrip (sc).

A note on scale, so every number below agrees with every other: a Mills labourer earns **about 30 cr a day**. A Vitabrick
costs **3 cr** at the company store. A litre of Halden Pure from a Mills tap costs **1.20 cr**. A square of your
board on floor 17 costs **a quarter of a credit a week**. The player's starting lease is the board as the game draws it, **25 x 20 =
500 squares**, so about **125 cr a week**. (A square is a square of the board, the cell an item sits on; see 3.1.) If a number
elsewhere in this file looks wrong against those four, the four win.

## 1. The money you hold

### 1.1 Credits and the Ledger

A credit is not a coin. It is a line in the **Halden Ledger**, the tower's account system, which was built at founding to
pay the Works payroll and to bill the rent of six thousand workers' flats on the same day without a single clerk. It still
runs. Nobody at Halden fully understands how. It sits in a climate-controlled room on floor 71 that people call the **Count
House**, and its upkeep is handled by four contracted technicians and one retired Old Hand who is paid a consultancy fee
larger than any of theirs because he is the only one who can read its fault codes.

Every tenant over fourteen has an account. Your account is your name, your lease number, your **Standing** (the credit rating,
section 5), your balance, your debts, and a running list of everything you have paid Halden for since the day you were
registered. It is reached through a **tally**: a flat plastic band worn on the wrist or on a cord, with a chip in it from the
Dark Floors' production line (nobody says this aloud). You touch your tally to a **pay post**, a waist-high column with a
cracked green screen, to pay or be paid. Pay posts stand at every lift lobby, every company store counter, every licensed
stall, and every launch pad. A drone's landing is booked to the Ledger the moment the pad reads its transponder.

What the Ledger does well: it never forgets a debt, it compounds interest to the hour, and it runs rent day across 104
floors in the four seconds after midnight on the seventh day.

What it does badly: it rounds every fraction of a credit up when you owe and down when you are owed ("**the Ledger's
thumb**"). It cannot handle names with more than 24 characters, so half of the Flats-born are registered as truncations of
themselves. It cannot record an asset it does not have a category for, which is why a founding-era part, the most valuable
thing in the tower, is listed at "0.00 / MISC SALVAGE" on every inventory the Company has ever audited.

**In play:** your balance is a plain number in the corner. Every income and outgoing is a line in a scrollable **ledger
slip** (the IDEAS "price board / your ledger" thought), printed at 20:00 each day: what sold, what each meter drew, what the
drones brought, what the Ledger took. Fractions are always rounded against you, visibly: a contract paying 41.6 cr lands as
41; a meter bill of 7.2 cr is charged as 8. Over a month the thumb costs the player around 2% of turnover, which is exactly
the kind of small humiliation the tone wants.

**Hook:** the Ledger keeps accounts for people who are dead. Debts carry on accruing; so do the credits of a few workers who
died during the Drowning with wages unpaid. One account, **WORKS-0-00117**, has been accruing a founding-era pension at the
founding-era rate for 86 years and now holds more than most Terrace families. It has no tally. Somebody has been trying to
make one.

### 1.2 Chits: credits you can drop

The Ledger does not reach everywhere. The Sump's pay posts drown or are smashed; the Flats have none; a deal you do not want
recorded cannot go through one. For that there are **chits**: bearer tokens issued by Halden itself, because a company that
owns every square still wants a cut of the trades it cannot see.

A chit is a thin rectangle of laminated card with a foil seal and a printed value: **1, 5, 20 and 100 cr**. You buy chits at
a pay post with a 3% "issuance fee", and you cash them back in at a pay post with a 3% "redemption fee". Halden makes six per
cent on every credit that passes through the dark and is perfectly happy with that. Chits are legal tender for any purchase
in the tower, including the company store, but stores are "permitted to decline" them, and the better ones on the upper floors
always do.

Chits wear. A chit with a lifted seal is "unverifiable" and a pay post will refuse it; in the Sump it still circulates at
80% of face. A chit with a torn seal circulates at half. Counterfeits exist (the foil is the hard part, and the Voided are very
good at foil, because foil is what brand seals are made of). An old saying in the Mills: "**A chit is what Halden owes you
until it doesn't.**"

**In play:** chits are physical items. A 1 cr chit is a 1x1 card; a stack of ten fits in a 1x1 **chit roll**; a **chit tin**
(2x2, holds 400 cr in any denomination) is the first strongbox you can buy. Chits take space on your shelves like any good,
which makes them the IDEAS "coins are items" idea with a reason behind it. Some contracts from the Sump and the Flats pay
only in chits, which you must carry or store until you choose to pay the fee and bank them. A drone that comes back from a
free shaft with chits in its bay can lose them to looters just like goods; credits on the Ledger cannot be looted, chits can.

**Alternative:** no chits. The Sump and Flats trade only in barter and charged cells (section 14), and the Ledger is the only
money there is. Simpler, and it makes the bottom more alien, but it loses the "Halden profits even from the dark" joke.

### 1.3 Halden scrip

Scrip is money that is only money at the **company store**. It is printed, not ledgered: small stiff notes in 1, 5 and 25,
dull green, with the founding-era Halden Works gear-and-tower mark, and along the edge in small capitals: **STORE CREDIT. NOT
LEGAL TENDER. NO CASH VALUE. VALID 90 DAYS FROM ISSUE.**

Scrip exists because it is cheaper for Halden to pay you in it. A credit paid in scrip comes back to the Company at the
store, with the store's margin on top, and if you let it expire it comes back with nothing on top because Halden never
spent it. The Company estimates (internally) that 11% of all scrip issued expires unspent. That 11% is booked as revenue.

Who pays in scrip:
- **Halden's own contracts** at the low end: maintenance tasks, chute clearing, meter-reading rounds, lift-shaft greasing. A
  Halden contract to the Mills often pays half credits and half scrip, or all scrip with a "bonus" that makes the face value
  look larger.
- **Orrin Provisions and Vey** pay suppliers below a certain size partly in scrip ("**supply credit**"), which is redeemable
  at Halden stores because both brands operate under Halden licence and settle with the Company at the end of each quarter.
- **The Foundation** never pays in scrip; it gives **vouchers**, which are worse (section 14.8).
- Some Middle employers pay part of their wage in scrip as a "staff benefit", and print the benefit on the wage slip as if it
  were a raise.

What scrip buys: everything at the company store. That is not nothing. The store sells drones, drone parts, licensed
machines, filters, cells, seed, Vitabrick and Halden Pure. A player who can live on store goods can live on scrip. A player
who wants anything from anyone else cannot.

The 90-day expiry is printed, but the Ledger does not track scrip notes, so the expiry is enforced at the counter by the
clerk, by eye. Store clerks are paid a small bonus per expired note they refuse, which they then hand to the shift manager,
who is meant to destroy them, and sometimes does.

**In play:** scrip is a second number (or a physical item: a 1x1 bundle of notes with a date on it, if coins are items).
Contracts on the board show their pay as, for example, `60 cr` or `40 cr + 30 sc` or `90 sc`. Scrip-paying contracts offer
more face value for the same job, so the choice is real: if you were going to buy a drone part at the store anyway, scrip is
as good as credits and the scrip contract pays better. If you need to pay rent, scrip is worthless. Expiry makes it a
perishable good: an old bundle shows its date and a tint, and on day 90 it turns into a 1x1 **dead scrip** item worth
nothing except as fuel for a stove or packing filler in a drone bay (which is, in fact, what people use it for).

### 1.4 The street rate

Scrip and chits trade against credits every day on every floor below 30, at a rate everyone knows and nobody prints. The
rate is said as a number out of a hundred: "**scrip's running at sixty**" means a hundred scrip buys sixty credits.

| Money | Usual street rate (cr per 100) | When it moves |
|---|---|---|
| Fresh scrip (60+ days left) | 62 | Rises before a store restock, falls after a Halden payday |
| Old scrip (under 15 days) | 35 | Collapses in the last week |
| Foundation Hope Voucher | 40 | Falls further in winter when more are issued |
| Sealed chit | 97 | Steady; the 3% is the cost of cashing it |
| Lifted-seal chit | 80 | Only below floor 12 |
| Torn chit | 50 | Sump only |
| Orrin supply credit | 70 | Better than store scrip because Orrin pays on time |

The people who make this market are **scrip jobbers**: small traders with a tin and a table who buy scrip for credits at the
street rate and spend it at the store on things they resell (drone parts, filters, Vitabrick by the crate) for credits again.
A good jobber turns 100 sc into 62 cr and then into 90 cr worth of goods sold on, and lives in the gap.

**In play:** a scrip jobber is a client and a supplier. She appears on the counter (or as a contract) offering to buy your
scrip at the day's rate, or offering to sell you store goods for credits at below store price because she paid in cheap scrip.
The day's rate is a visible number that moves with events (Halden payday on day 1 and 15 floods the market with scrip; a store
restock on day 10 lifts it). Holding scrip is a small speculation game you can ignore.

**Hook:** **Ines Coyle**, the biggest jobber on floor 12, has started paying above street rate for scrip, steadily, for
weeks, more than she could ever spend at the store. Somebody is buying up scrip in bulk. Either they know something about
the store (a price rise, a new drone model that only sells for scrip) or they know something about the plates the scrip is
printed from.

## 2. The company store

Official name: **Halden General Supply**. In the Middle the branches are bright counters with a queue line painted on the
floor, called "the Supply". In the Mills the branch is a counter behind a steel grille with a hatch the width of a Vitabrick
crate, and everyone calls it **the Hatch**. In the Sump there is one branch, on floor 8, and it opens three days a week.

There are eight branches in the tower: one in the Sump (floor 8), three in the Mills (floors 11, 18, 27), three in the Middle
(floors 33, 44, 55), one in the Terraces (floor 70, called "Halden Select", which does not accept scrip), and none in the
Crown, where Halden goods are delivered by people who never say the word "store".

### 2.1 What it sells

The store has a monopoly on nothing except three things: **new licensed drone parts** (frames, lane transponders, rotors, bay
liners), **licensed water** in bulk (Halden Pure by the jerrycan, filters with the Halden mark) and **new standard cells**
(the H-cell, section 10). On everything else it simply competes, and it competes by being the only shop that takes scrip, never
running out of the boring things, and selling on account.

Catalogue sections, as printed on the wall of the Hatch on floor 18:
- **Provisions:** Vitabrick (plain, "Savoury", "Sweet Ration"), Orrin noodle bricks, Orrin "Real-Blend" tins (8% real), salt,
  sugar substitute, Halden Pure by the litre and the 20-litre can.
- **Health:** Vey basics (fever tabs, gut tabs, mould-lung inhaler, wound film), Vey DoseLock refills (section 10), plasters.
- **Power:** H-cells new, H-cell recharge exchange (bring a spent cell, take a charged one, pay the charge and a "handling
  fee"), cable, plugs, licensed sockets.
- **Air and water:** Halden filter cartridges (water and air), tap washers, meter seals (you are not permitted to buy these;
  the item exists in the catalogue for wardens).
- **Growing:** Orrin OneCrop seed packets, Orrin grow medium, Brightline grow lamps and spectrum keys, plant food.
- **Workshop:** tools (licensed brands only), solder, tape, fasteners by weight.
- **Drones:** frames, bays, rotors, transponders, Lane Permits (sold here, issued by the Lift Guild, section 8), bay seals.
- **Machines** (by order, delivered in 1–3 days): insect bins, grinders, presses, air-wells, filter stacks, grow beds. All new,
  all licensed, all with a service contract you are required to sign.

### 2.2 How it prices

Store prices are set floor-by-floor by a pricing table at the Count House. The rule is simple and never written down: the
store charges whatever the floor can bear plus the cost of being the only place that never runs out. In practice:
- **The Mills pay more than the Middle** for the same Vitabrick (3 cr against 2.60), because the Mills have fewer alternatives
  and are poorer at haggling. Halden calls the difference "logistics weighting" (the Mills are closer to the warehouse than
  the Middle are).
- **Scrip prices are different from credit prices.** A Vitabrick is 3 cr or 4 sc. The scrip price is always higher, and the
  store's signs show the scrip price in large print because the number is the one people look for.
- **Account buying.** A tenant at Standard Standing or better can "buy on account": take now, have the cost added to the next
  rent bill at 4% interest per rent week. The store pushes this hard at the end of the month.
- **Exchange schemes.** Bring the old one back for a discount on the new: spent cells, empty Halden Pure cans, used filter
  cartridges. The returned goods are never refurbished; they go down the chute, VOID-stamped, because a working returned
  cartridge sold at half price would undercut a new one.

**In play:** the company store is a vendor screen (one of the IDEAS "special vendors" that sell machines). It is the only
place you can always buy a new drone part or a machine, at a high price, for credits or scrip. It never buys anything from
you except through the exchange schemes (bring 1 spent cell, get 2 cr off a new one). Its machines are reliable but bland:
average stats, a mandatory **service contract** that costs a fixed amount per week and lets a Halden fitter visit and
"maintain" it (in practice: replace seals with licensed seals and read its meter). A second-hand machine from a Sump fixer is
cheaper, odder and has no service contract, but it is not licensed, which matters in an inspection.

**Hook:** the Hatch on floor 18 has a back counter, behind the scrip window, where things are sold that are not in the
catalogue: crates "damaged in transit", cartons "past their compliance date". The clerk who runs it, **Sully Breck**, pays
Halden's stock auditors in Skywater. He will sell to you once your Standing says you are not a warden.

**Alternative:** the store is not one chain but franchises: each branch is leased by Halden to a **storekeeper** who buys stock
from the Company at a fixed price and keeps the margin, so each Hatch is run by a person with their own debts, favourites and
fiddles. The storekeeper on floor 11, **Mrs. Adaeze Plumb**, has held her franchise for 31 years and lends money to half the
floor in scrip she buys back cheap.

## 3. Rent per square

### 3.1 What a square is

A **square** is a square of your board: the cell an item sits on. A cell is 1x1, a charger rack 3x3, a crate 4x4; your lease is
a block of them, and the rent is so much per square. The game draws your shop as that block, 25 squares across and 20 down
(500 squares) to start, and renting more rows makes the board bigger and the bill too. Nothing in this folder gives a square a
size in metres. What a square is *on the floor* is the Company's business.

In the fiction the Company's side of it is a brass **lease stud** set in a lift-out service tile under every leased square.
The studs carry a faint founding-era signal, read by a surveyor's wand, that tells the Ledger which squares belong to which
lease. Nobody can install new studs. When a stud is pried out (they are brass, and brass sells) the square goes **dark** on the
Ledger, and dark squares are a whole small economy: a square the Ledger cannot see cannot be billed, and also cannot be
leased, so the Company sends **chalkers** to mark it by hand, which the Ledger does not trust, so it bills the neighbouring
lease for it "pending survey". The Company has never published how big a square is, and nobody has ever needed it to.

**In play:** your shop is a block of leased squares; the block is the game's field and the rent is per square. Squares can
have **features** that come from the building, not from you: a **pillar** (dead squares that cannot hold anything, billed at
half), a **pipe run** (a row where only flat items, 1-high, fit), a **drain** (anything liquid spilled there is lost; also the
only place a still or a bin can legally vent), a **chute hatch** (where your garbage chute lands), a **frontage** edge on the
corridor (see 3.4). A lease is a shape on the grid, not always a rectangle, so a cheap lease is often an awkward one.

*The starting board (working assumption, a proposal for the layout):* the two rows nearest the corridor are frontage (50
squares); a 2x2 pillar stands near the back wall with the socket board on its face; the catch lands in a 3x3 apron beside it;
a Company **Works Bench** (3x2) stands against the left wall. Everything else is open floor.

### 3.2 The lease

Every tenancy below the Crown is written on the same form, the **Halden Standard Tenancy Agreement** (people say "the
HSTA" or "your paper"), in one of three versions: **Domestic** (a bunk or a flat), **Commercial** (a shop or workshop) and
**Mixed** (both, at the commercial rate). The form is founding-era in wording and has been amended 140 times. Clauses tenants
know by number:

- **Clause 3 (Demised Squares):** the lease is a list of stud coordinates. Your squares are, for example, `F17/C-01..C-25/R-01..R-20`.
  Anything outside them is not yours, including the air above your squares higher than 2.4 m, which is "Common Overhead"
  and may be used by the Company for "services" (pipes, cable, drone lanes).
- **Clause 7 (Rate):** "the Rate per Square shall be as published in the Floor Rate Card for the Floor and Period, as amended."
  This is the clause that makes rent climb. You did not sign a number; you signed a reference to a card Halden prints.
- **Clause 9 (Metering):** all water, power and atmosphere shall be drawn through Company meters, and "self-supply" requires a
  licence. A water-from-air well is "self-supply". So is a solar sheet.
- **Clause 11 (Fit-out):** anything bolted to the floor becomes Company property at the end of the lease. (This is why nobody
  bolts anything; machines stand on rubber feet and wobble.)
- **Clause 23 (Refuse Access):** grants the use of the chute hatch inside the demised squares "as-is", and forbids "the sale
  or supply of refuse recovered therefrom where such refuse bears a brand mark", which is everything.
- **Clause 19 (Quiet Enjoyment):** the tenant shall have quiet enjoyment of the squares "save for entry by the Company, its
  agents, inspectors, wardens and contractors, at any hour".
- **Clause 22 (Arrears):** after one missed rent day, the Company may "suspend services" (meters); after two, it may "secure
  the squares" (a lockout); after three, the lease ends and "contents are forfeit in settlement".
- **Clause 31 (Assignment):** you may not sublet. Every sublet baron in the tower has a lawyer's opinion that their
  arrangement is a "licence to occupy", not a sublet, and the Arbiter has accepted the argument since Y61 (A/61/0007) because Halden
  likes the rent the barons pay.

Leases run for one **rent week** (seven days) and renew automatically unless either side gives notice. Halden gives notice
whenever it wants the squares. A tenant can give notice only on rent day, in person, at a pay post, and must leave the
squares "broom clean", which a warden inspects, for a fee.

**In play:** the lease is a physical slip you can read (it is also funny to read). Its practical content is three numbers and a
shape: your squares, your rate, your rent day. Taking more squares is signing a **lease extension slip** at the pay post: new
rows appear on your field (if the adjacent squares are free) and the rent rises. Giving squares back is free but takes a
day ("broom clean inspection"), and the warden may find something.

### 3.3 The Floor Rate Card

Rent is **base rate for the floor x condition x frontage**, charged per square per rent week. The base rates, from the current
card (Period 86/3). The Ledger rounds the quoted rate up to the next hundredth of a credit, visibly:

| Floors | Band | Base rate (cr per square per week) | A 500-square board costs per week |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4–5 | Sump (tidal) | 0.05 | 25 |
| 6–9 | Sump | 0.10 | 50 |
| 10–13 | Mills (low) | 0.15 | 75 |
| 14–19 | Mills | 0.20–0.30 (floor 17: 0.25) | 125 on floor 17 |
| 20–21, 27–29 | Mills (high) | 0.35–0.45 | 175–225 |
| 22–26 | Dark Floors | not leased | — |
| 30–39 | Middle | 0.60–0.90 | 300–450 |
| 40–49 | Middle | 1.00–1.40 | 500–700 |
| 50–59 | Middle (high) | 1.50–2.20 | 750–1,100 |
| 60–74 | Terraces | 3.00–5.50 | 1,500–2,750 |
| 75–89 | Terraces | 6.00–9.50 | 3,000–4,750 |
| 90–104 | Crown | not leased; held as **title** | (a title on 92 last changed hands for 1.4 million cr) |

Condition multipliers (applied per square, printed in small type below the card):
- **Wet** (standing water at high tide, Sump only): x0.5.
- **Dark** (no corridor light): x0.8.
- **Vent-adjacent** (next to a shaft vent: bad air, noise): x0.8.
- **Pillar / pipe run:** x0.5, still billed in full on the Domestic form because "a bunk is a bunk".
- **Chute-adjacent** (smell): x0.9 on Domestic, x1.1 on Commercial (because a chute hatch is "an amenity" to a business).
- **Frontage:** x1.5 (see below).
- **View** (a window to the outside): x2 on the Terraces, x0.9 below floor 30, where a window means you can see the Flats and
  the storm comes in through the seals.

### 3.4 Frontage

A frontage square is a square of your lease on the corridor edge: on the board, the rows along the front. Customers walking past
see what is on it. That is where your show window goes, where your shelves sell themselves, where the counter faces out.
Frontage is billed at x1.5.

A Mills shop lease is usually cut so that a tenth to a fifth of it is frontage. A shop with no frontage is a workshop: it can make, it
can launch drones, but nothing on its shelves sells to passers-by. Sublet barons buy frontage in long strips and rent single
frontage squares to hawkers by the day.

**In play:** this is where the IDEAS shop-and-shelves line meets the rent line. Shelves and the show window only sell when
they stand on frontage squares; a frontage square costs half again as much. The player's starting lease has **50 frontage
squares** (the two front rows) out of 500. Taking more frontage is the "grow the shop" path; taking back rows is the "grow the workshop" path.
Both cost rent; one costs more.

### 3.5 Cutting leases

Leases are drawn and redrawn by **Halden Survey and Allocation**, whose surveyors everyone calls **chalkers** (they mark new
lines in yellow chalk before the Ledger is updated, and the chalk goes down before the notice does). A chalker carries a stud
wand, a chalk line, a stamp, and the authority to "rationalise" a floor: take two bunks and make three, take a shop and give
its frontage to the lease next door.

Rules the chalkers work by:
- **Minimum lease:** a 4 x 4 block (16 squares). Below that it is "a locker", leased on a different form at a higher rate.
- **Contiguity:** a lease should be contiguous, but "should" is a word chalkers know the weight of. An L, a U, or a lease
  with a common corridor cutting through it are all legal.
- **Offcuts:** squares left over after a re-cut, often around pillars and pipes, are let as **offcut leases** at x0.7. Offcuts
  are where the poor of the Mills have their shops, and where the player can expand cheaply into shapes nobody else wants.
- **Rationalisation fee:** if the Company re-cuts a floor and your lease changes shape, you pay a 15 cr survey fee, because the
  survey was for your benefit.

**In play:** **re-cut events.** A notice slip arrives: "Survey and Allocation will rationalise floor 17, sector C, on day 32."
On that day the shape of your lease changes: you might lose a frontage square to the neighbour, gain an offcut around a
pillar, or have a common corridor cut through your workshop. Anything on a lost square is moved into a **displaced goods
crate** at the door (a container you must empty). You can pay a chalker (grey, 30–60 cr) to "survey favourably" before the
event. This is a small, sharp way to make the field shape itself a resource that the world takes from you.

**Hook:** **Dorrit Vance**, chalker, floors 14–19, has been resurveying the same twelve squares on floor 16 every quarter for
three years, each time a little differently, each time for a different tenant. The squares sit directly under a sealed hatch
in the ceiling that is not on any floor plan.

### 3.6 Rent day, arrears, lockout

Rent day is every seventh day, at midnight. The Ledger takes the rent from your balance automatically. If the balance is short:
- **Day 1 short:** a red **Arrears Notice** prints. Your Standing drops sharply (section 5). Meters are "suspended", which in
  practice means throttled: the tap gives a trickle (a tenth of normal flow) and the socket runs at half power. Late fee: 10%
  of the rent.
- **Day 8 short (second rent day missed):** **lockout**. A Halden contractor seals the frontage with a mesh shutter and a
  padlock. You can work inside, but nothing on your shelves sells, the counter is closed, and the launch pad is disabled.
- **Day 15 short:** forfeiture. The lease ends; contents are seized "in settlement". Debt collectors are assigned for any
  shortfall (file 05 covers them).

The Lease Office's own step-by-step version of the same ladder, with debt sale and lease adjustments between the rent days, is
in 05.

**In play:** rent day is the weekly heartbeat. The red notice, the trickle from the tap and the mesh over the window are all
visible states of the field. The trickle is a nice touch for hour steps: every machine that draws water gets a tenth of its
need, so grow beds slow and insect bins dry, and the player feels arrears as their production stalling, not just a number.

### 3.7 The Creep

Rent climbs. Officially through **Rate Reviews**: every 30 days, a new Floor Rate Card is printed with the new Period number,
and the base rates of most floors rise between 1 and 4 percent "in line with the cost of services". Tenants call the whole
process **the Creep**.

The Creep is not even. It comes from the top. When the Terraces want more space, Halden **reclassifies** a floor: the top floor
of the Middle becomes a Terrace floor, its tenants are given notice, its leases are re-cut into larger units with glass
frontages, and its rate triples. The floor below it now sits next to the Terraces and gets a "**proximity uplift**" of 20%,
because its tenants are now near the Terraces (and its location is "improved"). Three floors below that, the uplift is 10%.
The line where the Terraces start is called **the Glass Line**, for the glass corridor fronts the Terraces install. Sixty years
ago the Glass Line was at floor 80. At the Partition in Y58, floor 70; after the first Lowering in Y69, floor 66. It is at floor 60 now. Old Hands in the Mills keep a chalk mark
on the lift lobby wall with the year and the floor, and the marks are getting closer together.

The Middle, squeezed from above, does the same thing below itself. Middle tenants priced out of floor 30 move down to floor 29,
outbid the Mills tenants for the best squares, and Halden quietly reclassifies floor 29 as "Middle (Transitional)". That line
has no nickname yet; the Mills call it "**the Chalk**", because that is how you find out where it is.

Rate Review schedule (working numbers for the game):
- **Baseline Creep:** every 30 days, every base rate below the Glass Line rises 2%.
- **Pull:** floors within 3 of a reclassified floor rise a further 10–20% on the next card.
- **Reclassification events:** the Glass Line moves down one floor roughly every **year** of game time; the Chalk moves down one
  floor roughly every **90 days**. Both are announced 14 days ahead by notice slip.
- At this pace, floor 17's base rate goes from 0.25 to about 0.32 in a year of baseline alone, and if the Chalk reaches floor 20
  it jumps to 0.35 or more.

**In play:** the Creep is the long clock. The rate card is a slip you can pin to the wall; each Period's card arrives on day
30, 60, 90, with the new number circled. It is the pressure the IDEAS notes asked for ("rent climbs as the upper floors expand
downward") and it gives the three classic answers: **earn faster**, **shrink** (give squares back, pack tighter), or **move
down** (cheaper rent, worse chute, worse routes). And it gives a fourth: **own** your squares before the Chalk reaches you,
which is the loan path (section 6). A player who moves up instead rides ahead of the Creep but pays far more per square.

**Hook:** the Chalk moves faster than the cards explain. The Mills Co-op has a theory that Halden is not reclassifying floors
because the Middle wants them, but because the Dark Floors (22–26) need the floors around them empty, and the easiest way
to empty a floor is to price it.

**Alternative:** no automatic baseline Creep; rent rises only through reclassification events, which are rarer but brutal
(+30% overnight). More dramatic, less steady pressure. Or a hybrid where the baseline is small (1%) and the events carry the
weight.

## 4. Meters and billing

Clause 9 of the lease says everything you breathe, drink or plug in comes through a Company meter. Three of them matter:
the **tap**, the **socket**, and, from the Mills up, the **vent**.

### 4.1 The meters themselves

The founding-era meters were built into the walls at founding, one per flat, with a brass face, a glass window and a ring of
little numbered drums. They were built to last two centuries. They have lasted eighty-six years, and they drift: an old
meter over-reads by 3 to 8 percent, sometimes more, never less (a meter that under-reads is found and replaced within the
week, because a Halden fitter's bonus depends on it). New meters, bought from Vantage's supplier, are plastic boxes clamped
to the old pipe, which read accurately and fail in the damp after two years. Halden puts new meters in the Middle and keeps
the old ones in the Mills, "for continuity of service".

Each meter carries a **meter seal**: a numbered lead-and-wire tag through the casing. A broken seal is a fine (150 cr) and a
Standing hit whether or not anyone touched the inside. Seals break in the damp. Wardens carry spare seals and will reseal
your meter for a small, informal fee before the inspector comes.

### 4.2 Water

Halden Pure is billed by the litre at the tap. The tariff is called **Volume-Fair Pricing**, and it works so that people who
use more pay less per litre:

| Tariff | Who | cr per litre |
|---|---|---|
| Lifeline | first 3 litres a day per registered adult, Domestic leases only | 0.80 |
| Domestic | above Lifeline | 1.20 |
| Commercial (Mills, Sump) | all draw on a Commercial or Mixed lease | 1.20 |
| Commercial (Middle) | volume tier, over 200 L a week | 0.95 |
| Terraces Residential | flat fee, "unlimited" | 140 cr a week |
| Crown | rain, collected from the roof; Halden Pure on request | none billed |

On top of the per-litre price comes the **standing charge**: 2 cr a day for being connected. A tenant who draws no water at
all still pays 14 cr a week to have a tap. Disconnection costs 40 cr and reconnection costs 80.

Under the tap there is a second pipe on most Mills floors: **service water**, the grey, cloudy outflow from the floors above,
settled, chlorinated and sold back down for washing, flushing and cooling at **0.15 cr a litre**. It is not for drinking and
says so in three languages on a plate. Everyone in the Mills knows exactly how many boilings and which filter it takes to drink
it anyway, and the Stinkers' whole trade begins at that pipe (file 03 has the water in depth).

**In play:** the tap is a machine on the field with two outputs, **Pure** and **Service**, and a meter display. Every millilitre
drawn into a vessel goes on the daily bill. Machines that draw water each hour (grow beds, insect bins) draw from a vessel in
their slot, so the player chooses what to fill it with:
- Halden Pure: expensive, and produce grown with it can be certified **Real** (section 11).
- Service water: cheap, but produce grown with it is only **Real (uncertified)**, which the Crown and Terraces will not
  accept, and insect bins on service water have a small chance per day of a "bad batch" (the insects die off, a slot of dead
  insects for the grinder at lower yield).
- Your own water (an air-well, a rain catch you are not allowed to have, a Stinker's still): free per litre, but takes
  squares, and squares cost rent. Always pay the tower or spend space, as IDEAS puts it.

### 4.3 Power

Power is billed in **Halden units (hu)**, roughly a kilowatt-hour, through the **socket**: a heavy founding-era wall plate
with four outlets and a meter drum, one per lease. Tariff in the Mills: **0.80 cr per hu**, with a **Peak Window** from
17:00 to 20:00 at 1.20 cr, because that is when the Terraces come home and cook. Standing charge: 1.50 cr a day.

Most machines in a Mills workshop draw between 0.1 and 1 hu an hour: a grow lamp 0.2, an insect bin's heater 0.3, a grinder
0.8 while running, a launch pad 2 hu per launch to spin the drone up, a charger 0.5 per cell-hour. A workshop running a grow
bed, two bins and a lamp all day spends 10–12 cr on power, half of a labourer's day.

The alternative is **cells**. A charged H-cell holds 2 hu. Bought charged at the store it costs 2.40 cr plus a 0.40
"handling fee" on exchange; charged in the Sump off a stolen line it costs 1 cr. A machine running off a cell in its slot
draws nothing from the socket. Cells are heavy and take a square each in a cell rack.

**In play:** the socket is a machine with a meter, and machines either plug in (billed per hour) or take a cell in a slot
(free while it lasts). The **Peak Window** makes the last three hours of each day more expensive to run, which nudges the
evening into a packing-and-launch time rather than a production time. A cell rack is a container; charged cells are an item
that loses charge over time (slowly) and runs out in machines (visibly). Swapping the bin's cell at the right hour is the kind
of hour-step tending the IDEAS still asked for.

### 4.4 Air

Air is metered on a sliding scale of honesty.
- **Below floor 30**, air is billed as a flat **Atmosphere Levy**: 0.012 cr per square per week (about 6 cr for a 500-square board), added to rent. It pays for the
  founding-era 30 Lung, which pulls outside air through salt and dust filters and pushes it up the vents. In
  the Mills the plant runs at about 40% of its rated capacity. In the Sump it does not reach.
- **From floor 30 up**, air is metered by the vent: each flat has a **vent register** that counts the volume of filtered air
  delivered, and tenants choose a tier: Standard, Fresh (an extra filter stage), and Fresh Plus (with "botanical notes",
  which are a scent cartridge).
- **In the Crown**, air comes through the windows.

**In play:** air is optional in the game. The lightest version is the levy on the rent bill, a line nobody can do anything
about, which is the point. A heavier version: a **vent** square on your lease that you can fit with an air filter (a machine)
or not, and a **damp** value for the shop that rises without one, which slowly grows mould on stored goods (mould is a state
that halves value, and mouldy food goes to the insect bin, where mould does not matter). This keeps the IDEAS rule: nothing
happens because two things sit near each other; damp is the shop's property and the filter is a machine.

### 4.5 The bill

At 20:00 each day the Ledger prints the **Daily Service Statement** (the bill slip): water by tariff, power by window,
standing charges, levy, the Ledger's thumb, and a line called **Common Load Adjustment**.

Common Load Adjustment is the share of power and water drawn on your floor that no meter accounts for: corridor lights,
lift lobby heaters, leaks, and theft. It is split across every Commercial lease on the floor by squares. On most floors it is
a few cr a week. On floors 19, 20 and 21 it is four times higher than on any other floor in the tower, and it is highest at
night.

The bill is paid automatically from your balance if your Standing is Standard or above (**postpay**). Below Standard, you are
on a **prepay meter**: you buy units in advance at a pay post (in blocks of 10 cr), and when they run out the tap and socket
stop at the end of that hour. A prepay tariff costs 10% more per unit "to cover administration".

**In play:** prepay is the early-game state. The player starts on prepay (Standing is Probationary), with a little stock of
units, and must top up. Running dry mid-afternoon stops every plugged-in machine in that hour step, which is a sharp, legible
punishment that the player can always see coming on the meter display. Reaching Standard moves you to postpay: the
machines never stop, and the bill comes at night, which is a real quality-of-life unlock and a reason to care about Standing
early.

**Hook:** the Common Load on floors 19–21 rises and falls with the hum from the Dark Floors above. Someone who could read the
meters on 19, 20 and 21 hour by hour (an Old Hand, say, or a meter reader with a grudge) could tell you what the Dark Floors
are making and when they run their big jobs. **Benedek Sarr**, meter reader for the Mills, has kept those readings in a
notebook for six years, and the notebook is for sale.

### 4.6 Meter tricks

Everyone below the Middle knows them, and every one is a fine if caught:
- **Magnet on the drum** (old meters): a strong magnet slows the drum. Founding-era meters are shielded, so it needs a big
  magnet, and the Flats magnet fishers sell the best ones.
- **Bypass loop:** a length of pipe or cable around the meter. Needs the seal broken. The oldest trick and the most often
  caught, because a bypassed meter reads zero, and zero is what inspectors look for.
- **Tuning:** a paid fitter opens the meter, adjusts the drum ratio, and reseals it with a genuine seal he bought from a
  warden. It reads 70% of the truth. Costs 60 cr, lasts until the next inspection.
- **Borrowing:** running a hose or a cable from a neighbour's meter, by agreement and for a share. Common between Co-op
  members, who split the bill by eye.
- **Off the books:** drawing on the unmetered corridor supply, which goes into the Common Load and is therefore paid by the
  whole floor, which is why floors hate it and wardens are paid to report it.

**In play:** a **meter tuning** service from a fitter client: pay 60 cr, the meter reads x0.7 until the next inspection
event, which rolls a chance each week; if found, a fine and a Standing hit. Pure risk-for-money, legible, optional.

## 5. Standing (the credit rating)

### 5.1 What it is

Officially, the **Tenant Standing Index**. Everyone says "**Standing**". It is a number from 0 to 1,000, computed by the Ledger
for every account, recalculated at midnight, and printed on your tally screen next to your balance, in a smaller font. It
decides almost everything Halden will let you do.

| Standing | Band | Called | Tally colour |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0–199 | Delinquent | "in the red" | red |
| 200–399 | Probationary | "on probation", "a pro" | amber |
| 400–599 | Standard | "standard", "flat" | white |
| 600–799 | Preferred | "pref" | blue |
| 800–1,000 | Trusted | "glass" (as in Glass Line) | silver |

New tenants start at 300. People born in the tower start at 250, because children of tenants "inherit a risk profile". People
from the Flats are registered at 150 and cannot get above Probationary for their first year.

### 5.2 How it is computed

The Ledger's formula is a founding-era scoring model with eight decades of Company amendments layered on top. Tenants know the
inputs by experience. Working numbers:

**What raises it:**
- Contract delivered on time: **+4** (Halden and licensed clients); **+2** (unlicensed clients who report through a pay post).
- Contract delivered early (more than a quarter of the deadline left): +1 extra.
- Rent paid on rent day: **+6**.
- Daily bill paid (postpay) or prepay never running dry that day: **+1**.
- Tenure: **+2** for each full rent week on the same lease.
- Loan payment on time: **+2** a day.
- A licence held in good order (food handling, reseller, launch pad registration): **+1** a week each.
- A warden's "good conduct" note: +5 (wardens may write three a quarter, and sell them).

**What lowers it:**
- Contract delivered late: **−10**. Failed (not delivered, drone lost, wrong goods): **−25**.
- Rent short on rent day: **−60**. Each further day in arrears: −10.
- Loan payment missed: **−20**.
- A meter fine: −40. A broken seal: −15.
- A warden's report: −5 to −40 depending on what is reported.
- Caught with VOID-stamped goods for sale: **−50** and a brand protection fine (section 10).
- **Association:** trading with a Delinquent account through a pay post: −3 per trade. Halden calls this "network risk".
- **Inquiry:** applying for a permit, a loan top-up, a lease extension or a move: **−5** per application, approved or not.
- **Thin file:** you cannot rise above 600 until you have 30 completed contracts on the Ledger, and not above 800 until you
  have 200 and a full year of tenure.

**What does not happen:** Standing does not recover on its own. Nothing is forgotten. A −60 from a missed rent day is paid
back only by doing ten rent days on time.

**In play:** one number, as IDEAS proposed, with a short visible log of the last few changes ("+4 delivered: Orrin
Provisions, F41 / −5 inquiry: lease extension"). The **inquiry penalty** is a small, mean, very legible rule: every
application costs you a little Standing, so you do not apply for things speculatively. The **association penalty** puts a
price on dealing with the poorest clients through the Ledger, which pushes such trades into chits and barter, which are off
the Ledger, which is exactly how the grey economy is made.

### 5.3 What it gates

| Gate | Needs |
|---|---|
| See a contract on the board | each slip has a minimum; most Mills slips 200–400, Middle 400–600, Terrace 600–800, Crown 800+ |
| Postpay meters | 400 |
| Buy on account at the store | 400 |
| Lane Permit (licensed lanes) up to floor 59 | 400 |
| Lane Permit up to floor 89 | 650 |
| Crown Lane clearance | 850 and a sponsor |
| Lease squares in the Middle | 500 |
| Lease squares in the Terraces | 750 |
| Title (the Crown) | not a Standing question; by invitation |
| Loan top-up (borrow more on the Pathway) | 450 |
| Insurance at standard premium | 400; below it the Mutual charges double |
| Reseller licence (to sell branded goods legally) | 500 |
| Water treatment licence | 700, and the fee |
| Sponsor a new tenant | 650 |

**In play:** Standing is the main **progression gate** for the contract board, alongside clearance on the drone. It is the
"rating" axis the IDEAS notes asked for, and it makes the top of the tower visible but unreachable early: you can see Terrace
slips greyed out with "Standing 600" printed in the corner, and the embossed card style (contract slips styled by origin) is
visible even when you cannot take them.

**Hook:** Standing can be bought. Not officially. A Terrace account in good order can **sponsor** a Mills tenant, which
copies a fraction of its Standing across for a year. Sponsors are paid for this, quietly, and the market price of a
sponsorship (about 1,500 cr for +150 Standing) is known on every floor. **Lady Fenwick-Orrell** of floor 77 sponsors forty
tenants at once, and the Count House has never asked why.

**Alternative:** Standing is not one number but three (Payments, Deliveries, Conduct), shown as three bars, and contracts
gate on different ones: a Vey medical contract wants Conduct, an Orrin bulk order wants Deliveries, the store wants Payments.
More texture, more to read; IDEAS prefers one number, so this is an option, not the main version.

## 6. The shop loan: the Proprietor Pathway

### 6.1 What you signed

Nobody in the Mills can buy a shop with money. You buy it with debt. The Halden product is the **Proprietor Pathway Advance**
("**the Pathway**"). Halden advances the cost of setting up a shop: the lease premium (a one-time fee for the right to take
Commercial squares at all), the first machines, a starter drone and launch pad, the first month of licences. You repay it
daily. When it is paid off, the squares named in the Pathway convert from leased to **held title**: you own them, rent
stops, and nobody can evict you.

The player's Pathway at the start (working numbers):

| Line | cr |
|---|---|
| Lease premium, 500 Commercial squares, floor 17 | 1,500 (3 per square) |
| Title price of those squares, fixed at signing | 2,500 (5 per square) |
| Starter machines (one insect bin, one grow bed, a grinder) | 620 |
| Starter drone (Halden "Wren" frame, 3x2 bay) and launch pad | 540 |
| Licences, first month | 80 |
| Arrangement fee | 120 |
| **Principal** | **5,360** |

- **Daily service charge:** 0.15% of the outstanding principal, added at midnight. On 5,360 that is 8.04 cr a day at the
  start.
- **Minimum daily payment:** 15 cr, taken at 20:00. If the payment is less than the day's charge plus 1 cr, the shortfall is
  added to the principal ("capitalised").
- **Prepayment:** you may pay more at any pay post; a **prepayment adjustment** of 3% is charged on any lump sum over 100 cr,
  "to compensate the Company for income foregone".
- **Rent continues** while the Pathway is open. You are paying for the squares twice: once to rent them, once to buy them.
  The Pathway brochure calls this "building equity while you trade".
- **Missed payments:** three missed in a rent week is a **Pathway Lapse**: the advance becomes immediately due; if it cannot
  be paid, Halden "restructures" it (a new Pathway with a new arrangement fee and a higher charge) or ends it, in which case
  everything paid is "applied to rent and services", and the shop is yours on a plain tenancy with no equity.

Paying only the minimum, the Pathway is repaid in about **500 days**. Paying 40 cr a day, about 150 days. A good player can do
it in under 100.

### 6.2 Held title

When the Pathway closes, the squares are yours as **held title**. You no longer pay rent on them, only a **Title Maintenance
Charge** of 0.025 cr per square per week, 12.50 for a 500-square board (subject to Rate Review), and the meters and levies as before. You cannot be locked out
for arrears on titled squares (only on any leased squares you added later). You can sell titled squares to anyone with the
Standing to hold title on that floor, which on floor 17 is anyone at Standard.

Squares added after signing are not in the Pathway. They are rented at the current card. You can buy them into title later
at the **current title price**, which moves with the Creep: a title on floor 17 costs 5 cr a square today, and about 10% more
every time the Chalk moves a floor closer.

**In play:** the Pathway is the clearest numeric spine the game has: a balance that goes down, a daily charge that shrinks as
it does, a big "Title" stamp when it ends. It is one of the canon endings ("own your squares") and also a mid-game turning
point, because rent stopping on 500 squares is a large, permanent change to the weekly maths, which then makes expansion
(more rented squares) the new pressure. A **Pathway statement** slip arrives weekly with the balance, the charge, and a
brochure line ("You are 18% of the way to owning your future!"). Moving floors while the Pathway is open is allowed, but the
title squares stay on floor 17: you can end up owning a shop on a floor you have left, which you can then sublet, which is
illegal, and which is exactly how sublet barons start.

**Hook:** the title price was fixed at signing, but the paper says "fixed at the Period of signing, subject to revaluation
on reclassification". If the Chalk reaches floor 17 before your Pathway closes, your floor is reclassified, and your title
price is revalued at the Middle rate. Tenants on floor 29 lost two years of payments this way. **Ruth Okonkwo-Hale**, one of
them, now runs a Mills Co-op school for reading leases, and she charges in produce.

**Alternative:** no rent while on the Pathway, but a higher daily charge, so it reads as a mortgage rather than a double
payment. Kinder in maths, weaker as satire. Or: the Pathway is not Halden's but a second lender, **Calder Proprietor Finance**,
which Halden owns through a sister company and which sells the debt on to collectors at the first lapse.

## 7. Insurance: the Mutual

**The Calder Mutual Assurance Syndicate**, called **the Mutual**, insures drones, cargo, shop stock and machines. It is the
insurance syndicate of the canon list, and it is a syndicate in the old sense: a pool of Terrace and Crown investors who put up
capital and take the premiums. It has an office on floor 64 with a waiting room that has never once been full, because claims
are made by slip.

### 7.1 Products

- **Bay Cover:** cargo cover for one drone launch. Premium: **4%** of declared value on a licensed lane, **12%** on a free
  shaft. Pays 60% of declared value on a covered loss.
- **Frame Cover:** the drone itself. 2 cr a day per frame.
- **Stock Cover:** goods on your squares against fire, flood and "sudden contamination". 1% of declared stock value per week.
- **Machine Cover:** breakdown of a licensed machine. Bundled with the store's service contract and not available
  separately. Unlicensed machines are not insurable.

### 7.2 Exclusions

The Mutual's standard schedule of exclusions runs to four pages. Mills tenants can recite the short list:
- **Acts of shaft:** any loss in a free shaft "attributable to the inherent hazards of unregulated airspace". Looting is an
  inherent hazard. So is jamming. So is a free shaft.
- **Acts of tide:** flood, damp, salt and anything "arising from the location of the premises below the historic waterline".
  Every floor below 30 is below the historic waterline, because the historic waterline is drawn at floor 30.
- **Inherent vice:** goods that spoil, rot, sprout, hatch, ferment or "change state". All food. All insects. All seed.
- **Civil disturbance:** any loss in which more than one person took part.
- **Unapproved packing:** cargo not packed to the Mutual's standard, which requires a Mutual-approved bay liner (sold at the
  store, 12 cr).
- **Recovery evidence:** a claim needs the drone back with its **bay seal** (a numbered tag) intact. A looted drone has a
  broken seal, which means the loss cannot be proved, which voids the claim.

The Mutual pays about one claim in nine. It advertises "**over 10,000 claims settled since founding**", which is true.

### 7.3 The Kitty

The shaft rats run their own cover: **the Kitty**, a pot kept by whichever courier is trusted that season, into which every
member pays one cargo item's worth a week (in chits or goods). If a member loses a bay to looters, the Kitty pays half its
value in kind, on the courier's word. It pays more often than the Mutual and goes broke every few months, when the keeper
disappears or a bad week takes three bays.

**In play:** insurance is a **slip** you pack into the drone bay alongside the contract slip (the IDEAS "permit packed in its
bay" pattern). It takes a 1x1 square of cargo space, which is itself a cost, and the premium is paid at launch. On a covered
loss it pays out on landing. The free-shaft exclusion makes the slip worthless on the routes where losses happen, so the
real choice is: licensed lane + cheap insurance (safe, costly) or free shaft + no insurance (risky, free), or free shaft +
**the Kitty** (a weekly fee, half value in goods, sometimes nothing). Joining the Kitty needs a shaft rat's vouching, which is
a relationship you build with courier clients.

**Hook:** the Mutual does pay one kind of claim promptly and in full: losses on drones carrying goods to St. Ober's Spire.
Nobody knows why. The Mills say the hospital owns the Mutual. The Flats say it is the other way round.

## 9. The chute economy

### 9.1 How the chutes work

Four founding-era chutes, the **North** (from 103, "the Crown Chute"), **East** (from 89, "the Kitchen"), **South** (from 85,
"the Long Drop") and **West** (from 88, "the Paper Chute"), run down the four corners of the tower's core to the Sump.
Each is a steel-lined throat about a metre across, with a hatch on every floor.

Refuse does not fall all day. Each floor's hatch empties into a **holding hopper**; the night's refuse is held at the Reclaim
Station on 59 through the Quiet and released at **06:00**: **the Morning Fall**. Below floor 30 you can hear it start as a
distant rumble and come closer, floor by floor, for about two minutes, and then end with the long crash into the grates of
the Sump. People set their mornings by it. Children in the Mills are told the Morning Fall is the Crown snoring.

Between the hoppers and the bottom, refuse can be taken in three ways:
- **The Reclaim Station** on 59: Halden's licensed sorting concession, **Halden Reclaim**, which pulls out anything on
  its rebate list (metals by weight, cells, certified-voided goods to log for the Disposal Rebate) and lets the rest fall.
- **Diverter hatches** on Commercial leases: a founding-era flap built into some floors' chutes, which tips a share of what
  falls past into a bin inside the lease. These are the "garbage chutes" of IDEAS: your hatch on floor 17 catches a slice of
  what the floors above you threw away and missed being skimmed. A diverter takes roughly one part in twelve of what passes.
  Clause 23 of your lease grants you its use "as-is".
- **The Bale Hall**, floor 8: where the chutes end at the Jaws; unsold bales go out by the Spill Door on 4. A long dripping hall
  of steel mesh above black water. What reaches the Spill is sorted by Sump crews under whichever gang holds the Spill that season.

### 9.2 The skim

The higher your diverter, the more there is to catch, and the more of it has already been taken. Refuse that reaches floor 17
has passed the Reclaim Station on 59, a dozen diverters in the Middle, and every Mills diverter above you. What
survives the skim is what nobody higher up could use or sell: food waste, packaging, VOID-stamped goods too damaged to bother
with, and things the people above did not recognise as valuable (founding-era parts, mostly, which look like junk to anyone
who did not work at the Works).

Which floors feed your chute matters more than which floor you are on. A diverter on the West Chute (Terraces offices, the Bureau)
catches refuse from the floors the West Chute serves; the South Chute (the Vey clinics on 64-65) serves the clinic floors (more
medical waste, more Vey packaging); the East Chute (Lantern Row, the Middle's family floors) carries food waste and packaging.
Leases with a North Chute catch rent at a premium on the black market of
sublets, and Halden prices them at x1.1 on the card as "an amenity".

**In play:** the chute is a special container on your field (the diverter bin) that fills at 08:00 each day from a **loot
table set by the chute and the floor**: a South Chute hatch rolls more Vey blister packs and clinic plastics; an East Chute hatch more
food waste and packaging; a lower floor has a thinner table. If you leave the bin full, the flap jams and nothing comes in
(the IDEAS rule: pay in space or lose supply). Moving floors changes your chute, which is a reason to move that is not about
rent. Founding-era parts sit at the very bottom of the table and are the jackpot of the morning sort.

**Hook:** the Crown Drop has no hatches. Except that the founding drawings, which the Archivists hold, show one: a service hatch
on floor 17, sealed during the Drowning year. Whoever opens it would catch the Crown's rubbish before anyone else in the tower
had touched it.

### 9.3 What each band throws away

The Crown (via the Crown Drop, straight to the Spill):
- Plate waste of real food: fruit peel, cores, bones, bread crusts, whole portions not eaten. Dyed Orrin Blue (9.4) but still
  real; the best insect feed in the tower and, if you know how, a source of **fertile seeds** from heirloom fruit that never
  went near a OneCrop packet.
- Furniture "refreshed" every season: real wood, real leather, cut through and stamped.
- Clothing: natural fibre, slashed. Shoes, soles cut.
- Wine and spirit bottles, real glass, punctured at the base.
- Founding-era fittings ripped out in renovations: door handles, light fittings, valve wheels, brass. The single richest source
  of founding-era parts in the tower, thrown out because they are "dated".
- Flowers, cut and dead. Soil from changed planters, which is real soil, which nobody else has.

The Terraces (Chutes B and D):
- Office electronics: screens, slates, terminals, retired on schedule, bricked by a Retirement Signal (10.2) and snapped.
- Cells at 80% capacity: corporate policy retires them early. Terminals clipped.
- Clinic waste (Chute D): Vey packaging, half-used DoseLock bottles, single-use instruments, dressing film, gloves.
- Clothing in synthetic fibre, slashed. Children's toys that have been recalled or outgrown, snapped.
- Real-food scraps from Terrace kitchens, less than the Crown's, dyed.
- Packaging in huge quantity: card, film, moulded trays.

The Middle (Chutes A and B):
- Appliances "out of support": kettles, fans, small heaters, grow lamps with expired spectrum keys (10.2). Often working.
- Metered cells at end of cycle count, terminals clipped.
- Real-Blend tins, Vitabrick wrappers, noodle trays, food waste (mostly synthetic, some real).
- Filter cartridges past the date on the strip (often only half used).
- Clothes worn through; shoes delaminated.
- Broken furniture (printed and pressed, worthless as material).

The Mills (Chute C):
- Very little. Ash. Insect frass (sometimes; usually it is sold as fertiliser). Broken things nobody could fix. Packaging that
  has been reused four times already.

The Sump throws nothing down the chute, because the chute ends at the Spill on floor 4, and the Sump is the Spill. The Sump's
waste goes into the water through the drains, and the Flats get it at low tide.

### 9.4 VOID: how goods are killed

Upper-floor tenants under brand licence agreements (and every Terrace lease includes one) must dispose of branded goods
according to the **Brand Integrity Disposal Standard**, which tenants call **BIDS**. A branded good that goes down the chute in
working condition is a breach; the brand can fine the tenant, and Halden can deduct Standing. Compliance is paid for: Halden
gives a **Disposal Rebate** of 0.40 cr per kilogram of certified-voided goods logged at a Reclaim Station, which in a large
Terrace household adds up to a meaningful credit on the monthly bill. Voiding is thrift.

Every BIDS method has a name:
- **Stamp:** an ink stamp **VOID** plus a rebate code, on anything with a surface. The minimum, used on things that are
  already broken.
- **Cancel:** a hand press called a **canceller** punches a VOID mark through card, plastic or thin metal and leaves a hole. On
  packaging, cases, tins, labels.
- **The X:** two cuts across the front of any garment, upholstery or bag, chest to hem. A shirt with the X is a pattern now,
  not a shirt.
- **Puncture:** a spike through the base of a bottle, can or vessel. Glass gets a star-crack at the base.
- **Snap:** a bend-and-break on anything with a hinge, a frame or a spine: devices, tools, toys, glasses.
- **Brick:** for electronics, the **Retirement Signal**: a code sent over the tower network that tells a device it has
  reached end of life. It erases the device's licence and refuses to boot. The hardware is usually perfect.
- **Clip:** for cells, a cutter snips the terminals and a punch dents the casing. A clipped cell has its charge in it and no way
  to deliver it.
- **Blue:** for food, a spray of **Orrin Blue**, a bright cobalt denaturant that marks food "not for human consumption". It
  washes off most surfaces with effort; it does not wash out of bread. It is mildly toxic to people. Insects do not mind it.
- **Crush:** for licensed seed, medicine and anything small and high-value: crushed in a **denaturing gel**, a grey paste.
  Medicine voided this way is gone. Seed voided this way is gone.
- **Leg:** the joke name for furniture: the legs of chairs and tables are sawn through at different heights.

Wealthy households employ **disposal valets** who do all of this for them, and who quietly do it badly in exchange for a
cut of what the Voided make from it downstairs. A bad valet's X misses the seams; a bad valet's puncture is a small hole
near the edge; a bad valet's Retirement Signal was never sent.

**In play:** VOID is an **item state** with a **method**: a garment item "VOID (X)", a bottle "VOID (puncture)", a slate
"VOID (bricked)", a cell "VOID (clipped)", bread "VOID (blue)". The method decides what can undo it and with what tool or
machine: a sewing kit for the X (and the result is "mended", a visible state with its own value), a cell re-terminating jig
for clipped cells, a wash basin and a lot of water for blue, an Old Hand or a flash rig for bricked electronics, nothing for
crushed. **Bad valet** goods are a variant: the VOID is cosmetic and the item is near-whole underneath, which the analyser
(IDEAS) can reveal ("?" until scanned).

### 9.5 Who sorts

- **You.** The diverter bin on your lease is your supplier. Sorting it each morning is a packing job: food waste to the insect
  bin, cells to the rack, metal to the scrap crate, VOID goods to the bench or the shelf, the rest to the **ballast** sack
  (packaging and inert rubbish, which some drone contracts actually want as bay filler, and which Halden charges 2 cr a sack to
  take away if you want it gone).
- **Pickers.** Mills kids and old people who sort other tenants' diverter bins for a share or a few cr a morning. A picker can
  be hired: give them the bin and a plan (what to keep, as in the IDEAS hirelings-as-plans idea), and they sort it during the
  first hour while you do something else, and bring a **left-behind** pile you can check.
- **Reclaim crews** on 59: Halden employees on shifts, paid by the kilo skimmed, who take what is on the rebate list and
  steal what is not.
- **Bale pickers:** the Sump's sorters at the Bale Hall's Jaws on 8 at 06:10 every morning, who get the North Chute's
  leavings. A sack of unsorted North Chute bale sells in the Sump for 30 cr and is the most gambled-on object in the tower.

**In play:** a contract or vendor: **"Crown sack, unsorted, 30 cr, Spill crew, floor 4"**: a 2x2 sack item that opens into a
small field of Crown refuse: real-food scraps, a voided silk shirt, a punctured wine bottle, maybe a brass valve wheel. A
buyable mystery box with real odds, sold by the lowest people in the tower out of the waste of the highest.

### 9.6 Undoing the void

Undoing voided goods is a trade, the trade of **the Voided** (file 06 has the faction). It is illegal under brand protection
law to sell, offer or supply any branded good that has been voided "or restored from a voided state". In practice the law is
enforced on the Middle floors where brand agents walk, and only intermittently below.

What can be undone, and what it fetches:

| Voided as | Undone by | Result | Fetches (of new price) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stamp | solvent, a rag, an hour | almost new; the stamp shadow shows under light | 70% |
| Cancel (packaging) | not worth it; repackaged in plain | plain-packed goods | 50% |
| The X (garment) | sewing, a patch inside, an hour or two | "mended" | 40% |
| Puncture (glass) | resin plug and a polish | resealed bottle; holds liquid, not pressure | 30% |
| Snap (device) | a splint, solder, luck | working, ugly | 30–50% |
| Brick (electronics) | a flash rig and a founding-era master key, or an Old Hand | working, unlicensed | 60%, grey only |
| Clip (cell) | re-terminating jig, solder | working cell, uncertified | 70% of a new cell, grey only |
| Blue (food) | washing (water!), peeling | insect feed if you are honest, food if you are not | as feed: full; as food: illegal |
| Crush | nothing | nothing | 0 |
| Leg (furniture) | new legs, or cut the others to match | a lower chair | 50% of a real-wood chair, which is a lot |

**In play:** this is one of the richest small chains in the game: chute → VOID item → a tool or machine for the method → an
"undone" item that sells only to grey clients, carries a **brand risk** (a chance per brand-agent event of a fine if found on
your shelves or in your bay), and fetches a large share of a new price. Every voided good is also a **choice** about who you
serve: a real-wood chair restored with new legs is worth a lot to a Middle client, and nothing to a Crown client who wants
it new, and very little to a Mills client who wants a seat. Bluewashed food is the dark edge: sell it as feed (legal, low) or
as food (illegal, high, and your Standing is gone if you are caught selling dyed food to people).

## 10. Brand protection and planned obsolescence

Every licensed brand in the Stack sells things designed to stop being useful on a schedule, to be repaired only by the brand,
and to be impossible to make again at home. These are the examples the Mills complain about most, with what each one means to
someone running a workshop.

### 10.1 Food and growing

- **Orrin OneCrop seed.** Hybrid, sterile: grows once, sets no viable seed. Packets of 10, 6–25 cr depending on crop. A
  tomato grown from OneCrop gives fruit with seeds that look perfect and never sprout. This is the reason the **seed savers**
  exist, and why a handful of fertile heirloom seeds is worth more than a crate of fruit.
- **OneCrop "Assured Germination" clause:** Orrin seed is sold under a licence that forbids saving seed, and a Grow Certificate
  is void if a bed is found growing a non-OneCrop variety. So growing saved seed costs you the Real grade (section 11), even
  though the produce is more real than anything Orrin sells.
- **Orrin grow medium** with a nutrient load that exhausts after one crop. You can re-use it, at half yield, which the
  packet calls "unsupported use".
- **Orrin Protein Starter colonies** for insect bins: a sterile line of mealworm and cricket that breeds for six generations
  and then crashes. A bin started from Orrin stock needs a new colony every season. A bin started from a Mills rancher's wild
  line never crashes, but a Vitabrick pressed from wild insects cannot carry the Orrin licence and must be sold unbranded.
- **Licensed press dies.** A protein-block press needs a die, and Orrin owns the shape of the Vitabrick. Pressing your own
  blocks in a Vitabrick die without a licence is brand infringement; pressing them in any other shape is legal and sells for
  less, because people trust the shape.

### 10.2 Power, light and electronics

- **The H-cell** (Halden standard cell, made by Halden Works, now made on the Dark Floors or by Bastion; nobody official says
  which). Each cell has a **cycle counter**: after 300 charges it reports itself as "End of Service" to any licensed charger,
  which refuses it. The cell still holds 85% of its charge. Only an unlicensed charger will charge it, and using an unlicensed
  charger is a breach of the socket's terms (Clause 9).
- **Certified Recharge Only:** a cell charged on an unlicensed charger flags itself as "Uncertified" to a licensed machine,
  which will run it at reduced draw "for your safety". So a grey-charged cell powers a licensed grow lamp at 70%.
- **Brightline grow lamps** need a **spectrum key** (a small plug-in chip) for each crop type, licensed per season (90 days),
  10 cr a key. Without a valid key the lamp runs a "maintenance spectrum" that keeps plants alive but halves growth. Expired
  lamps go down the chute by the hundred from the Middle with perfectly good LEDs.
- **Retirement Signal:** every licensed device has an end-of-life date set at manufacture. Halden's network sends the signal,
  the device bricks. Screens 3 years, slates 4, drone controllers 5. Founding-era electronics predate the system and never
  receive it, which is part of why they are worth so much.
- **Halden Lane transponders** are paired to one drone frame. Moving a transponder to a different frame needs a "re-pair" at
  the store (25 cr). A drone with a mismatched transponder cannot enter a licensed lane.
- **Licensed rotors** carry a chip that the transponder checks. Unlicensed rotors fly perfectly; the transponder will not
  arm, so a drone with grey rotors cannot use a lane, only the free shafts.
- **Bay seals** (the numbered tags that prove a bay was not opened) are single-use and sold only at the store, 1 cr each. The
  Mutual will not pay a claim without one; some contracts require one.

### 10.3 Water and air

- **Halden Pure SureSeal cans:** 20-litre cans with a valve chip that counts fills. After 40 fills (or any fill from a
  non-Halden source, which the chip detects by flow rate) the valve locks shut. A locked can is a sealed plastic box with
  water-shaped space inside. It goes down the chute, punctured.
- **Halden filter cartridges** carry an **expiry strip** that turns red after 30 days whether or not the filter has been used.
  A licensed filter housing will not accept a red-strip cartridge. A half-used cartridge with a red strip filters exactly
  as well as it did the day before.
- **Skywater bottles** have a one-way valve in the neck: they can be emptied, never refilled. Skywater sells the bottle as
  "tamper-proof purity". Refilled Skywater bottles (valve drilled out) are the most common grey good in the Middle.
- **Halden air filter stacks** (the vent machine) report their filter state to the vent register; a stack running a non-Halden
  filter is logged as "unfiltered" and the tenant is charged the Standard tier anyway.

### 10.4 Medicine

- **Vey DoseLock:** Vey medicines in the Middle and up come in a dispenser with a timed cap that releases one dose at a set
  interval and locks. Refills are cartridges that only fit the dispenser they were prescribed to. A dispenser dropped in the
  chute with three doses left cannot be opened without breaking the pills.
- **Vey "Continuity" pricing:** the first course of anything is cheap. The second is not. A mould-lung inhaler costs 8 cr the
  first time and 22 cr a refill.
- **Unit-of-use dating:** Vey medicines expire 60 days after opening and 180 days after manufacture; the date is printed in a
  colour that fades. Old Hands say the actual stuff lasts years.

### 10.5 Tools, clothing, everything else

- **Licensed tools** from the store carry a **service mark**: opening one voids its warranty, and the warranty is what makes it
  legal to use in a licensed workshop under the service contract. A broken licensed tool is returned to the store on exchange
  (3 cr off a new one) and goes down the chute snapped.
- **Halden Works machines** from the store come with a mandatory service contract (5–15 cr a week). If you let it lapse, the
  machine shows "Service Due" and runs at 80%.
- **Workwear** from the store is sold with a seam glue that fails after about 80 washes, which in the Mills (where washing uses
  water, which costs) means about two years.
- **Boots:** soles bonded, not stitched, so they cannot be resoled. A Mills cobbler stitches them anyway, and the boots
  outlive the brand.
- **Packaging deposits:** Orrin tins carry a 0.20 cr deposit, refundable only at an Orrin collection point in the Middle,
  which is open on alternate Tuesdays.

**In play:** each of these is a **rule on an item or a machine**, visible and steerable, never hidden tedium:
- Seeds carry **OneCrop** (no seed from harvest) or **saved** (seed from harvest, no Certified grade).
- Cells carry a **cycle count** and a **certified / uncertified** flag; licensed machines throttle uncertified cells.
- Grow lamps carry a **spectrum key** slot; the key is a 1x1 item with a 90-day life.
- Filter cartridges carry an expiry; a licensed housing refuses expired ones; a grey housing (from a fixer) accepts anything.
- Machines carry a **service contract** state; lapsing it is cheaper and slower.
- Drone parts carry **licensed / grey**; grey parts lock you out of lanes, never out of shafts.
Every one of these gives the player a choice between paying the brand and working around it, and every workaround costs
something else: grade, legality, lane access, a slower machine.

**Hook:** founding-era goods have none of these locks. A founding-era cell has no cycle counter, no certification flag, no
Retirement date, and still holds most of its charge after eighty years. Halden Works knew how to make that cell. Halden
stopped making it in Y41, the year of the layoffs. The Archivists have the spec sheet.

## 11. Real and synthetic: grades

### 11.1 The marks

Every food, fibre and material good in the Stack can carry a grade mark, and contracts name the marks they accept. The marks
are printed or stamped by the producer and checked (in theory) by licensing inspectors and (in practice) by the buyer's nose.

| Mark | Means | Who checks | Typical price multiple (vs Synthetic) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **R✓ Certified Real** | grown from licensed seed, in a certified bed, on Halden Pure | food inspector, Grow Certificate | x6–x12 |
| **R Real** | actually grown, but not certified (saved seed, service water, no certificate) | the buyer | x3–x5 |
| **RB Real-Blend** | synthetic base with a stated share of real (Orrin's Real-Blend is 8%) | the label | x1.5 |
| **S Synthetic** | protein paste, printed fibre, pressed board, flavoured base | nobody | x1 |
| **Rc Reclaimed** | recovered from refuse: voided goods undone, washed, repaired | nobody, and it is illegal for branded goods | varies |
| **F Founding** | founding-era make (parts, tools, fittings) | Old Hands, Archivists, a penthouse fixer's eye | priceless; see 12 |

**Real** is a word for food and fibre (a real tomato, real cotton, real wood, real leather); **Founding** is a word for things
made by the Works and its suppliers before Y41.

### 11.2 Who wants which

- **The Crown** accepts **R✓** only, and for luxury items prefers **heirloom** (a sub-mark, H, for named old varieties, which can
  only be grown from saved seed, which means an heirloom can never be R✓; the Crown pays fixers to get around this, and the fixers
  pay inspectors).
- **The Terraces** accept R✓ for anything they eat, R for gifts and "artisanal" goods, Rc for nothing, publicly.
- **The Middle** buys RB for daily eating, R✓ for occasions, R when they can get it cheap and want to tell people about it.
  Middle clients are the main buyers of Rc goods: restored, refilled, mended, at a third of new.
- **The Mills** buy S and Rc. R is a treat for a birthday or a funeral.
- **The Sump** buys S, and Rc of the worst kind.

### 11.3 Grade fraud

**Real-washing** is the art of making S look like R: a synthetic tomato paste extruded into a tomato shape and dipped in wax;
printed fibre with a cotton slub texture; pressed board stained to look like wood. Penthouse fixers carry a **spectro wand**
that tells the difference; most Middle buyers carry nothing. A real-washed tomato is a crime only if someone checks.

**Certificate-washing** is the art of making R into R✓: a grower with a certificate lends their stamp to growers without one
for a share. The Mills Co-op does this openly between members and calls it "**pooling**".

**In play:** the grade is a mark on the item (the IDEAS "effects" mark idea), and contracts list accepted marks. The chain decides
the grade:
- A grow bed with a OneCrop seed, Halden Pure in its vessel and a Grow Certificate on file gives **R✓**.
- The same bed with service water, or saved seed, or no certificate gives **R**.
- An insect bin → grinder → press always gives **S**.
- Mixing (a press with a slot for "real" additions: a few grams of real herb into the paste) gives **RB**, a cheap way to
  climb a little.
- Anything through the VOID-undo chain gives **Rc**.
- A founding-era part is **F** whatever its condition.

A **spectro wand** (a bought tool) lets you check an item's real grade when buying from a supplier; without one, a supplier
might sell you real-washed goods marked R, which then fail an up-floor contract's check on delivery (the delivery check from
IDEAS: wrong goods = failed contract = −25 Standing). A grey **certificate stamp** borrowed from a Co-op member turns R into R✓
on one batch, at the risk of an inspection event.

## 14. The informal economy

Below floor 30 more value moves off the Ledger than on it. Halden knows, and is fine with it, as long as rent is paid in
credits. Everything below is how people get by between rent days.

### 14.1 The yardstick: the cell

On the Ledger the unit is the credit. Off it, below floor 20, the unit is a **charged H-cell**. Everyone needs power, everyone
can measure charge (every trader carries a thumb-sized **cell gauge**, a needle on a dial), and a cell is small, hard to fake
and useful in itself. Prices on the Ledge (14.11) are said in cells as often as in credits:

| One charged cell (2 hu) is worth about | |
|---|---|
| Vitabricks | 1 (Mills), 1.5 (Sump) |
| Litres of Stinker water | 4 |
| Litres of Halden Pure | 2 |
| Hot-bunk shifts | 1 |
| Tallow candles | 5 |
| Chillies | 20 |
| An hour of a picker's work | 2 |
| Credits on the street | 2.40 to 3 |

A half-charged cell is worth half. A cell that reads "Uncertified" to a licensed machine is worth 80% to someone who owns
licensed machines and full value to someone who does not. A cell slowly loses charge on a shelf (about 1% a week), so cell
money is money that leaks: people below 20 spend fast and save in other things.

What people save in, when they can: **founding-era cells** (a Works cell under the bunk is a pension; it never leaks), real
soil, heirloom seed, a good drone part, a debt someone else owes them.

**In play:** charged cells already exist as items (section 4.3). Letting Sump and Mills clients **pay and accept payment in
cells** makes them a second currency without a new system: a contract from floor 6 pays "3 charged cells", a fixer sells a
grey air-well for "60 cells or 160 cr". The cell gauge is a cheap tool that shows a cell's exact charge; without it, cells
from strangers show "?" (the IDEAS hidden-but-visible rule) and might be half flat.

### 14.2 Jugs

A **jug** is a litre of clean water owed. **Jug tickets** are issued by Stinkers and by Co-op stills: a scrap of hard plastic
cut from a SureSeal can, punched with the still's mark (a star, a key, three holes), good for one litre at that still on
demand. Because everyone needs water and every still has a reputation, jugs from a known still circulate as money on its floor
and the floors around it. A Mills family's savings might be forty jugs from the **Marrow Street still** on floor 14 and twelve
from the Co-op still on 16.

A jug is only as good as its still. When a licensing inspector seals a still (file 05), its jugs die overnight; holders
swarm the still's door the morning after a rumour, and a run on a still is the closest thing the Mills have to a bank crash.
The saying: "**Never hold more jugs than the still holds water.**"

The **Rain Church** refuses to issue jugs. Water cannot be owed, they say; it is given. This makes the Church's free water the
only water in the Mills that cannot be used as money, and therefore the only water that reliably goes to the people who need
to drink it.

**In play:** a jug ticket is a 1x1 item with a still's mark. Grey clients can pay in jugs; you can redeem jugs at that still
(a vendor) for water in your vessels, or pass them on. An **inspection event** on a still makes its jugs worthless; a
**rumour** item from the counter ("they say Marrow Street's next") is the warning. Holding jugs is cheaper than holding Pure
in your squares, which take rent. Saving in water without taking up space, at the risk of a crash.

### 14.3 The slate and markers

Every canteen, bar and Co-op hall in the Mills has a **slate**: a board with names and numbers in chalk. "On the slate" is
credit; being wiped off the slate is the best day of a month. A written IOU is a **marker**: a card with a sum, a name, and a
thumbprint in ink. Markers circulate: if Ama owes you 20 and you owe Petrus 20, you give Petrus Ama's marker. **Marker brokers**
buy markers for cash at a discount (a reliable name at 80, an unreliable one at 30) and collect them themselves, or sell them on
to Halden's contracted debt collectors, who buy them by the bundle. **Taddeo Quill**, floor 12, buys any marker at all, which
is how everyone knows he is working for someone.

**Co-op share hours** are a different kind of marker: the Mills Co-op keeps an **hour board** where members log work done for
the Co-op (or for each other, through it). An hour of anyone's work is worth an hour, whatever the work: an Old Hand's hour of
repairing a founding-era pump and a child's hour of picking count the same. Share hours buy what the Co-op holds: tool loans,
time on the Co-op's big press, a water share from its still, space in its grow hall, a seat at its meetings. They cannot be
spent anywhere else and they cannot be converted to credits, which is the rule that keeps the Co-op from becoming a bank.

**In play:** markers are items: a client short of cash pays in a marker on someone else ("Ama Dorset owes the bearer 20 cr"),
which you can hold, collect (a contract to that person's floor), pass on, or sell to a broker for a fraction. Co-op share
hours are a separate counter on the Co-op's screen: earn them by doing Co-op contracts (low pay, high goodwill) and spend them
on the Co-op's machines (rent a big press for a day instead of buying one), which is a whole progression branch that never
touches the Ledger.

### 14.4 Favours: turns

The oldest currency in the Stack has no number. A **turn** is a favour owed. "I owe you a turn." "He's three turns down to the
Old Hands." Turns are not transferable, not countable, not forgotten. Old Hands work almost entirely in turns: they will look at
your failing founding-era meter for a turn, and you will owe them, and one day an Old Hand will knock and ask you to carry a
crate to floor 9 without looking inside.

**In play:** a **turn** is a relationship flag per client or faction, not an item: "Old Hands: you owe 1 turn" or "Marrow Street
still: owes you 2 turns". A turn owed to you can be called in as a service (a repair, a contract without a fee, a warning about an
inspection); a turn you owe gets called in as a contract slip you cannot refuse without losing the relationship. Simple,
visible, and it lets factions exist in the economy without currencies of their own.

### 14.5 The hock

Pawnshops are called **hocks**. A hock lends 40% of what it thinks a thing is worth, charges 10% a week, and keeps the thing if
you are four weeks late. The **hock ticket** (a numbered stub) is itself traded: a ticket for a good drone part with two weeks
left is worth buying from a desperate owner at a little over the loan. **Granny Oyelaran's** on floor 11 is the oldest hock in
the tower; she has a back room of unredeemed founding-era things she will not sell to anyone the Old Hands have not vouched for.

The thing that cannot legally be hocked is a **tally**. People hock them anyway. Lending your tally lends your account: your
balance, your Standing, your lease. A Delinquent tenant can **rent the tally** of someone at Standard and trade through it, and
the owner takes a cut and the risk. It is how half the grey economy touches the Ledger at all.

**In play:** a **hock** vendor buys anything at 40% as a loan, with a ticket and a deadline, and sells forfeited goods at
something like full grey price. It is a way to raise cash before rent day without selling a thing you need back, and it
punishes misjudging the week. A tally-renting client offers to trade through your account for a cut, which earns money and
costs Standing through association (5.2) when their trades go bad.

**Hook:** the most valuable tally in the Mills belongs to a man who died in Y79. His account is still at 640 (Preferred),
because he never missed a rent day in thirty years, and nobody has told the Ledger he is dead. It is passed from hand to hand on
floor 14, rented by the day, and anyone holding it can see the Middle's contracts.

### 14.6 Water, power and favours between neighbours

Most of the Mills' informal economy is not markets. It is neighbours. A hose through the wall to a neighbour's tap, the bill
split by eye. A cell lent until Thursday. A bin of insect feed passed over because yours is full. A child sent to watch a
neighbour's grow bed while she sleeps. None of it is recorded and all of it is remembered.

**In play:** **neighbour** slots. The squares next to your lease belong to other tenants (visible on the field's edge as their
stuff); a neighbour is a small recurring client who can lend (a cell, a vessel of water, an hour of watching your machines),
borrow, and complain to the warden. Neighbours change when a re-cut or the Creep moves people.

### 14.7 Sublets and hot-bunks

Sublet barons exist because of one sum. A baron on floor 13 leases **a whole hall** from Halden for 600 cr a week, cuts it into
33 bunks, and lets each bunk by the shift: three 8-hour shifts a day, seven days, 3 cr a shift. A full bunk earns
63 cr a week on 18 cr of rent. Thirty-three bunks earn over 2,000 cr a week. The baron pays wardens to not report, a fixer to
keep order, and Halden its rent on time, every time, which is why Halden calls him a "valued Commercial tenant" and has never
once read Clause 31 to him.

The **hot-bunk house** rules, posted on every door:
- Shifts: **First** (06–14), **Second** (14–22), **Graveyard** (22–06). Graveyard is cheapest because it is the loudest: the
  Morning Fall comes through at the end of it.
- **Warm bunk** (straight after the last sleeper): 2 cr. **Aired bunk** (an hour's gap, sheet turned): 3 cr. **Own sheet**: 3.50.
- **Locker** for your things while you work: 1 cr a day. Things not in a locker belong to the next shift.
- No cooking. No water on the bunk. No visitors. No refunds after the first hour.

Barons also sublet **frontage**: a stall's width of busy corridor for a hawker's day, 3 cr; a strip of four for a week, 60. And
**squares by the hour** for workshops without space: a 3x3 patch next to your board with a socket, for four hours to run a
grinder, 1.20 cr plus whatever the socket draws.

The biggest baron in the Mills is **Moss Underhill**, who holds some thirty halls across floors 11 to 14 through eleven
different names. He started with a dead uncle's bunk on floor 12 and a tally nobody cancelled.

**In play:** sublets work in both directions:
- **Renting from a baron:** take a few extra squares (a patch beside your board) for a day or a week at a higher rate than Halden's but **without a lease
  extension**, without an inquiry hit on your Standing, and without a broom-clean inspection when you leave. Expensive,
  flexible, and illegal for both of you, so a warden event might find it.
- **Being a baron:** once you hold title (section 6) or more squares than you use, you can let them by the shift or the day. A
  slow passive income, a risk (a warden report, a Clause 31 action at the Arbiter), and a choice that the world will remember.

### 14.8 The Foundation's vouchers

The Foundation is the Crown's charity. Its **Hope Desk** opens on floor 12 every fourth day. To receive a **Hope Voucher** you
register (your tally is read; your account is flagged **Assisted**), attend a one-hour **Dignity Session** (a talk about budgeting,
given by a Terrace volunteer, with a printed workbook), and receive one voucher with a face value of **20 sc**, valid for 30 days
and spendable only at a Halden store on the **Essentials List**: Vitabrick, noodle bricks, Halden Pure, Vey basics, workwear.
Not seed. Not tools. Not drone parts. Not anything that would let you stop needing a voucher.

How it is paid for: Crown donors give credits to the Foundation and receive **Benevolence Credit** from Halden, which reduces their
Title Maintenance Charge by one and a half times the donation. Halden pays donors more than the vouchers cost, because every
voucher comes back to Halden's store at Halden's margin, and about a fifth of them expire unspent. The Foundation's annual gala on
the Pleasure Pier is where the donations are announced; the Mills hear about it on Static.

An **Assisted** flag stays on an account for a year after the last voucher. Assisted accounts cannot open a Proprietor Pathway and
are shown Halden's "Opportunity" contracts first, which are the low-paid maintenance work paid in scrip.

Vouchers trade on the street at **40** (40 cr per 100 sc of voucher face), lower in winter, and the trade is how most voucher
recipients actually eat: sell the voucher for credits, buy slab and Stinker water at grey prices, and get more food for it than
the Essentials List would have given.

**In play:** the Hope Desk is a choice the player can make when broke: a free 20 sc voucher (useful only at the store, for the
List) at the cost of an **Assisted** flag that blocks the Pathway for a year. If the player already holds a Pathway, the
Foundation will not serve them, because they are "already being helped". Clients pay in vouchers sometimes, and the player
can trade them at the street rate.

### 14.9 Blood and other things the body makes

**Plasma buyers** work out of a back room on floor 9 and a cart that comes up to the Mills on rent day. They pay 15 cr for 450 ml
(12 in the Sump, where people are weaker and have less choice), punch a card so the same donor cannot give more than twice a
month (and sell cards with fresh rows to those who want to), and sell the plasma on to **St. Ober's Spire**, where it is billed to
patients at many times the price. "**Bleeding for rent**" is a phrase every Mills child understands.

Other things the body can be paid for: hair (the Crown's wigs; 20 cr for a long head of it), teeth (founding-era fillings had
real gold and silver; a dentist on floor 7 buys old teeth), and being a test subject for Vey (a week in a Terrace clinic for
300 cr and whatever the trial does).

**In play:** these are not mechanics for the player to perform on themselves; they are **client types**. A plasma buyer is a
client who pays well for a cold-bay courier run to St. Ober's (a late-game tower contract). A woman at the counter sells her hair
because rent day is tomorrow, and wants a jar of real soil in exchange, because her daughter grows beans. The economy of the body
lives in clients and their reasons.

### 14.10 Produce as small change

In the Co-op halls and on the Ledge, small amounts are paid in what grows: a **chilli** is worth about 0.15 cr, a **bunch of
greens** about 2, a **tomato** about 5 (R, uncertified). The Co-op pays part of its wages in produce, which is how real food reaches
people who could never buy it, and how the Co-op avoids paying Halden's Ledger fee on wages.

**In play:** some Mills contracts pay partly **in produce** (a crate reward of chillies, greens, tomatoes). It is lower value than
credits but it is real (R), which can go up the tower in your own next contract. A neat closed loop: the Co-op pays you in what
it grows, you sell what it grows up the tower, the Crown eats it.

### 14.11 Where it happens

- **The Ledge**, floor 13: the wide landing of the old Works stair between the lift lobby and the Co-op hall, where a market sets
  up every morning from 06:00 to 10:00 after the Drop has been sorted. Pickers sell what they sorted; jobbers sell scrip;
  Stinkers sell water by the cup; hawkers sell everything. HPS walk through once a morning and buy breakfast.
- **Gutter Row**, floor 7: a corridor in the Sump where the founding-era drain gutters run open along the wall, lined with
  fixers' stalls: grey machines, rebuilt drone frames, re-terminated cells, cracked doses. Prices in cells and chits.
- **The Spill Door**, floor 4: unsold bales sold off at low tide.
- **The back counter** of the Hatch on floor 18 (section 2).
- **Your own counter.** A shop in the Mills is a place where the informal economy comes to you.

**In play:** each is a **vendor** with its own stock table, opening hours (in hour steps: the Ledge is 08:00–10:00 in game time,
the first two steps of the day) and accepted currencies (cr, chits, cells, jugs, markers). Reaching them costs hours (the lift, a
toll) or can be done by sending a drone with a shopping list and money in the bay.

### 15.3 Pressures and levers

| The Creep | Rate Reviews, the Chalk | outrun it (earn), own (title), move down, move up |
| Meter bills | every hour a machine draws | own water (air-well, Stinker), cells, run off-peak, tune the meter (risk) |
| The Pathway | daily charge | pay the minimum, pay extra (3% fee on lumps), lapse (lose everything) |
| Standing | every delivery and every bill | deliver on time, avoid inquiries, avoid association, buy a sponsor |
| Supply | the chute, the store, the Ledge | sort well, move to a better chute, buy Crown sacks, deal with fixers |
| Brand protection | every grey good on your squares | sell restored goods only by drone to grey clients, never on shelves; pay wardens |
| Losses on routes | free shafts | licensed lanes (permits, fees), the Mutual (on lanes), the Kitty (in shafts) |

Every lever costs one of the game's real resources: **squares** (space), **hours** (time), **Standing** (access), **credits**, or
**risk** (a roll that may cost any of those). No lever is free, and each style of play leans on a different pair.

### 15.4 Economic events

Events change prices and rules for a few days. Each is announced by a slip, a counter rumour, or a Static broadcast the day
before, so the player can react.

1. **Halden payday.** Scrip rate falls to 52 for two days; Mills clients flush with scrip; store-good contracts pay in scrip.
2. **Store restock.** New drone frames and parts in the store; scrip rises to 66.
3. **Rate Review.** The new card; your rent changes; a slip with the number circled.
4. **Re-cut notice.** Survey and Allocation will rationalise your sector on day X (3.5).
5. **The Chalk moves.** A floor reclassified as Middle (Transitional); proximity uplift on the floors below it; tenants from that
   floor appear as new clients and new competitors.
6. **Water shortage.** The air plant or the treatment works fails; Halden Pure rationed to Lifeline volumes only; Stinker water
   doubles; jugs become the most valuable thing in the Mills; contracts for water filters and Pure pay x2.
7. **Stinker raid.** A licensing sweep seals three stills; their jugs die; grey water triples for a week; the Rain Church's queue
   reaches the lift lobby.
8. **Retirement wave.** A batch of devices hits its Retirement date; the chute fills with bricked slates and screens for a week;
   flash rigs pay off.
9. **Crown renovation season.** The Crown refreshes its fittings; founding-era brass floods the Spill; Crown sacks are worth more;
   founding-era part prices halve for a fortnight (and the Old Hands buy everything).
10. **Vey recall.** A batch of DoseLock dispensers is recalled; the chute fills with half-full dispensers; cracked doses get cheap,
    real Vey gets scarce and dear.
11. **Orrin colony crash.** Orrin Protein Starter colonies crash across the Mills at once (the six-generation clock); wild-line
    colonies become valuable; Vitabrick prices rise 30% for a week.
12. **Brand sweep.** Brand protection agents walk the Mills for three days; any VOID-restored good on a shelf or frontage square is
    a fine and a Standing hit; goods in a drone bay are safe unless the drone is stopped on a lane.
13. **Drop jam.** A chute jams between floors; no diverter fills on that chute for two days; Halden pays a chute-clearing contract
    in scrip (40 sc a day, dirty work, Standing +).
14. **Disposal Rebate change.** Halden raises the rebate; the upper floors void more thoroughly; the chute tables shift toward
    Crush and Brick (worse for salvage) for a month.
15. **Foundation gala.** The Hope Desk opens daily for a week; more vouchers on the street; the voucher rate falls to 30.
16. **Inspection week.** Meter inspectors walk your floor; any tuned or bypassed meter rolls for discovery.
17. **Storm.** Tide high, Sump floors 4–5 flooded, Spill closed, Flats salvage halts; prices for everything from the bottom rise;
    the Sounding sells the forecast two days ahead to anyone who pays.

### 15.5 Progression in money terms

The economic ladder, roughly in order:
1. **Survive the first rent day.** Prepay meters, Probationary, Mills contracts only, chute and slab.
2. **Postpay** (Standing 400): the machines stop stopping. Store account buying.
3. **First Lane Permit** (Middle, 25 cr a week): Middle contracts, R✓ produce up, insurance that might pay.
4. **First licence** (food handling or Grow Certificate): Orrin supply contracts, the Certified grade.
5. **A second drone** or a bigger bay (Kestrel, 820 cr): two launches at once.
6. **Kitty membership** or a shaft-rat's frame: cheap risky routes as a second line.
7. **Preferred** (600): Terrace contracts visible; Middle squares leasable.
8. **The Pathway closes:** title on floor 17; rent stops on 500 squares. The ending "own your squares", or a midpoint.
9. **Expansion or a move:** more squares at the current card, a second lease on another chute, or a Middle lease.
10. **Trusted** (800) and a sponsor: Crown Lane clearance; the ending "reach the Crown" becomes a price, not a dream.
11. **Tower trade** (late; file 07): contracts to Vantage, Greenhold, St. Ober's, the Pier; batteries and frames to cross the
    bay.

Alongside, off the Ledger: Co-op share hours, Old Hand turns, a baron's sublets, a founding-era cell under the bunk. A player can
climb the Ledger, or the other ladder, or both. The game should let them be rich in different currencies.

### 15.6 The choices that make a style

- **Store or grey.** Licensed machines (reliable, bland, service contracts, safe from inspection) or fixers' machines (odd stats,
  cheap, no contract, risky).
- **Certified or saved.** OneCrop and Halden Pure (R✓, high pay up, high cost) or saved seed and service water (R, steady, cheaper,
  Middle and below only).
- **Lane or shaft.** Permits and fees, or tolls and looting.
- **On the Ledger or off it.** Credits, Standing and access, or chits, cells, jugs, markers and turns, invisible and unrated.
- **Serve up or serve down.** Embossed slips that pay in credits and want R✓, or crumpled ones that pay in cells and want slab,
  filters and cracked doses.
- **Restore or report.** VOID goods are money if you undo them, and Standing if you hand them to a brand agent (a small reward
  and +5 for "compliance").

None of these is the solved best. Each costs a different thing.

### 15.7 The smallest version

If only a little of this goes in, the smallest version that still shows the divide (extending the IDEAS minimum):
1. **Credits** and one **rent** per square per week, with a frontage multiplier.
2. **The Creep** as a Rate Review every 30 days.
3. **Two meters** (tap and socket) with an hourly draw and a daily bill.
4. **Standing**, one number, gating contracts.
5. **The Pathway** as a balance that falls.
6. **The chute** with a table per chute/floor and VOID as an item state.
7. **Two grades** (R and S) on contracts.
Scrip, chits, insurance, jugs, markers, sublets, vouchers and the rest are additive and can arrive one at a time, each one a new
choice rather than a new chore.

## 16. Loose stones

Small things that did not fit anywhere, for panning.

- **"Volume-Fair."** Every Halden tariff has a name with "Fair" in it: Volume-Fair water, Fair Peak power, Fair Share Common Load.
  The Mills say "fair" the way other people say "fine" when they mean the opposite.
- **The Ledger's birthday.** On the founding anniversary the Ledger writes off every debt under 1 cr, in every account, and prints
  "With the Company's compliments" on every tally screen. It costs Halden less than the ink.
- **Rent-day crowding.** On the evening before rent day the lift lobbies fill with people waiting to be paid by people who are
  waiting to be paid, and the jobbers do their best business of the week.
- **Dead scrip** burns hot and fast; Mills stoves are lit with it. On a cold week the street rate of expired scrip rises to 2.
- **The Count House clock.** The Ledger runs on founding-era time, which has drifted eleven minutes from the tower's clocks. Rent is
  taken at the Ledger's midnight, which is 23:49 on the wall. Every tenant who has ever paid at 23:55 believes they paid on time.
- **"Halden Fair Trading Award."** A brass plaque given each year to the Mills shop with the highest Standing. It comes with a 2%
  rent discount for a year, which is withdrawn if your Standing falls, and the plaque must be returned "in good condition".
- **Penny tasks.** The cheapest Halden contracts pay less than the launch costs in power. They exist to raise the Standing of
  people who cannot afford to fail anything else.
- **Grade inversions.** Synthetic goods marked R✓ by a bribed inspector go up; real goods with no certificate go down. On the
  Pleasure Pier, the Crown pays a premium for "authentic Mills slab", served on silver as a novelty.
- **The Founding price.** A founding-era valve wheel is "0.00 MISC SALVAGE" on the Ledger and 900 cr to an Old Hand. Every
  economic actor in the tower agrees on its worth except the one that keeps the books.
- **Tally ink.** Flats people registered at 150 Standing get a tally with a printed **F** in the corner. Mills shops are told to
  "use their judgement".
- **Tips.** Nobody tips in the Mills. In the Terraces, tipping a drone courier is customary, so licensed lane pilots earn
  double, and drones with a Mills transponder are never tipped.
- **The first credit.** The Archivists keep a printed Ledger slip from Y0, the first wage ever paid by Halden Works: 1 day, 1 shift,
  a sum that, at founding prices, bought a week's food. Today it buys three Vitabricks.
- **Interest on the dead.** Debts do not die with the debtor; the Ledger moves them to the nearest registered relative by lease
  proximity, which is often a neighbour. "Don't let your neighbour die broke" is a Mills blessing.

**Hook:** the scrip plates. Scrip notes are printed on the Terraces from founding-era plates that cannot be re-made. One set went
missing in Y82 and was never reported missing, because reporting it would admit the risk. Ines Coyle's buying spree (1.4), Moss
Underhill's ever-growing holdings (14.7) and the Hatch on floor 18's back counter (2.2) might be one story.

**Hook:** a Rate Review notice arrives that lowers the rate on floor 17. It is the first decrease in the tower's history. The Mills
assume a misprint. It is not: someone in Survey and Allocation wants floor 17 full and happy for exactly ninety days, and nobody
knows what happens on day ninety-one.

**Hook:** WORKS-0-00117 (1.1), the founding-era pension account, makes a payment. One credit, to the player's account, with a
memo line from the Works payroll format: `SHIFT 2 / HALL 17 / OUTSTANDING`.
