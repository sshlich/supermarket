# I

Every evening at the hour the bells used to ring, Anselm took his ladder and his can of oil and went out to light the lamps of Merrow Street.

There were forty of them once. There were eleven now. The rest had lost their glass to the winters, or their brass to people who needed brass more than the street needed light. Anselm did not blame them. He had sold the brass of his own front door the year his wife was ill, and the door had not minded.

He lit the eleven in the same order he had always lit the forty, skipping the dark posts as a man skips the steps he knows are missing on a stair. The oil was poor now, thin and smoky, and some nights it would not take. On those nights he stood under the lamp with his match burning down to his fingers and waited, as if patience were a kind of fuel.

# II

The children of the street followed him, as children always had. They no longer asked to carry the ladder. They asked what the lamps were for.

"For seeing," said Anselm.

"Seeing what?" said the smallest of them, a girl with one shoe. "Nobody goes out at night."

Anselm thought about this for the length of two lamps. "So that if somebody did," he said at last, "they would know the way home."

The girl considered this, and seemed to find it acceptable, and the next night she brought her own match.

# III

Winter came early, and the oil ran out in its first week. Anselm went to the shop at the end of the road with the last of his money and a book he had been keeping for a reason he could no longer remember.

The shopkeeper turned the book over in her hands, looked at its spine and its worn corners, and set it on the counter beside a bottle half full of lamp oil.

"That's not a fair trade," said Anselm. "The book's worth more."

"I know," she said. "Light the lamps."

He did. And on the coldest night of that winter, a man who had lost his way in the snow saw eleven small lights in a row on Merrow Street, and walked toward them, and lived.

Nobody told Anselm. That is how it usually goes with lamps.
